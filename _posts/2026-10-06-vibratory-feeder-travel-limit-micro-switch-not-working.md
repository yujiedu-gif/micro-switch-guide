A vibratory feeder travel limit micro switch not working can indicate several different faults: the mechanism may not reach the actuator, the switch may not change electrical state consistently, or the wiring or control input may fail to carry or recognize that state. Separate these possibilities before deciding that the switch itself has failed. Keep the feeder isolated and secured for inspection, and verify absence of voltage before any continuity test. Do not bypass the travel limit to diagnose the problem.

## Safety and symptom boundaries for a vibratory feeder travel limit micro switch not working

Begin by following the feeder’s applicable isolation and securing procedure. A continuity check applies to an isolated circuit, not to an energized feeder. Verify absence of voltage before testing. If the isolation point, wiring, or safe method of securing the mechanism is uncertain, stop and seek qualified service rather than probing or moving parts.

Describe what happens before opening or testing anything. Does the switch appear not to change state when the feeder reaches its intended limit? Does the indication come and go? Or does the switch appear to act correctly while the control system does not respond? These are different observations, not diagnoses. An indication at the controller alone does not establish whether the switch, wiring, or input is at fault.

Do not bypass the travel limit as a shortcut. A bypass removes the distinction the test needs to establish and may defeat a protective or control function. Use only an approved, safe method to move the mechanism during observation. Stop if motion cannot be controlled safely, if a safety circuit is damaged, or if the next check would require energized testing outside your qualifications.

## Check whether the feeder actually reaches the micro switch

With the feeder secured and isolated as required, inspect the travel path and the switch’s mounting. Look for an obstruction, a loose mounting point, misalignment between the moving mechanism and actuator, contamination, visible damage, or evidence that the mechanism strikes the switch instead of contacting it in the intended way. These observations can point toward a mechanical reach problem, but they do not establish the electrical condition of the contacts.

Use only an approved method to move the mechanism and observe whether it reaches the intended limit and physically contacts the actuator. The key distinction is whether the actuator is reached at all. If it is not, investigate the travel path, mounting, and alignment before treating the switch as defective. Do not force the mechanism or infer a required travel distance from the component category; device-specific travel limits must come from the applicable specification sheet.

If the actuator is reached, observe whether it moves freely and whether the lever or other actuator movement is consistent. A visible contact with the actuator is not proof that the switch contacts change electrical state. Conversely, a lack of control response does not prove that the actuator was never reached. Keep mechanical observation separate from the electrical test that follows.

## Verify micro switch actuation and electrical state change

Before a continuity test, isolate the relevant circuit and verify absence of voltage. Identify the terminals from the installed switch documentation or its exact device schematic. In generic changeover terminology, COM means common, NC means normally closed, and NO means normally open; confirm that these labels apply to the installed device rather than assuming a terminal arrangement from appearance. If the documentation is missing or the terminals cannot be identified confidently, stop rather than guessing.

Use the documented test method to check the isolated switch while moving its actuator through its operating movement. Record the result in each defined actuator state and whether the state change repeats as the actuator is moved and released. A consistent transition indicates that the isolated switch changed state under those test conditions; it does not prove that the feeder wiring or control input works. No transition, or a transition that cannot be repeated, narrows the concern to the switch or the test setup, but does not by itself identify the cause.

Compare the observed operating behavior and travel with the documentation for the installed switch. Do not invent a travel limit or force requirement, and do not force or adjust the actuator beyond documented limits. Numerical travel limits are device-specific and must be checked in the applicable specification sheet. If the specification, suitable test method, or terminal reference is unavailable, the result is inconclusive and should not be used to justify adjustment or replacement.

## Separate vibration, wiring, and control-input faults

If the switch changes state during an isolated test but the feeder symptom is intermittent, consider whether mounting or actuator movement is unstable. Inspect mounting security, alignment, free actuator movement, contamination, damage, and loose terminals. During an approved observation, look for inconsistent actuation or chatter rather than assuming that every intermittent control indication means the contacts have failed. Vibration-related instability and a stable open or closed state are different patterns, but observation alone may not locate the fault.

With power isolated, inspect connectors and wiring for looseness or visible damage. Any continuity check through the relevant circuit must use the applicable feeder documentation and a defined isolated test condition. Do not treat a switch-only test as a test of the complete wiring path. If the switch state is repeatable but the control input does not recognize it, trace the relevant input circuit and logic using the feeder documentation. A controller indication by itself cannot distinguish a wiring fault from an input or logic issue.

Do not extend these checks into unrelated motor, drive, or controller troubleshooting. If identifying the input circuit requires energized measurements, uncertain wiring changes, or access to a damaged safety circuit, stop and refer the work to a qualified technician. Keep conclusions tied to what each isolated test actually established.

## Decide what the test results mean and when to stop

Use the observations to classify the next question, not to jump to a replacement decision. If the mechanism does not reach the actuator, the evidence points first to travel, mounting, obstruction, or alignment. If the actuator is reached but the isolated switch does not change state repeatably under the documented test, investigate the switch and test conditions against its documentation. If the state is inconsistent, inspect for unstable mounting or actuation as well as the switch and its connections. If the isolated switch changes state correctly but the control input does not respond, trace the documented wiring and input path rather than condemning the switch from the indication alone.

Any correction or replacement decision should follow the installed switch specification and feeder procedures. Do not adjust beyond documented limits, infer compatibility from a similar-looking part, or return the feeder to service on the strength of a single isolated reading. Stop and seek qualified service for energized testing, uncertain terminal identification or wiring, damaged safety circuits, uncontrolled or unexplained motion, or results that do not fit the documentation.

The phrase vibratory feeder travel limit micro switch not working describes a symptom, not a confirmed component failure. Distinguish whether the mechanism reaches the actuator, whether the isolated switch changes state consistently, and whether the documented signal path reaches the control input. That sequence supports a bounded fault-isolation decision without treating one observation as proof that the whole assembly is good or bad.
