An intermittent end-of-stroke symptom does not, by itself, show that a micro switch has failed. The power shear end of stroke micro switch intermittent operation described by an operator could involve inconsistent mechanical actuation, the switch contacts, wiring, or another part of the control system. The machine’s documentation is needed to confirm that a micro switch is used and to identify its intended role. Diagnosis should separate those possibilities rather than treat the symptom as a component verdict.

The useful sequence is to establish whether the machine’s mechanism consistently reaches and moves the intended actuator, then compare a documented, isolated switch check with the signal observed in the installed circuit. Each observation has limits: a result at the switch does not establish how the installed wiring or downstream controls behave, and an installed symptom alone does not locate the fault. Use the machine-specific procedure and qualified service support wherever isolation, access, or expected electrical states are uncertain.

## power shear end of stroke micro switch intermittent operation

Treat intermittent operation as an observation to investigate, not proof of a failed switch. A micro switch is an electromechanical component with an actuator moved by an external mechanism. In an end-of-stroke arrangement, the machine’s mechanism may move that actuator, and a contact change may contribute to a signal path. Whether a particular power shear uses this arrangement, and what the signal means to its controls, must be confirmed from the machine documentation.

Do not infer the switch’s contact arrangement or normal state from the words “end of stroke,” from the symptom, or from a click. Those details depend on the actual device and machine design. A generic changeover contact may be marked common, normally closed, and normally open, but the exact device marking or schematic controls. “Normally” must be interpreted using the documented reference state; it does not independently establish the machine’s control response.

Keep the fault domains separate from the outset:

- The mechanism may not reach the intended position consistently.
- The mechanism may reach it but fail to move the actuator as intended.
- The isolated switch may not show the documented continuity behavior at a defined actuator state.
- The installed wiring, terminal connections, or downstream controls may account for an intermittent signal.

These are investigation paths, not confirmed causes. Do not assume that every power shear has a micro switch at end of stroke or that a switch’s contact change necessarily stops, enables, or otherwise controls a particular machine function.

## Check whether the mechanism consistently actuates the micro switch

Begin with the machine-specific documentation. Use it to identify whether a micro switch is part of the end-of-stroke arrangement, which mechanism is intended to move its actuator, and what position or state is relevant. Do not identify the intended position from a generic description of a power shear. If the documentation does not establish the arrangement or expected actuation, that uncertainty must be resolved before drawing conclusions about the switch.

Follow the machine’s required safe isolation procedure before observing the mechanism or actuator. Do not reach into hazardous machinery, move machine parts, or attempt an adjustment based on generic guidance. Within the documented safe procedure, the diagnostic question is limited: does the mechanism reach the specified position and move the intended actuator consistently across the observations that the procedure permits?

If it does not, the intermittent signal remains consistent with an input-path problem. The mechanism’s travel, alignment, or linkage may need machine-specific evaluation, but an observation of inconsistent actuation does not establish which mechanical issue exists. Nor does it prove that the switch is fault-free: more than one issue may remain possible. Keep this result distinct from evidence about the switch’s isolated contacts.

If the mechanism appears to reach the specified position and move the actuator consistently, that finding narrows the question but does not prove the switch contacts or installed circuit work correctly. Continue only if the documentation provides a safe, supported method for checking the isolated switch. If the intended actuator, position, or safe observation method is unclear, stop and obtain qualified, machine-specific guidance rather than improvising an inspection or adjustment.

## Separate switch-contact behavior from wiring and control faults

An isolated continuity check can address the switch’s contact behavior only when the switch can be isolated safely and a documented method identifies the expected terminals and actuator states. Follow that method. For each observation, record the defined actuator state and the corresponding continuity result specified by the device documentation or machine procedure. Do not substitute an assumed state, terminal label, or expected result.

A generic changeover contact may use common, normally closed, and normally open labels. The device marking or schematic, rather than the generic convention, determines which terminals apply to the switch under examination. Likewise, do not assume a particular contact state at an end-of-stroke position. The accepted behavior must come from the specific documentation.

An isolated check and an installed signal observation answer different questions. The isolated check concerns the switch under the defined test conditions. It does not establish that installed wiring, terminal connections, or downstream controls transmit or interpret the signal correctly. Conversely, an intermittent signal observed in the machine does not prove that the switch contacts caused it. Wiring, terminal identification, and faults elsewhere in the control system remain distinct possibilities.

Do not bridge the gap between these checks by bypassing the switch, probing a live circuit, or assuming that an isolated reading demonstrates safe or correct machine operation. If a documented safe isolation method or expected continuity behavior is unavailable, the check is not established as appropriate for this case. Leave it to qualified personnel using the machine-specific procedure.

## Interpret observations without overclaiming

Use each result to narrow the next question, not to declare a complete diagnosis:

- **Actuation is inconsistent:** The mechanical input path needs attention in the diagnostic process. This observation does not identify a particular mechanical cause or rule out a separate switch or circuit issue.
- **Actuation appears consistent, but the isolated result is inconsistent with the documented behavior:** The switch merits further qualified evaluation. The result alone does not establish why the behavior differs or whether the test conditions and terminal identification were correct.
- **The isolated result matches the documented behavior, but the installed signal remains intermittent:** The switch’s observed isolated behavior does not explain the installed symptom by itself. Wiring, terminals, and downstream controls remain unresolved possibilities.
- **The available documentation does not define the expected state or safe check:** There is not enough support for a meaningful comparison. Do not fill the gap with a presumed contact arrangement or machine response.

A click is not a continuity result, and an isolated continuity result is not an overall assessment of the machine. Keep the observations tied to their conditions: the mechanism’s position, the actuator state, the switch’s isolation status, the identified terminals, and the expected behavior stated in the documentation. If any of those is unknown, qualify the interpretation accordingly.

These distinctions also prevent a false either-or conclusion. A mechanical input concern and an electrical concern are different fault domains, but the available observations may not exclude more than one. An intermittent installed signal can prompt further investigation without proving that the switch, wiring, or control system alone is responsible.

## When to stop and use machine-specific service guidance

Stop when safe isolation, access, terminal identity, expected actuator state, or the correct continuity behavior is uncertain. Use qualified personnel and the machine’s service procedure to resolve those questions. Do not adjust the shear mechanism or switch, alter wiring, or replace a component on the basis of this general diagnostic sequence; the necessary machine-specific evidence and procedure are not established here.

Do not bypass the switch or perform live probing. Do not infer a safe machine state from a click, a single continuity reading, or an apparently consistent actuator movement. Each is limited evidence about one part of a larger mechanical and electrical path.

The practical decision boundary is therefore conditional: inconsistent actuator movement points back to the documented mechanical input path for evaluation; inconsistent isolated behavior calls for qualified switch assessment under defined conditions; and normal isolated behavior alongside an intermittent installed signal leaves the wiring and downstream controls unresolved. The phrase power shear end of stroke micro switch intermittent operation describes the symptom, not its cause. Reach a repair or replacement decision only after the fault path is established using the machine-specific procedure.
