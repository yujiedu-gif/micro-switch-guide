The phrase **light switch cable** can describe wiring associated with a lighting switch, but it does not identify a particular conductor, terminal, or circuit. In a microswitch-operated lighting control, the cable and the microswitch are related parts of the system, not interchangeable terms. The cable contains conductors; the switch has terminals and contacts that change state according to its design. Understanding that distinction helps keep a general phrase from being mistaken for a device-specific wiring description.

A wiring diagram and the documentation for the exact switch and equipment are needed to understand intended connections. A cable name alone does not establish conductor function, circuit voltage, switch compatibility, or the meaning of a terminal’s physical position. This article explains those concepts without giving installation or replacement instructions.

## What does “light switch cable” mean?

“Light switch cable” is a context-dependent phrase for wiring associated with a lighting switch or lighting-control circuit. It may refer generally to a cable, or to conductors within that cable, but the phrase does not specify which conductors are present or what each one does. Its meaning depends on how it is used in the relevant equipment documentation or conversation.

A cable and a conductor are not the same thing. A cable is an assembly that contains one or more conductors. Conductors carry electrical current in a circuit, but their functions cannot be determined from the phrase “light switch cable” alone. The phrase does not establish whether a conductor is a supply, a switched connection, or another part of a particular circuit. Those details require applicable circuit documentation.

A microswitch is a switching device, not a cable. Its terminals provide connection points for the circuit, and its contacts change electrical state as the switch operates. The cable may connect parts of a lighting-control system that includes a microswitch, but calling something a “light switch cable” does not identify which microswitch terminal it reaches or what contact state applies.

This distinction matters when reading descriptions, labels, or diagrams. A cable description concerns wiring associated with a system; a terminal designation concerns a connection point on a device. Neither should be treated as a substitute for the other. Even if a cable is shown near a switch in a drawing, that visual relationship alone does not establish its electrical function.

The phrase also does not specify a circuit’s voltage, load, or suitability for a particular device. Electrical ratings are tied to stated loads and test conditions in the applicable specification sheets, rather than being universal switching capacities. Therefore, the phrase “light switch cable” cannot establish that a microswitch is appropriate for a given lighting circuit.

## light switch cable and microswitch terminals are different things

Wiring conductors and switch terminals describe different parts of a circuit. A conductor is part of the wiring; a terminal is a connection point on the switch. The switch’s contacts describe electrical relationships within the device. Keeping these categories separate helps avoid reading a wiring description as though it were a terminal label.

A generic changeover contact may use the labels **COM**, **NC**, and **NO**. COM means common: the shared contact in the changeover arrangement. NC means normally closed, and NO means normally open. These terms describe contact relationships in the switch’s unoperated or “normal” state; they do not name cable types or identify conductors in an installation. The exact device marking or schematic controls how those labels apply to a particular switch.

The words “normally closed” and “normally open” refer to contact state, not to whether a cable is connected, energized, or physically present. They also do not, by themselves, describe a lighting-control circuit’s full behavior. That depends on the switch’s role and the documented circuit. A generic description of a changeover contact cannot establish that a particular device has those terminals or uses the same markings.

Physical appearance is not a reliable substitute for documentation. Terminal positions can vary, and a generic drawing or assumed layout does not establish which terminal is COM, NO, or NC on a specific device. A relevant diagram, terminal marking, and exact switch documentation should be read together. If they do not clearly identify the connection, the label “light switch cable” cannot resolve the uncertainty.

The distinction is useful even when a diagram shows both cable and switch. A line representing a conductor and a label identifying a contact terminal answer different questions. The line indicates a documented circuit connection; the terminal designation describes a point or contact relationship on the device. Neither should be inferred solely from the other.

## What can a wiring diagram establish?

A wiring diagram can communicate intended connections and, when included, the relationship between switch contacts and operating states. Its meaning depends on the diagram applying to the exact switch and equipment in question. A generic illustration or an isolated label may not represent the installed device or its circuit.

For terminal identification and circuit interpretation, the relevant evidence includes the connection diagram, the terminal markings, the circuit function, and the permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. These details provide a basis for interpreting the diagram; the physical arrangement of terminals alone does not. If a diagram uses COM, NO, or NC, those designations should be understood in the context of the device’s own marking or schematic.

The phrase “light switch cable” cannot establish what a conductor does, what voltage is present, or whether a switch is compatible with the circuit. It does not provide a load rating or specify the conditions under which a rating applies. Electrical ratings are specification-sheet requirements tied to stated load and test conditions, not universal capacities that can be inferred from a general cable description.

A diagram also has limits. It can show intended relationships for the documented device and equipment, but a generic diagram cannot settle details for an unidentified installation. If the documentation is missing, unclear, or does not match the equipment, the phrase “light switch cable” does not fill that gap. Interpretation should remain unresolved until the applicable documentation is available.

## What to verify before acting on a lighting-control diagram

Before relying on a lighting-control diagram, establish that it applies to the exact switch and equipment being discussed. Check that device identity and documentation match the context. A general diagram, a familiar-looking terminal arrangement, or the phrase “light switch cable” is not enough to confirm that a connection or rating applies.

Use the documented connection diagram and terminal markings to understand the device’s connections and contact states. Do not identify COM, NO, or NC solely from terminal position or appearance. Where a generic changeover arrangement is described, treat its labels as general terminology; the exact device marking or schematic determines the relevant interpretation.

Also distinguish terminal identification from rating and compatibility. A label such as COM, NO, or NC does not establish what load the switch may control. The permissible load and electrical ratings must come from the documentation for the exact switch and equipment, including the stated load and test conditions. A cable description cannot supply those specifications.

This is a conceptual explanation, not a wiring, connection, or replacement procedure. Installation-specific questions require applicable documentation and qualified guidance, including relevant deenergization and qualification requirements. If the documentation does not clearly address the specific installation, the available information is insufficient to infer a safe or suitable connection. In that situation, leave the conductor function, terminal mapping, and compatibility unresolved rather than relying on a generic phrase or physical layout.

In short, **this topic** refers broadly to wiring associated with a lighting switch, while a microswitch’s terminals and contact states describe the device. The exact diagram and documentation, not the phrase or terminal position alone, determine how those elements relate.
