A boom lift end of stroke micro switch contact rating is meaningful only when read with the electrical conditions attached to it. It is not a universal measure of what every switch can control, and the boom-lift application alone does not establish whether the switch carries motor current or signals a control circuit. To check suitability, compare the switch’s documented rating with the actual circuit voltage, current, AC or DC supply, load category, and switching duty. If any of those conditions are unknown, the rating cannot be confirmed from its number alone.

## What a boom lift end of stroke micro switch contact rating specifies

A contact rating is an electrical limit stated for particular conditions, not a general measure of switch quality or a promise that the contact can switch any circuit. The rating values are specification-sheet requirements tied to the stated load and test conditions. That boundary matters because a printed voltage or current value cannot be separated from the other conditions that define it.

Check the documentation for the voltage and current, whether the circuit is AC or DC, and the load category associated with those values. These details must match the circuit being evaluated. An AC rating does not establish a DC rating, or vice versa. Likewise, a value stated for one load category should not be treated as a universal capacity for other loads.

A resistive-load rating and an inductive-load rating are not interchangeable. If the available marking or specification gives a rating only for a particular category, that does not establish suitability for a different category. Where the load category, test conditions, or applicable specification are missing, treat the rating as unresolved rather than filling the gap by inference.

For a boom lift end-of-stroke switch, the application label does not supply those missing electrical facts. Different designs can use different circuit arrangements and ratings. The rating must therefore be interpreted from the switch’s own marking or authoritative specification, then compared with the circuit in which it is actually installed.

## Why load type changes the rating comparison

A simple comparison of a circuit’s voltage and current with numbers on a switch may be insufficient if the load category differs. Inductive and capacitive loads can involve switching transients or inrush effects that affect contact stress. The relevant question is not merely whether a number appears to match, but whether the documented rating covers the actual load and switching conditions.

An end-of-stroke micro switch may directly switch a load, or it may change a low-current control input or relay coil while another part of the circuit controls a motor or other higher-current load. These arrangements have different electrical demands at the switch contacts. The presence of a motor in the equipment does not prove that the micro switch itself switches motor current. Nor does the switch’s location at a travel limit identify the circuit it controls.

Determine the switched load from the applicable circuit documentation or other reliable service information. If a relay coil or control input is involved, its electrical conditions still need to be identified and compared with the switch rating. Do not assume that a control circuit is suitable simply because it is not the motor circuit; the available evidence must establish the actual conditions.

The switching duty also matters to the comparison. A rating should be evaluated under the conditions stated for it, including the relevant switching duty where documented. If the circuit’s load behavior or duty is unclear, a nominal rating alone cannot settle suitability. This is a boundary of the available information, not a reason to estimate or substitute a value.

## How to check the rating against the actual circuit

Start with the switch marking and its authoritative specification sheet, if available. Record the stated voltage, current, AC or DC designation, load category, and any conditions associated with the rating. Keep those qualifiers together. A partial label or a number copied without its conditions may not provide enough information for comparison.

Next, establish what the contacts actually switch. Use the applicable circuit schematic or qualified service information to identify whether the switch controls a motor or another load directly, or instead operates a control input or relay coil. Confirm the circuit voltage, current, AC/DC type, load category, and switching duty from that documentation or from qualified measurements where appropriate. Do not infer these values from the equipment’s general purpose or from the switch’s position in the mechanism.

Then compare the documented circuit conditions with the rating conditions. The comparison is supported only when the actual circuit falls within the conditions stated for the rating. If the load category differs, a required value is missing, or any condition exceeds or cannot be reconciled with the documentation, suitability remains unverified. The appropriate conclusion is to seek an appropriately specified design or qualified technical review, not to assume the switch is adequate.

Ratings and circuit arrangements vary by design, so this check cannot identify a particular switch or establish compatibility across boom lifts. A marking that appears to match one circuit value is not enough if other rating conditions or the actual load remain unknown. The rating and the circuit documentation must be considered together.

## Safety and design limits for boom lift end of stroke micro switch contact rating

An end-of-travel switch may be part of an equipment control or safety function. Do not bypass or defeat that function to test the switch. Inspection or measurement should be performed only with equipment isolated and under applicable service procedures. If those procedures or conditions are not available, do not improvise a test.

A switch marking by itself does not establish the full circuit arrangement, prove that the switch is correctly wired, or show that the equipment’s end-of-travel function behaves as intended. Likewise, knowing the equipment application does not establish the contact’s electrical duty. Keep those questions separate: one concerns the documented switch rating, and the other concerns the actual circuit and its operation.

If markings are absent or unclear, circuit data are unavailable, or access makes safe testing uncertain, a qualified technician should resolve the question using authoritative equipment and switch documentation. The boom lift end of stroke micro switch contact rating can be judged against its circuit only when the rating conditions and actual switched load are known; otherwise, suitability remains unresolved.
