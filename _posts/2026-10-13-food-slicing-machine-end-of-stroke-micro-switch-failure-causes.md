## What an end-of-stroke symptom can, and cannot, tell you

End-of-stroke behavior is an observation of the machine as a system. It does not identify which component, if any, caused the behavior. A machine may use a micro switch as an input to its control system, but the arrangement is not established for every food slicing machine. The symptom therefore cannot prove that a switch exists in the relevant sensing path, much less that it has failed.

Even where a micro switch is part of the arrangement, its role must be kept distinct from the machine's control logic. A mechanical part may move an actuator, and the switch may change contact state, but what the machine does in response depends on its actual design and circuit. The available information does not establish a particular normal contact state, alarm, shutdown response, or end-of-stroke sequence for a food slicing machine.

Treat the symptom as a reason to distinguish possible paths, not as a component diagnosis. First, determine from the applicable machine documentation whether a micro switch is used in the relevant function. If that remains unknown, do not assume that switch-level explanations apply. If the documentation does identify one, separate whether the external mechanism reaches and operates it from whether its contacts change state. Then keep terminal identification and wiring distinct from faults elsewhere in the equipment.

This boundary matters because similar outward behavior can have different causes. A switch may not be actuated, its contacts may not behave as expected, its terminals may be misunderstood, wiring may be involved, or another part of the machine may account for the symptom. These are distinct possibilities, not confirmed faults. The observation alone cannot select among them.
