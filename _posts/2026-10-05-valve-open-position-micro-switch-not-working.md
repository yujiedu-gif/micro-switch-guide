A valve may be physically open even when its position indication says otherwise. When a **valve open position micro switch not working** symptom appears, first establish the valve’s actual position, then separate a failure to actuate the switch from a contact, wiring, or downstream indication problem. A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement and is commonly used for position or limit detection, but the signal alone does not establish the valve’s position.

Use the equipment’s procedures and documentation to guide inspection and testing. A switch test can help identify whether its contacts change state, but it does not by itself prove that the complete valve indication circuit works.

## Confirm the valve is open before diagnosing the micro switch

Establish the valve’s position independently of its electrical indicator, using a position reference permitted by the equipment procedure. Do not treat the indicator as proof that the valve reached its open position: the valve may not have completed its travel, or the indication path may not be reporting its state correctly.

If the valve is not open, the immediate issue is not yet isolated to the micro switch. The valve, its drive, or its travel may require assessment under the equipment’s own procedure. If the valve is open by the permitted independent reference but the indication says otherwise, proceed to distinguish mechanical actuation from electrical signal problems.

Keep the component and system roles separate. The switch responds to mechanical movement; the effect of its contact state on an indicator depends on the actual wiring and downstream circuit. A micro switch’s general use for position or limit detection does not establish a particular valve design, contact arrangement, or indication logic.

## valve open position micro switch not working: isolate the fault safely

Before inspection or a continuity test, isolate power and follow the applicable equipment-specific safety procedures. Do not assume that a circuit is de-energized. Use the required isolation or lockout process, and keep any live-circuit measurements within the equipment procedure and qualified personnel’s scope.

Before disconnecting conductors, record their connections and identify the switch terminals from the device markings or exact switch documentation. Do not infer terminal functions from appearance alone. If a generic changeover contact is involved, its terminals may be labeled common, normally closed, and normally open, but the exact device marking or schematic controls.

Make any test disconnection only as allowed by the procedure, so a reading can be attributed to the switch rather than an attached circuit. If safe isolation, terminal identification, or test conditions cannot be established, stop and refer the work to qualified personnel. Do not bypass the switch or treat an indicator fault as permission to defeat a control function.

## Check valve travel and micro switch actuator engagement

With the equipment in the procedure-approved condition, inspect the mechanical path between valve movement and the switch actuator. Depending on the design, that path may include a cam, linkage, lever, or plunger. Check only components actually present; no particular arrangement can be assumed for every valve.

Determine whether valve travel carries the actuator to its specified operating point and allows it to release when the valve moves away. Compare the relevant points with the equipment documentation. Do not assume a universal adjustment or infer correct engagement merely because the valve appears open or the switch makes a sound.

Pretravel runs from the actuator’s free position to its operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. Those terms describe actuator travel, not proof that a particular valve reaches either point. If the mechanism does not reach the specified operating point, the switch may not be actuated even if the contacts themselves are functional. If it reaches the point but the expected state change is absent, continue with an isolated contact test.

## Test micro switch contact state in each specified position

With power isolated and the switch disconnected as required by the procedure, use continuity testing across the terminals identified by the device markings or exact documentation. Test the specified released and actuated states. Compare each result with the switch markings and circuit documentation; do not assume which terminals should show continuity without that information.

A generic changeover contact may use common, normally closed, and normally open labels. These names do not replace the exact device marking or schematic, which controls terminal identification and the expected test. Keep the actuator state attached to each reading so the result is interpretable.

If the contacts do not change state as specified, the result points to a switch-contact or actuation issue, but does not alone establish the underlying cause. Recheck that the actuator was moved to the documented state and that the test was made across the correct terminals under the required isolation conditions. If the expected change still does not occur, further assess or replace the switch according to the equipment procedure. Do not claim that a continuity result proves the valve, wiring, or full indication path is sound.

## Trace wiring and indication when the switch tests correctly

If the isolated switch changes state as specified, look beyond the switch. Under the applicable procedure, inspect terminals and conductors for looseness, damage, or incorrect termination. Compare the actual connections with the wiring diagram; do not infer a wiring fault from a failed indication alone.

Then assess the downstream input or indicator according to its own documentation and permitted test procedure. The contact state at the switch terminals and the indication at the system level are separate observations. A correct switch test narrows the investigation, but it does not prove that the wiring carries the state correctly or that the downstream input responds as intended.

A **valve closed position micro switch not working** symptom may offer a comparison only if the circuit design and documentation support that comparison. It is not proof that the open-position switch has the same fault, shares the same wiring path, or uses the same logic. Keep conclusions specific to the component and signal path actually checked.

## Verify the repair across the valve operating cycle

After corrective work, restore connections and guards and return the equipment to service only under its procedure. Check the valve’s physical position and its corresponding electrical indication at open and at the other required positions in the operating cycle. Confirm that the mechanical movement reaches and releases the actuator as specified, and that the indication follows the documented circuit behavior.

If the physical position and indication still disagree, or if test results remain inconsistent, stop and escalate the diagnosis under the equipment procedure. Do not bypass an interlock or treat one successful switch reading as proof that the whole assembly is functioning correctly. The fault is isolated only as far as the observations support: the valve’s physical state, actuator engagement, switch-terminal state, and downstream indication each require their own evidence.

In short, resolve a **valve open position micro switch not working** symptom by confirming the valve position independently, checking mechanical engagement, testing the isolated contacts against device documentation, and tracing the signal path if the switch behaves as specified. This sequence distinguishes likely fault locations without assuming a particular valve design or claiming that one test proves the entire system.
