The **painting robot end of stroke micro switch location** cannot be inferred from the robot’s endpoint display or from where its motion appears to stop. A micro switch is present for that purpose only when the machine’s design documentation confirms one. To identify its location, trace the documented mechanical path from the moving robot member or linkage at the relevant endpoint to the switch actuator, then compare the observed mounting point with the machine-specific diagram.

This approach identifies a physical component without confusing it with a software limit, controller response, or other endpoint sensor. If the documentation does not establish a micro switch for that endpoint, its location remains unconfirmed; appearance, an alarm, or a click is not enough to identify it.

## What “painting robot end of stroke micro switch location” means

The question concerns a physical micro switch associated with an endpoint, not a feature that every painting robot necessarily contains. A micro switch is an electromechanical component. Its actuator is the part moved by an external mechanism. The robot’s endpoint logic is a separate matter: the controller may interpret an input or use another method to determine or respond to an endpoint, but that relationship depends on the documented machine design.

Start by checking the applicable machine-specific documentation for an end-of-stroke switch, its depicted mounting point, and the moving member or linkage associated with it. If the documentation identifies a micro switch, use those details to guide a physical inspection under the machine’s approved access procedure. Do not assume the switch is at the visible end of travel, beside a mechanical stop, or near a sensor simply because those features appear related.

The relevant location is where the documented mechanical arrangement places the switch and allows the moving part or linkage to act on its actuator. That location may not match a software endpoint marker or the position shown on a controller display. Without documentation that confirms the component and its role, the correct conclusion is that the location is unknown, not that a nearby device must be the switch.

## Trace the physical actuation path to the micro switch

Follow the physical motion, not just the endpoint label. Begin with the documented moving member or linkage at the stated endpoint. Using the machine-specific layout or service documentation, trace how that member could reach or move a switch actuator. Then compare the documented mounting point and motion direction with the component visible during approved access. This creates a mechanical link between the moving part and the candidate switch instead of relying on proximity alone.

A switch may be mounted where a linkage transfers motion to its actuator rather than directly on the member that travels. Therefore, an inspection should account for the complete documented path: the moving part, any linkage shown, the actuator, and the switch mounting location. Do not assume an arrangement that the diagram does not show, or infer a switch location solely from the robot’s geometry.

A hard stop marks or constrains motion; it does not by itself establish that a micro switch is present. Likewise, a device near the endpoint is not identified by location alone. Confirm that the machine’s documentation depicts a micro switch there and connects it mechanically to the relevant moving member or linkage. If the diagram and observed arrangement do not agree, do not resolve the discrepancy by guessing. The physical location remains unconfirmed until the applicable documentation or qualified service information clarifies the design.

## Distinguish the switch from endpoint settings and other sensors

A software or controller limit is a setting or system behavior, not the physical location of a micro switch. The endpoint shown on a display may describe a configured limit or a reported state, but it does not establish that a particular electromechanical switch is mounted at that point. The component’s mounting position must be identified through the documented physical arrangement.

A painting robot may use another sensing method, or its design may not use a micro switch for the endpoint in question. A visible sensor, a controller indication, or an alarm therefore does not prove that a micro switch provides the signal. Attribute a signal or control response to a micro switch only when the machine documentation establishes that relationship.

Keep three questions separate: where a documented physical switch is mounted, how the machine establishes or represents an endpoint, and what the controller does with an input. The first is answered by tracing the mechanical actuation path and confirming the mounting point. The latter two require their own design documentation. Identifying the switch’s location does not, on its own, explain the robot’s endpoint settings or controller logic.

## Verify the location without assuming function

Compare the observed component and actuator path with the applicable machine diagram and the documented endpoint condition. The comparison should support both parts of the identification: that the component is the documented micro switch and that the moving member or linkage can act on its actuator in the stated arrangement. If either relationship is missing, treat the identification as unresolved.

A click does not establish switch identity or function. Nor does a displayed endpoint state prove that a particular switch caused it. A click or indication alone does not establish electrical continuity, correct wiring, or the switch’s role in the robot’s control system. Do not use those observations as substitutes for the machine-specific documentation.

Use only the machine’s approved access procedure. Do not infer wiring, terminal identity, or operating state from a component’s physical layout. ## When the location cannot be confirmed

If the machine documentation does not show a micro switch for the endpoint, report its location as unconfirmed rather than guessing from a nearby component, motion stop, alarm, or display. The endpoint may use another sensing method, or the switch may have a different mounting arrangement than expected. Neither possibility identifies a physical location without design-specific evidence.

Consult the machine-specific technical documentation or qualified service personnel for identification. A reliable location claim requires documentation that establishes the switch, its mounting point, and the mechanical path to its actuator. Until those details are confirmed, do not attribute the robot’s endpoint indication or control response to a micro switch.
