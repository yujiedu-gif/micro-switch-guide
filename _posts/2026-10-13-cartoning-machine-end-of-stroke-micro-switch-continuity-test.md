For a cartoning machine end of stroke micro switch continuity test, first confirm that the machine’s end-of-stroke input actually uses an electromechanical micro switch. An isolated continuity measurement can show how the identified contacts behave in the actuator-released and actuator-operated states. It cannot establish that the machine’s wiring, input circuitry, control sequence, or overall safety is correct.

The useful comparison is between two defined actuator states, interpreted against the contact arrangement documented for the exact switch. A single reading, an assumed terminal layout, or an audible click is not enough to diagnose the complete end-of-stroke function. Keep the component test separate from checks of the linkage and the machine circuit.

## What this cartoning machine end of stroke micro switch continuity test can establish

This procedure applies only when the cartoning machine’s end-of-stroke input contains a micro switch. The name of the machine or the presence of a travel-related input does not establish that a particular design uses one. If the component is not confirmed as a micro switch, this test description may not apply.

With the switch electrically isolated from the surrounding circuit, a continuity test can compare contact behavior in two specified conditions: actuator released and actuator operated. The readings are useful only when the tested terminal pair and expected contact states are known from the switch’s markings or documentation. They describe the switch under those test conditions, not the machine’s complete electrical behavior.

An end-of-stroke event also depends on how the machine’s mechanism moves the switch and how the surrounding circuit uses its contacts. A result at the isolated switch cannot verify that the linkage reaches the actuator correctly, that connected wiring is intact, or that the machine’s control logic responds as intended. Nor does a component-level reading establish that the machine is safe or fully functional.

Treat the test as one limited observation in a diagnosis, not as a pass-or-fail verdict on the cartoning machine. If the contact results match the documented states, that supports only the tested switch-contact behavior in those states. If they do not match, the cause still needs to be distinguished from incorrect identification, invalid test conditions, or inadequate mechanical actuation.

## Isolate the switch and identify its terminals before testing

Before making a continuity measurement, follow the applicable machine isolation procedure and verify the required conditions for the work. The switch must be electrically isolated from the surrounding circuit for the component reading to be meaningful. Disconnect it only as part of a safe, authorized procedure; do not probe a live circuit.

For United States workplace electrical work, the cited rule generally requires exposed live parts to be deenergized before work. A qualified person must verify the deenergized condition with test equipment, and work on energized parts is limited to qualified persons. Follow all applicable requirements. If the machine’s isolation cannot be verified, do not proceed with this component test; refer the work to qualified personnel.

Once work can proceed under the applicable procedure, identify the switch terminals from the switch’s documented markings or connection diagram. Do not assign terminal roles from the terminal’s physical position, shape, or appearance. The documented connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load for the exact switch and equipment govern identification and use; a generic layout is not a substitute.

Determine which documented contact pair is to be measured and what contact state is expected in each reference condition. Do not assume that every micro switch uses the same terminal arrangement or contact behavior. If the documentation does not identify the relevant terminals or expected states, the test result cannot be interpreted reliably from appearance alone. Resolve the identification before taking readings rather than guessing.

## Measure continuity in released and actuated states

With the switch isolated and the relevant contact pair identified, place the actuator in its released state. Use the continuity function of the measuring instrument across that documented pair, and record the result with the actuator state. The state reference matters: a reading without a clear account of whether the actuator was released or operated cannot be compared reliably with the documented contact arrangement.

Next, move the actuator through the intended end-of-stroke action and measure the same contact pair again. Record that result as the actuator-operated state. Do not force the actuator beyond the motion permitted by the design. The purpose is to compare the two defined states, not to test the switch by applying extra force or travel.

Compare both observations with the contact-state expectations documented for the exact switch and reference condition. Do not impose a universal continuity pattern, numerical threshold, or assumed normal state. The available evidence does not establish one pattern for every micro switch. If the documentation gives no expectation for the identified pair and actuator states, the readings remain observations without a supported pass-or-fail interpretation.

Keep the conditions attached to each result: the switch was isolated, the terminal pair was identified from documentation, and the actuator was either released or operated. If a reading appears inconsistent, verify those conditions and the measuring connections before drawing a conclusion. A single measurement in one state does not show whether the contact behavior changes as documented between states.

## Interpret readings without overdiagnosing the micro switch

A mismatch between a measured result and the documented state expectation may point to a contact or actuation issue, but it does not identify the cause by itself. First confirm that the switch was isolated, the correct terminal pair was selected, the measuring connections were sound, and the actuator actually reached the intended state. An error in any of those conditions can make the observation misleading.

Then distinguish electrical contact behavior from mechanical input. If the actuator does not move as intended during the end-of-stroke action, the linkage or other mechanism may not be actuating the switch adequately. That possibility is different from a contact problem inside the isolated switch. A click may indicate movement, but it does not prove continuity, correct terminal identification, or correct operation of the surrounding circuit.

If the isolated switch behaves as documented in both states, the test supports only that limited finding. It does not test the machine wiring, the input circuitry, or the control sequence. A fault elsewhere in the cartoning machine can therefore remain possible even when the isolated contact readings match expectations. Conversely, a disputed reading should not be attributed to the switch until the test conditions, terminal identification, and actuator movement are checked.

Keep these possibilities separate rather than treating an end-of-stroke symptom as proof of a failed switch. The component test addresses contact behavior at the switch. The mechanical path that moves the actuator and the electrical path that carries the resulting signal require their own diagnosis under the applicable procedure. This article does not establish how every cartoning machine implements end-of-stroke control.

## Decide what the continuity test leaves unresolved

If the isolated switch’s readings match the documented contact states for both released and operated conditions, record that result as a component-level finding. It does not establish that the linkage actuates the switch correctly in the machine, that the wiring is intact, or that the control system responds as intended. Further machine-circuit diagnosis belongs to the approved procedure and must be performed by personnel with the appropriate qualification.

If the readings do not match, or if they remain unclear, do not infer a definitive cause from the continuity test alone. Recheck the defined states, terminal identification, isolation, and measurement connections. Consider whether the actuator reached the intended position before distinguishing a contact issue from a mechanical actuation problem. The evidence available here does not provide a universal reading or threshold that settles the diagnosis for every switch.

Stop rather than proceeding if safe machine isolation cannot be verified, the terminals or expected states cannot be identified, or the next step would involve work outside the applicable qualification. Refer unresolved work to qualified personnel. Do not use a click, one reading, or a successful isolated test as proof that the cartoning machine is safe or fully functional. The cartoning machine end of stroke micro switch continuity test is limited to documented contact behavior in the states actually measured.
