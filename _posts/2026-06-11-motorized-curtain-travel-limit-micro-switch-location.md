A motorized curtain travel limit micro switch location depends on the drive design; there is no universal mounting point. Some systems use an electromechanical micro switch to sense a travel boundary, while others use a different sensing or control arrangement. The curtain’s edge, motor housing, and controller are not interchangeable clues to the switch’s position.

When a micro switch is present, identify it by tracing how motion reaches its actuator, using system documentation and only safely visible mechanical parts. A switch may sit near a drive mechanism or linkage rather than beside the visible edge of the curtain. Do not infer its presence or location from a click, a stopped curtain, or the appearance of a control board alone.

## Where a motorized curtain travel limit micro switch location depends on the drive

There is no single location that applies to every motorized curtain. The drive design determines whether a micro switch is used and, if it is, where a moving part can operate the switch at the intended travel boundary. The switch’s position follows that mechanical relationship, not a universal rule about curtain rails, edges, or motor housings.

A design that uses a micro switch places it where a drive-linked part can move its actuator as the curtain mechanism reaches a defined travel boundary. The relevant location is therefore the point at which the mechanism’s motion can reach the actuator. The component may be mounted near part of the drive or along a linkage, but the available evidence does not establish a particular housing or mounting point for all systems.

Other systems may use a different limit-sensing or control method. A curtain reaching an endpoint does not by itself show which method is used. Nor does the fact that a motorized curtain has a motor prove that it contains a micro switch. Treat the switch’s presence and position as design-specific facts to confirm in documentation or through safe, visible identification.

Travel limits also should not be used to guess the switch’s exact geometry. Numerical travel limits are requirements of the applicable specification sheet, not values that can be inferred from the component category. Where a switch specification defines travel, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position; overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These definitions do not identify where a curtain-system switch is mounted or establish the travel limits of an unspecified device.

## Trace the mechanical path to a travel limit micro switch

Begin with the system documentation, if available, to learn whether the design specifies a micro switch for travel-limit sensing. If a relevant mechanism is safely visible, trace the motion path: start with the curtain drive, identify the moving part associated with end-of-travel detection, and follow its mechanical relationship toward any switch actuator. The goal is to establish a plausible physical actuation path, not to identify a switch by proximity alone.

The switch may be near a drive mechanism or linkage rather than at the visible curtain edge. A curtain’s endpoint describes where the curtain stops; it does not establish where the sensing component sits. Similarly, seeing a motor does not show that a limit switch is mounted on or inside its housing. Without system-specific evidence, do not assume a rail, housing, or other particular structure contains the switch.

Look for a mechanical relationship in which movement of a drive-linked part could operate the actuator at the travel boundary. A nearby component without that relationship is not enough to establish that it is the travel-limit micro switch. If the actuator or linkage cannot be seen safely, documentation may be needed to resolve the location. Do not remove covers or dismantle equipment to complete this visual trace.

Keep the identification question narrow. The task is to determine whether a documented or safely visible electromechanical micro switch occupies the travel-limit position, not to adjust a limit or diagnose the whole curtain system. The supplied evidence does not specify a universal actuator form, mounting arrangement, or location, so those details remain unresolved unless the system’s own documentation establishes them.

## Distinguish the micro switch from the motor and controller

A micro switch is the electromechanical component that may be operated by mechanical movement. The motor supplies motion, while a controller may interpret or respond to a limit input. These parts can be associated within one system, but they are not the same component, and the motor’s location does not establish the switch’s location.

The effect of a travel-limit event depends on the actual circuit and control design. A stopped curtain, for example, does not prove that a micro switch caused the stop. The system could use another sensing or control arrangement, and the observed result alone does not reveal which one. Do not infer a particular contact state, wiring, or control response from the word “limit.”

A click is also insufficient to identify the component or confirm its function. It does not establish that the sound came from a micro switch, that the switch is at a particular point, or that it is part of the travel-limit arrangement. Likewise, a visible control board does not establish that a micro switch is present or show where one is mounted. These observations may prompt a closer look at documentation, but they do not resolve the design.

Keep the switch separate from the curtain assembly as a whole. The curtain and drive create motion; a linkage or other moving part may pass that motion to a switch actuator; and the controller’s response depends on the system design. Evidence for one link in that chain does not prove every other link. Confirm the switch and its location only when documentation or safely visible mechanical evidence supports that conclusion.

This provides a bounded way to identify the component without assuming a standard mounting point or treating the motor, controller, or curtain edge as the switch.

Do not remove covers or work near energized parts as an identification shortcut. If the relevant mechanism is not safely visible, stop at the available evidence rather than opening equipment to search for a switch. If documentation does not confirm the design and no safe view establishes a mechanical path to an actuator, leave both the switch’s presence and its location unresolved. A click, symptom, or stopped curtain cannot fill that evidence gap. Seek qualified service when identification requires access beyond safe visibility.

The practical answer is design-dependent: a travel-limit micro switch, when used, is positioned so a drive-linked moving part can operate its actuator at the intended boundary.
