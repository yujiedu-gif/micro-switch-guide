## What an assembly robot travel limit micro switch replacement involves

On some robot designs, a micro switch provides a mechanical input associated with a travel boundary. A moving part, actuator, or linkage can contact the switch, but the exact arrangement and resulting control response depend on the robot’s documented design. The switch is a component in the motion system, not a complete description of how the robot limits travel.

Keep two positions distinct. The switch’s mechanical actuation point is the position at which the switch is operated by the mechanism. The robot’s programmed stopping position, if applicable, is a motion-control setting. One should not be treated as proof of the other: a switch may change state at one point while the robot’s control system determines how motion responds. The available information does not establish a universal relationship between those positions or specify a transition point for a particular robot.

Before replacing the component, identify it through the robot’s documentation and its role in the travel-limit arrangement. Do not assume that every travel limit uses a micro switch, or that a particular contact arrangement, wiring pattern, or stopping behavior applies to all robots. Follow the documented design rather than inferring function from appearance, location, or an actuator’s movement.

The replacement must preserve the intended mechanical relationship between the actuator and the moving mechanism. Record the existing mounting position and contact relationship before removal so they can be compared with the documented arrangement afterward. Numerical travel limits are specific to the applicable specification sheet; they must not be inferred from the component category. If the documentation does not provide the required limits or adjustment criteria, the correct setting remains unresolved.

## When replacement does not restore the travel limit

A replacement that does not restore the expected travel-limit behavior calls for separating possible causes, not immediately changing the switch position. The documented failure paths include misalignment, damaged actuator contact, wiring faults, or a separate control-system issue. Any of these may require a different inspection or service action; the symptom alone does not determine which one is present.

Compare the installed position and actuator contact with the recorded reference and the robot’s documented arrangement. If the mechanical relationship differs, treat that as an alignment concern to resolve only by the approved instructions. If the switch transition is not confirmed under the specified check, investigate within the documented switch and wiring procedures. If the switch transition is confirmed but motion does not respond as intended, do not conclude that the switch has failed again: a separate control-system issue remains possible.

Do not bypass the switch, defeat an interlock, or move the travel limit without approved instructions. If the cause remains unresolved, or if any safety-related motion behavior is unexpected, stop and escalate to qualified service personnel. The assembly robot travel limit micro switch replacement is complete only when the documented component relationship and verification path have been addressed; a replacement part or isolated switch check alone does not establish correct robot stopping behavior.
