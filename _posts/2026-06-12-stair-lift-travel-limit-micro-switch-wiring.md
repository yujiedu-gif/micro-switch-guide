A **stair lift travel limit micro switch wiring** arrangement may use a small electromechanical switch to detect a position at the end of permitted travel. The switch can change an electrical contact state when the lift’s mechanism moves its actuator, but that change alone does not establish what the lift’s control circuit will do. The complete limit-control system includes the switch, its mechanical input, wiring, and the lift’s model-specific control design.

The useful distinction is between the switch’s local action and the system response. Identifying the switch terminals or observing a contact change does not reveal, by itself, whether the lift stops, signals a condition, or responds in another way. Those details must come from the applicable lift and switch documentation.

## stair lift travel limit micro switch wiring: What the switch does

A micro switch may serve as one component in travel-limit sensing. At a travel limit, a stop, cam, linkage, or another part of the lift mechanism may move the switch actuator. If the particular switch design has a snap-action mechanism, actuator movement can cause its contacts to change state. The switch reports a local mechanical event through that contact change; it is not the lift’s complete limit-control system.

The exact mechanical arrangement is not established by the presence of a micro switch. A lift may use a particular mechanism to contact the actuator, but the arrangement, actuator movement, and position at which contact occurs depend on the actual design. Do not infer a universal mounting position, direction of actuator movement, or adjustment method from the term “travel limit switch.”

Likewise, the switch’s contact transition does not establish the lift’s response. The control circuit could interpret the signal according to the lift’s design, but the available facts do not establish whether a particular lift stops, sounds an alarm, changes direction, or takes another action. Treat those as control-system questions, not properties of the switch alone.

## How contact labels relate to a travel-limit signal

COM, NO, and NC identify contacts only when the specific switch’s contact arrangement and reference condition are documented. COM means common. NO and NC describe contact states relative to the stated unoperated or other specified reference condition. They are not universal wire names, and the labels alone do not establish the lift’s control logic.

For a documented arrangement, actuator movement may change which contacts are connected. To interpret that change, use the switch’s diagram and the specified actuator state. Do not assume that a contact labeled NO is the one used by a travel-limit circuit, or that NC must be used. The circuit designer’s choice and the expected signal depend on the specific lift design.

A contact-state change is not proof of a particular system outcome. Even if a switch changes state as expected in an isolated check, that result does not show how the lift’s powered control circuit interprets the signal. It also does not establish that the switch is correctly wired into the lift or that the rest of the system responds properly. Keep the component-level observation separate from conclusions about stopping, alarms, or motion.

## Why stair-lift wiring details are design-specific

Terminal assignments, wiring paths, and control responses must be established from the documentation for the exact switch and lift. A generic physical-layout inference is not a reliable way to identify a terminal. Use the documented connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load for the exact equipment. If those details are unavailable or unclear, the connection remains unresolved.

Wire colors do not substitute for terminal identification. A color may describe a conductor in a particular documented installation, but no universal color assignment follows from the fact that a micro switch is used for travel-limit sensing. Nor does a generic sketch establish which wire belongs on COM, NO, or NC. Do not transfer a terminal arrangement from another lift, switch, or drawing unless the applicable documentation confirms it.

The switch’s permissible load is also a model-specific boundary. A contact transition can be observed without knowing whether the connected circuit is an appropriate load for that switch. Do not infer a rating or suitability from appearance, contact labels, or the fact that the switch is installed in a lift. The applicable documentation must establish the permitted load and connection.

Most importantly, a switch diagram is not a complete lift wiring diagram. Even a correct identification of the switch terminals does not explain every connection in the host control circuit or prove what the circuit will do when the input changes. Keep the switch-level diagram and the lift’s system documentation distinct, and do not fill gaps with assumed colors, pinouts, or control behavior.

## What a continuity check can and cannot establish

A continuity check can address the isolated switch’s contacts only when the test procedure, actuator states, and expected behavior are defined by the applicable documentation. The test concerns the switch separated from the lift circuit, with the actuator in each specified state. Compare the reading with the documented contact arrangement and state; an unexplained reading cannot establish which part of the installed system is responsible for a symptom.

An isolated contact reading has a narrow meaning. It does not show that the switch is installed at the correct travel position, that the mechanical input moves it as intended, that the wiring is correct, or that the lift’s control circuit responds properly. It does not establish that the installed circuit is safe. A result consistent with the documented switch behavior therefore cannot, by itself, prove the whole lift works correctly.

Do not convert a continuity observation into a diagnosis of the complete travel-limit system. A problem could involve the mechanical input, the switch contacts, terminal identification, wiring, or equipment elsewhere. The available evidence does not supply a universal diagnostic sequence or expected reading for a particular lift. If the applicable documentation does not define a safe, model-specific check, leave the verification to qualified service personnel rather than improvising a test.

## When to stop and seek qualified service

Do not bypass a travel-limit switch or improvise connections to see how the lift responds. A bypass would alter the system being evaluated, and no safe or appropriate bypass procedure is established here. Do not infer a connection from wire color or a similar-looking switch. When the exact diagram, terminal assignment, permissible load, or expected contact behavior cannot be confirmed, stop short of making a wiring change.

Electrical work must remain within applicable requirements. Under the cited United States workplace rule, exposed live parts generally must be deenergized before work; a qualified person must verify the deenergized condition with test equipment; and work on energized parts is limited to qualified persons. Verify all applicable requirements. This boundary does not supply a procedure for working on a stair lift or make an isolated switch test sufficient to establish that the equipment is safe.

The practical conclusion is limited but useful: a micro switch may provide a contact-state change when a travel-limit mechanism moves its actuator, while the lift’s response depends on its documented control design. For **stair lift travel limit micro switch wiring**, confirm switch terminals, contact states, and permissible load from the exact documentation, and leave unresolved system behavior or electrical work beyond a documented safe procedure to qualified service personnel.
