For a dock leveler guard interlock, **dock leveler guard interlock micro switch operating force** refers to a force parameter of the switch, not automatically to the force a person applies at the guard. The distinction matters because a guard, lever, cam, or linkage may transfer force through a mechanical arrangement before it reaches the switch actuator. Without the exact switch documentation and the installed geometry, the two forces cannot be treated as equal or used to establish suitability.

A dock leveler interlock may use a micro switch, but the switch is a component within the host mechanism, not another name for the complete interlock. The parameter can describe the switch’s actuation requirement only within its stated definition and conditions. It does not, by itself, establish the interlock’s electrical logic, the force required at the guard, or whether the overall system meets an acceptance or safety requirement.

## What dock leveler guard interlock micro switch operating force describes

Operating force, also called actuating force in the cited definition, is the force or torque applied to the actuating mechanism to operate the contacts. That definition identifies the switch actuator as the point where the parameter applies. It does not say that the same force is present at a guard or elsewhere in the interlock.

The applicable definition is given in MIL-PRF-8805K, section 6.5.1. A definition alone does not supply a numerical value, a universal range, or the conditions under which a particular switch value was measured. Those details must come from the documentation for the exact switch. If the measurement point or test conditions are not available, a number labeled “operating force” cannot be reliably interpreted for the installed arrangement from its name alone.

The actuator is the part of the switch moved by the external mechanism. In a guard interlock, an external part may contact or move that actuator, directly or through other components. The switch’s operating-force parameter concerns what is applied at its actuating mechanism to operate the contacts; it should not be reported as the force applied at the guard unless the documentation and mechanical arrangement establish that relationship.

This is a mechanical definition, not a description of the interlock’s full function. It does not identify whether contacts are normally open or normally closed, what the host circuit does when they change state, or what guard position the equipment treats as acceptable. Those points depend on the actual switch, wiring, and equipment design and should not be inferred from the force parameter.

## How the guard mechanism transfers force to a micro switch

Some dock leveler guard interlocks may use a micro switch actuated by a guard, lever, cam, or linkage. That describes a possible arrangement, not a feature of every dock leveler or guard interlock. The switch and the surrounding mechanical and electrical system remain distinct: the guard mechanism supplies motion, the switch responds at its actuator, and the host circuit determines the consequence of a contact change.

Force can be transferred through the mechanism between the point where a person acts on the guard and the switch actuator. The geometry and position of the components affect that transfer. A direct contact, a lever, or a linkage can place the switch actuator at a different location and in a different mechanical relationship to the applied guard force. Alignment and the design of the interlock also matter. Consequently, a switch operating-force value cannot be converted into a guard-force value without evidence about the actual mechanism.

The reverse inference is also unsupported. A force felt at the guard does not, by itself, reveal the force at the switch actuator or prove that the contacts have operated. The arrangement may involve other mechanical resistance or constraints, and the available claims provide no quantitative relationship between guard force and actuator force. Do not assume a one-to-one force transfer or calculate one value from the other without documented geometry and relevant data.

A change in switch contacts also does not disclose the host equipment’s response on its own. The force definition does not establish the interlock’s normal state, circuit logic, or behavior when a guard moves. Those are separate design questions. Treating the switch’s parameter as an interlock setting or as a statement about dock leveler behavior would extend the definition beyond what it establishes.

## Why a switch force value alone cannot establish interlock suitability

A switch force value is only interpretable for an application when its scope is clear. Relevant evidence includes the exact switch documentation, the parameter’s stated test conditions and measurement point, the actuator interface, and the installed mechanism geometry. Without those details, the force at the switch and the force at the guard remain distinct quantities with an unresolved relationship.

Even when a documented operating-force value is available, that value describes the switch parameter under its stated conditions. It does not establish a universal force limit for dock leveler guards, a host-system acceptance criterion, or a safety requirement for a particular installation. No universal operating-force range or dock-leveler requirement follows from the definition. Suitability therefore remains unresolved unless the switch and installation evidence, along with the applicable host-system requirements, support a conclusion.

Keep the component and system decisions separate. A switch’s operating force concerns actuation at the switch. Whether the installed interlock works as intended depends on the mechanical interface and the equipment’s actual design and requirements. A value for one component cannot, by itself, establish that the complete guard interlock is suitable or functioning correctly.

Other force-related terms should not be substituted for operating force. For example, the cited definition of overtravel is the distance or angle between the operating position and the extreme position to which the actuator may be moved. The cited release-force definition concerns the force or torque to which force on the actuator must be reduced to permit the contacts to return to the unoperated position after operation. These describe different aspects of switch behavior; neither supplies the missing relationship between force at the switch and force at the guard.

## How to verify the operating-force question safely

Start with the exact switch and interlock documentation. Confirm how the documentation defines operating force, where the force is applied or measured, and which test conditions apply. Then identify the documented actuator interface and installation conditions. The purpose is to determine whether a switch parameter can be related to the installed mechanism, not to assume that a force at the guard equals the stated switch value.

For the equipment and interlock, use the applicable connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. A generic inference from physical layout does not establish terminal identification or circuit behavior. Follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. Do not push, adjust, bypass, or electrically test an installed safety interlock without an approved procedure; this article does not provide an installation-specific test procedure.

Movement or a click is not enough to establish correct operation. It does not, by itself, prove contact condition, correct wiring, or the host interlock’s function. Nor does an operating-force value alone establish that the guard mechanism meets a system requirement. If the documentation does not identify the relevant conditions or the safe procedure is unavailable, leave the assessment to qualified personnel rather than improvising a test or adjustment.

The defensible conclusion is limited: operating force is a switch-actuator parameter under its documented definition and conditions. The force applied at the guard is a separate quantity whose relationship to that parameter depends on the actual mechanism. Without the exact switch evidence and installation geometry, dock leveler guard interlock micro switch operating force cannot establish guard force or interlock suitability.
