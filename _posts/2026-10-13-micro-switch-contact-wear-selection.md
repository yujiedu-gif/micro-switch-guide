Contact appearance can provide a clue about a micro switch, but it cannot establish how much electrical life remains or whether the switch suits a particular duty. Micro switch contact wear selection should therefore begin with documented electrical ratings and test conditions for the intended load, then treat visible wear as limited supporting evidence. A click or an inspection of appearance alone does not verify electrical contact performance.

## What micro switch contact wear can, and cannot, tell you

Contact wear is a possible observed condition, not a universal visual diagnosis. A visible mark may prompt closer evaluation, but appearance alone does not establish the contact’s electrical behavior, the cause of a change, or the amount of service life remaining. There is no general visual wear score here that can convert an appearance into a reliable life estimate.

Keep three kinds of evidence separate. Visual evidence describes what can be seen. Electrical evidence describes measured behavior under a defined method and state. Life evidence reports performance under a stated endurance procedure. They answer different questions and cannot substitute for one another.

A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, contact condition, correct wiring, or suitability for a load. Likewise, a contact-resistance result is meaningful only in the context of the applicable specification and test procedure. Under MIL-PRF-8805K, contact resistance is a measured switch requirement; the method, connection points, and limit must come from the applicable specification and test procedure. Without those details, an isolated number or observation is not enough to judge performance.

Treat wear observations as a reason to check the supporting evidence, not as a stand-alone pass/fail criterion. In particular, do not use appearance to claim that a switch has reached the end of its electrical life, or that it has substantial life remaining. Those conclusions require relevant documented evidence and conditions.

## Separate electrical life from mechanical life

Mechanical life and electrical life are distinct measures. Mechanical life concerns mechanical operation under the conditions defined for that measure. Electrical life concerns switching operation under specified electrical conditions. A count for one is not a substitute for a count for the other, and neither should be read as an unconditional prediction of service life in an application.

Electrical switching conditions matter when interpreting an endurance figure. A published electrical operation count must be read with its stated load, operating sequence, and test conditions. If those details differ from the intended duty, or are missing, the count does not establish the same life in that duty. Do not convert an electrical endurance count into a generic promise about how long a switch will operate.

A mechanical-life figure also cannot settle whether the contacts will perform appropriately while switching the intended load. Conversely, visible contact wear does not by itself show that a mechanical-life limit has been reached. Keep the measure, test conditions, and question being answered aligned.

If the relevant life evidence is unavailable, state the limitation rather than inferring a life expectancy from appearance, a click, or a different life category. The appropriate next step is to obtain the applicable documentation or seek qualified evaluation, not to invent a remaining-life estimate.

## Micro switch contact wear selection: match the evidence to the actual duty

Selection should turn on whether documented ratings and test conditions match the intended application’s actual electrical duty. First identify the load type and electrical conditions, including whether the applicable rating is for AC or DC. Then check that the rating and its stated test condition cover those facts. A rating for one current type, load, or condition does not establish another.

Do not transfer an AC rating to DC, or a rating for one load type to another. Use only the rating explicitly stated for the applicable current type, load, and test condition. If the available documentation does not establish the intended conditions, suitability remains unresolved. That gap is not, by itself, proof that contact wear caused a problem or that the switch is defective.

Next, compare the stated switching duty with any life or endurance evidence. A published operation count is relevant only with its load, sequence, and test conditions attached. If the intended duty cannot be matched to those conditions, the figure cannot support a dependable life conclusion. Do not rank switches by a bare cycle count or treat a count from unlike conditions as a wear score.

This approach separates two questions: whether the documented electrical conditions match the application, and what the wear evidence can establish about the observed switch. A suitable documented rating does not prove that a particular switch’s contacts are unworn or functioning correctly. Visible wear does not prove that a rating is inadequate. Each question needs evidence appropriate to it.

## What to verify before making a selection decision

Gather the documentation needed to compare the switch with the intended duty: its applicable electrical ratings, the current type and load those ratings cover, relevant switching conditions, and any life-test load, operating sequence, and test conditions. Also identify the documented contact arrangement if it matters to the circuit. Do not assume a contact arrangement or terminal meaning that the documentation does not establish.

If contact resistance is part of the evaluation, use the applicable specification and test procedure for its method, connection points, and limit. A resistance value without that context is not a complete comparison. An isolated-switch check and an assessment of installed wiring or equipment are also different tasks: a result from an isolated component does not establish that the surrounding circuit is correctly wired or functioning.

Keep verification within an appropriate, defined procedure. Do not infer that a single observation or measurement establishes the safety or condition of an installed assembly. If the required test method, safe procedure, or interpretation is not established, leave that work to qualified personnel. The purpose of gathering evidence is to identify what is documented and what remains unknown, not to improvise an inspection or repair method.

A selection decision has a firmer basis when the applicable ratings match the actual load and electrical conditions, and the life evidence is interpretable against the intended duty. If a key condition is absent or mismatched, record the uncertainty and seek further qualified evaluation rather than filling the gap with an appearance-based conclusion.

## Decision boundary: when wear evidence is not enough

Wear observations can guide what evidence to review, but they cannot independently establish remaining electrical life, suitability, or a definitive fault. A click is not proof of electrical contact performance. A visual inspection is not a substitute for a defined electrical test, and a test result is not a universal verdict about the installed equipment.

Before concluding that a switch suits the intended duty, establish the applicable rating for the actual current type, load, and test condition, and interpret any endurance count using its stated load and operating sequence. If those facts are missing, the decision remains unresolved. Do not infer an expected life or diagnose a wear-related cause from an unmatched rating or an appearance alone.

The practical boundary is straightforward: documented conditions can support further evaluation when they match the intended duty; incomplete or mismatched evidence cannot settle suitability. Keep observations, measurements, life data, and application requirements distinct. When electrical safety or test interpretation is uncertain, qualified personnel should assess it. Micro switch contact wear selection is an evidence-matching task, not a visual prediction of service life.
