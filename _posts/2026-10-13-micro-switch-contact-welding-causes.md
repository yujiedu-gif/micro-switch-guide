Contacts inside a micro switch can weld when electrical stress at the switching contacts produces arcing and material fusion. The phrase micro switch contact welding causes describes a possible failure mechanism, not a diagnosis that can be made from a symptom alone. Whether a particular switch is suitable depends on its design, the load, and the electrical limits documented for it under applicable conditions.

A switch that appears to remain electrically closed may instead have a sticking actuator, misidentified terminals, a wiring error, or a fault elsewhere in the equipment. A click does not distinguish these possibilities or establish contact condition. Verification therefore needs a defined actuator state and an isolated switch, and its result must be interpreted within the documented contact arrangement. Even then, a continuity check addresses only the isolated switch, not the safety or condition of the complete circuit.

## Micro switch contact welding causes: the direct mechanism

Contact welding means that switching contacts remain joined because their contact material has fused. The supported causal path is electrical stress at the contacts, followed by arcing and material fusion. This explains how welding can occur, but it does not show that every switch which fails to change state has welded contacts, or identify the cause in a particular installation.

The relevant stress is tied to the actual switching application. A switch’s suitability depends on its design, the load it switches, and the documented electrical limits and test conditions that apply. Electrical ratings are not universal switching capacities: rating values are specification-sheet requirements tied to the stated load and test conditions. Without those details for the switch and circuit in question, the stress level and the reason for a failure remain unresolved.

A useful diagnosis separates the physical failure mechanism from the observation that prompted concern. Contacts that remain electrically joined after the actuator changes state may be consistent with welding, but that observation should be checked against the switch’s documented terminal arrangement and reference condition. It does not, by itself, establish why the contacts remained joined or rule out other faults in the mechanism or equipment.

## Electrical conditions that can raise welding risk

The relevant concern is whether the electrical stress imposed by the real load and switching conditions exceeds what the particular switch is documented to handle. The applicable specification-sheet requirements connect rating values to stated loads and test conditions. A number taken without its load and test context cannot establish suitability for a different circuit. Nor does a general description of a switch or its contacts establish that it is suitable for an unspecified application.

The evidence available here does not establish numerical thresholds, a universal ranking of stressors, or separate conclusions for particular load types or AC and DC circuits. Those distinctions must not be guessed. If the applicable documentation does not identify the load and test conditions relevant to the installation, the conditions that may have contributed to welding cannot be determined from a general rating reference alone.

This boundary matters when interpreting a failure. Evidence that contacts are electrically joined may support the observation that the switch is not changing state as expected. It does not identify whether the underlying cause was unsuitable electrical stress, nor does it justify assigning a cause to a particular voltage, current, load, or switching event when those details are not established. The cause remains conditional on the actual switch, load, and documented limits.

A label or a nominal rating should therefore be read as conditional information, not as proof that the switch is appropriate for every circuit at or below an assumed value. The applicable requirements and test conditions are part of the meaning of the rating. Where those conditions are missing or do not match the known application, suitability and cause remain unknown rather than confirmed.

## Welding, sticking, and circuit faults are different diagnoses

An apparent failure to change state can arise at different points. Contacts may remain electrically closed; an actuator or its mechanism may fail to move as intended; terminals may be identified incorrectly; wiring may be wrong; or a fault may lie elsewhere in the equipment. These possibilities are not interchangeable explanations, and a symptom at the equipment level does not locate the fault inside the switch.

Mechanical sticking concerns movement of the actuator or mechanism. Contact welding concerns an electrical connection that remains because contact material has fused. A mechanism that does not move freely can therefore mimic an electrical contact failure in the observed result, while an electrical continuity result does not by itself explain whether the actuator moved correctly. The two questions require distinct observations.

Terminal identification also matters. A continuity result has meaning only when the tested terminals and the documented contact arrangement are known, and the actuator is in a defined reference state. If the terminals were assumed rather than identified from applicable documentation, an unexpected reading may reflect that identification error rather than welded contacts. Likewise, incorrect wiring or a downstream circuit fault can produce an equipment symptom without demonstrating an internal switch failure.

A click is not proof that contacts are electrically open or closed, that the terminals are wired correctly, or that the load is suitable. It can indicate movement, but it cannot distinguish contact welding from a problem elsewhere. Treat it as an observation, not a diagnosis. The next useful distinction is whether the isolated switch’s documented contacts show the expected continuity for a specified actuator state.

## What an isolated continuity check can show

Continuity verification must be limited to the switch itself. The switch must be isolated, the actuator placed in a defined state, and the result compared with the documented contact arrangement and reference condition. Without those details, a reading cannot be interpreted reliably as evidence for or against welded contacts. A test of unspecified terminals or an unspecified actuator state does not establish the expected behavior.

If an isolated reading shows continuity where the documented arrangement and defined state call for an open contact, that is evidence of an unexpected electrical state at the tested points. It may be consistent with contacts remaining joined, but a single reading does not establish the cause. Confirming that terminals and reference conditions were identified correctly is essential to interpreting the observation; a reading alone cannot prove material fusion rather than another unresolved switch condition.

Conversely, a reading that matches the documented expectation in one state establishes only the result for the isolated switch, tested terminals, and defined state. It does not prove suitability for the installed load, correct wiring, or the condition of the complete assembly. Insulation resistance is a separate measurement: it is evaluated between specified electrically isolated points using the applicable test method and conditions. It should not be confused with a continuity check or interpreted without those conditions.

An isolated measurement also does not establish that an installed circuit is safe. It cannot rule out wiring or downstream faults, and it does not provide a complete assessment of the equipment. Work beyond a properly bounded isolated check, especially where the circuit or equipment cannot be safely isolated or its test conditions are unclear, should be left to qualified personnel rather than improvised from a switch definition.

## When the cause remains uncertain

A welding hypothesis becomes more plausible when a correctly identified, isolated switch shows continuity that conflicts with the documented contact arrangement in a defined actuator state. That observation supports an unexpected electrical state; it does not, by itself, prove fusion or explain the cause. Evidence about the actual load and applicable rating conditions would still be needed to assess whether electrical stress contributed.

If the actuator does not move as expected, mechanical sticking remains a separate possibility. If terminal identification is uncertain, or if wiring and other equipment have not been distinguished from the switch, the symptom cannot be assigned to welded contacts. A click, a reported equipment failure, or one continuity result is not enough to settle those alternatives.

The practical conclusion is bounded: electrical stress can lead to arcing and contact-material fusion, but the specific cause must be established for the particular switch and load. When the reference state, contact arrangement, isolation, or applicable electrical conditions are unresolved, report the cause as uncertain and seek qualified inspection as needed. Do not infer a whole-equipment diagnosis from a single test.
