A circuit that stays on or off does not, by itself, show that a micro switch has failed internally. **Micro switch contact welding explained** means examining a specific failure: contacts inside the switch adhere when they should separate. That is different from an actuator that cannot move, a wiring error, or a fault elsewhere in the equipment. The distinction matters because the same persistent output can have several causes. Contact separation under electrical load can produce arcing; under relevant load and switching conditions, arcing can cause localized heating and may lead to contact material fusing. Establishing that this mechanism occurred requires more than noticing a symptom or hearing a click. It also requires evidence about the switch, its contact arrangement, the connected load, and the conditions under which it operates. The explanation below separates the possible failure paths and sets limits on what an isolated check can establish.

## micro switch contact welding explained

Contact welding is an internal contact failure in which conductive contacts adhere to one another. If they remain adhered, their intended separation may not occur when the switch mechanism changes state. The term describes the condition of the contacts, not simply an equipment output that remains energized or de-energized.

A persistent output is a symptom, not a diagnosis. It may be consistent with contacts that have not separated, but it can also arise because the actuator or linkage has not moved as intended, because the terminals were misidentified, because wiring is incorrect, or because another part of the equipment is controlling the result. A circuit’s observed behavior alone does not locate the fault inside the switch.

The switch’s contact arrangement and reference state also matter when interpreting a test. Terminal labels and contact behavior cannot be assumed for an unidentified arrangement. In a documented arrangement, the relevant comparison is whether the contacts have the expected continuity in the specified actuator state. Without that identification and state, a reading cannot be called evidence of welding merely because it differs from an expectation based on an assumed terminal function.

Contact welding is therefore a narrower claim than “the switch is stuck.” It says that the internal contacts have adhered, not that every mechanism, circuit, or equipment condition producing a stuck output has the same cause. The physical mechanism can make welding plausible under some electrical conditions, but plausibility is not confirmation in a particular switch.

## How micro switch contacts can weld

The failure path begins at contact separation. When contacts separate while switching an electrical load, an arc may occur between them. An arc can concentrate heat in the small contact region. If the electrical load and switching conditions are sufficiently stressful for the particular switch, localized heating may contribute to contact material fusing. Once material has fused, the contacts may remain adhered rather than opening as intended.

This is a conditional mechanism, not a claim that every separation produces damaging arcing or that every arc welds a contact. Whether the mechanism is relevant depends on the documented switch and the load and test conditions applicable to it. The evidence here does not establish a universal voltage, current, load type, switching frequency, or threshold for welding. Those values should not be supplied by guesswork.

Electrical ratings must be read within their stated scope. Under MIL-PRF-8805K 6.1.1 and applicable specification sheets, rating values are requirements tied to stated load and test conditions, not universal switching capacities. A rating listed for one condition therefore does not establish suitability under a different load or operating condition. Nor does the general possibility of arcing prove that a particular switch was overloaded or that arcing caused its failure.

The causal chain is useful for understanding a suspected failure: contact separation, possible arcing, localized heating, and possible fusion. But each step remains conditional when the relevant operating evidence is absent. A conclusion about an actual switch needs more than the mechanism alone. It needs the applicable switch documentation and facts about the load and switching conditions, and it must be distinguished from other causes that can produce the same equipment symptom.

## What can resemble welded contacts

A mechanical input problem can prevent the switch from reaching the state expected by the surrounding mechanism. An actuator may be obstructed or binding, or a linkage may fail to move it through its intended motion. These conditions concern how force reaches or moves the switch; they do not establish that its internal contacts have fused. A click may indicate movement of a mechanism, but it does not prove that the contacts changed electrical state or that they are welded.

Terminal identification is a separate issue. If a tester or installer assumes the wrong terminal function or reference state, an unexpected continuity result may be misread as a stuck contact. The actual contact arrangement and the state in which terminal behavior is defined must be known before interpreting the result. A terminal-label misunderstanding is not evidence of internal fusion.

Wiring faults can also make an output persist independently of the switch’s internal contact condition. Likewise, a fault elsewhere in the equipment can produce behavior that appears to follow the switch. The equipment’s control logic determines how a contact change affects the larger circuit; observing the output does not isolate the switch as the cause.

These possibilities should remain distinct rather than being combined into one diagnosis. A mechanical inspection addresses whether the input can move; terminal documentation addresses identification; an isolated electrical check addresses switch continuity under a defined state; and analysis of wiring or equipment behavior concerns the larger circuit. Evidence from one path does not automatically settle the others. In particular, a click or an installed-system symptom cannot distinguish welded contacts from wiring or downstream faults on its own.

## How to assess a suspected welded contact safely

An electrical check can be meaningful only when the switch is isolated from the surrounding circuit, the terminal arrangement is documented, and the actuator state is defined. With those conditions established, continuity between the specified electrically isolated points can be assessed using the applicable test method and conditions. The expected result must be based on the documented contact arrangement and the selected actuator state, not on an assumed terminal pattern.

MIL-PRF-8805K’s insulation-resistance requirement and referenced test method define a different kind of assessment: insulation resistance is evaluated between specified electrically isolated points using the applicable test method and conditions. That requirement should not be confused with a continuity check for contact state. Each test has its own purpose, points, and method; neither should be interpreted without its stated conditions.

An isolated continuity result describes the switch under the test condition used. If contacts that should be separated remain electrically connected in the defined state, that observation may support a concern about failure to open. It does not, by itself, prove that the cause is welding rather than a different internal or identification problem. A result consistent with expected continuity also does not establish that the switch is suitable for its operating load or that the complete equipment circuit is functioning correctly.

A click is not a substitute for an electrical test: it can indicate movement, but not continuity, contact condition, or correct wiring. Installed-system behavior is also not a substitute for an isolated result because the rest of the circuit can affect the observed output. This guidance does not provide an installed-circuit test procedure. If electrical investigation cannot be limited to a properly isolated switch and its documented test conditions, leave that work to qualified personnel.

## When the evidence is not enough to call it contact welding

A firm conclusion is not justified when the switch’s contact arrangement, actuator state, applicable ratings, load, or switching conditions are unknown. A persistent equipment output and an audible click do not fill those gaps. Nor does a general description of arcing show that it occurred in a particular switch or that it fused the contacts.

The available rating evidence is conditional: MIL-PRF-8805K 6.1.1 and applicable specification sheets tie rating values to stated load and test conditions. Without the relevant documentation and operating facts, a reader cannot determine whether the switch was used within those conditions or whether electrical stress contributed to a failure. No universal cause or capacity follows from the existence of a micro switch.

Keep the conclusion proportionate to the observation. A stuck output identifies a symptom. An isolated test can report continuity for the documented terminals and actuator state under its test conditions. A conclusion of contact welding requires evidence supporting internal contact adhesion, while excluding plausible confusion about actuation, terminal identification, wiring, and the rest of the equipment. If those distinctions cannot be established from available evidence, the cause remains unresolved. Do not infer a repair method or replacement compatibility from this explanation.

In short, **micro switch contact welding explained** is a conditional failure mechanism, not a label for every stuck circuit: arcing can create localized heat during contact separation, and under relevant conditions that heat may contribute to fusion. A persistent output, a click, or a single isolated reading has limited scope. The switch documentation, load conditions, contact arrangement, and defined test state determine what can responsibly be concluded.
