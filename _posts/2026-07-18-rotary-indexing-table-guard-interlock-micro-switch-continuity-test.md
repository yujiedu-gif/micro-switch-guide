A rotary indexing table guard interlock micro switch continuity test can show whether the isolated switch’s contacts change as expected between defined actuator states, when those states and terminals are identified from the exact switch documentation. It cannot, by itself, establish that the guard interlock, machine control circuit, or complete machine functions correctly or is safe. The distinction matters because a fault may lie in the guard’s mechanical movement, the switch, its identification or wiring, or the downstream control system.

## What a rotary indexing table guard interlock micro switch continuity test can establish

A continuity check is a limited assessment of the micro switch as an isolated component. With the switch safely isolated according to the applicable equipment procedure, a person following the documented test method can compare contact behavior in specified actuator states with the behavior documented for that exact switch. A reading can therefore provide evidence about whether the tested contacts change state under those conditions.

The test has a narrow boundary. It does not establish that the switch is mounted or actuated correctly in the machine, that conductors are connected to the intended terminals, or that the control circuit responds as designed. Nor does a contact reading verify guard performance or prove that the machine is safe to operate. A result applies to the component, terminal pair, actuator state, and test conditions actually checked, not automatically to the installed interlock as a whole.

Interpret the reading against the switch’s documentation, not an assumed universal pattern. A generic changeover contact may use common, normally closed, and normally open labels, but that possibility does not establish the arrangement or markings of the particular switch. The exact device marking or schematic controls. Likewise, a continuity result does not establish suitability for a circuit load; use only the rating stated for the applicable current type, load, and test condition. A rating for one condition does not establish another.

## Separate the guard mechanism, micro switch, and control circuit

A guard interlock can involve distinct parts: movement of the guard, any linkage or other mechanism that transfers that movement, the micro switch actuator, the switch contacts, and the machine’s control circuit. The actual arrangement must be confirmed for the equipment in question. Some guard designs use a micro switch; the equipment name alone does not prove that this one does or show how its mechanism is arranged.

When a design uses a micro switch, guard movement may or may not produce the intended actuator movement. A continuity test performed on the isolated switch does not examine the guard, linkage, mounting, or mechanical relationship between them. If the actuator is not reaching the defined states during normal guard movement, a switch-only result may fail to reveal that installation or actuation issue.

The electrical consequence of a contact change depends on the actual circuit and control logic. Do not infer from the presence of a micro switch whether a contact is normally open or normally closed in the equipment, what machine response is intended, or whether the table stops or permits operation. Those details require the relevant equipment and switch documentation. The switch’s contact behavior and the interlock’s control behavior are related questions, but they are not the same test.

This separation also prevents an overbroad diagnosis. A machine symptom does not, on its own, prove that the micro switch has failed. The cause could be mechanical actuation, contact behavior, terminal identification, wiring, or another part of the control system. The continuity check addresses only a limited part of that possible fault path.

## Prepare only under the documented isolation procedure

Before any measurement, follow the applicable equipment procedure and qualification requirements to reach a safe isolated condition. Do not treat an isolated-component continuity check as permission to work on an energized circuit or as a substitute for the equipment’s required safety process. If the documented procedure does not cover the work, stop and refer it to qualified personnel.

Use the exact switch documentation to identify the contact arrangement and the terminals to be checked. Do not infer terminal identity from physical position, appearance, or a generic diagram. A generic changeover contact may be labeled common, normally closed, and normally open, but the actual marking or schematic for the switch controls. Do not assume that those labels are present or that the particular device has that arrangement.

The test must concern the isolated switch, not an assumed reading through installed wiring or other connected circuit elements. The method, terminal pair, and actuator states need to match the documentation. If a terminal cannot be identified, the documentation is unavailable, or the equipment procedure leaves the isolation or test boundary unclear, do not guess. Electrical work beyond the documented safe procedure belongs with qualified personnel.

## Check defined actuator states and interpret the readings

Compare readings only in actuator states that the switch documentation defines and that can be established under the applicable safe procedure. Record which documented terminals and states are being assessed, and use the documented test method. This ties a result to observable conditions instead of treating “continuity” as an unqualified pass or fail.

A change in reading between defined states may be consistent with the documented contact behavior for the isolated switch. It does not verify that guard movement produces those states in the installed assembly, that the right terminals are connected, or that the equipment’s control logic interprets the contact correctly. A continuity change alone therefore cannot certify the installed interlock.

If readings do not change as expected, or differ from the documented behavior, treat that as a reason to investigate rather than proof of one particular failure. First keep the test boundary clear: confirm that the intended terminals and defined actuator states were identified from the exact documentation and that the documented method was followed. The result may relate to terminal identification, the way the actuator state was established, or contact behavior. The reading alone does not distinguish among those possibilities conclusively.

Do not invent expected states or contact behavior when the documentation does not specify them. If the available information does not define a meaningful comparison, the test cannot support a reliable interpretation. Stop rather than supplying a generic terminal pattern or assuming a machine response.

## Use the result to choose the next diagnostic boundary

If the isolated switch behaves consistently with its documentation in the tested states, that supports only a conclusion about the contacts and conditions checked. It does not prove that the guard and linkage move the actuator correctly, that the installed terminals and wiring are correct, or that the downstream control circuit functions as intended. Those are separate diagnostic boundaries requiring the relevant equipment information and appropriate assessment.

If the readings are inconsistent, do not jump directly to replacing or repairing the switch. Keep possible causes distinct: terminal identification may be wrong; the actuator state may not match the documented test condition; the mechanical path may not produce the required motion; or contact behavior may differ from the documentation. Wiring and control-circuit faults cannot be settled by the isolated switch reading. The evidence from this check alone does not identify a single cause.

Do not bypass or defeat the guard interlock, and do not improvise repairs from a continuity result. When the required isolation, terminal identification, test method, or next diagnostic step is not covered by the documented procedure, stop and seek qualified assessment. The useful conclusion is deliberately limited: a properly scoped continuity check can assess documented contact behavior of an isolated micro switch in defined actuator states, but it cannot establish that the complete rotary indexing table guard interlock or machine is functional or safe.
