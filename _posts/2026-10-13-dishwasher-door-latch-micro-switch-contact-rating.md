A **dishwasher door latch micro switch contact rating** describes electrical use of the switch contacts under stated conditions. It does not, by itself, describe the door latch, establish how a dishwasher control circuit works, or prove that a switch is suitable for a particular appliance. A micro switch is an electromechanical component that can change an electrical contact state in response to movement of its actuator. In a particular design, a latch mechanism may move that actuator; the switch and latch are distinct parts of the arrangement.

The rating is meaningful only with its conditions attached. To compare it with an actual dishwasher circuit, first establish that the design uses a micro switch in the latch arrangement, then identify the load the contacts control and the documented circuit conditions. Compare those details with the rating’s stated voltage, current type, load, and test conditions. If the necessary information is missing or does not match, suitability remains unverified.

## What the dishwasher door latch micro switch contact rating describes

A micro switch contact rating is an electrical-use requirement for the contacts within the conditions stated for that rating. MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.1.1 and applicable specification sheets tie electrical rating values to stated load and test conditions. The number is not a universal switching capacity that can be separated from those conditions.

In a door-latch arrangement, the latch or its associated mechanism may provide the mechanical movement that operates a micro switch. The switch is the electromechanical component; the latch is the mechanism or assembly that may actuate it; and the dishwasher’s control circuit is the surrounding electrical system. A rating for the switch contacts does not rate the whole latch or dishwasher, and it does not explain what the appliance does after a contact changes state.

The first question is therefore not simply whether a rating number appears on a part or document. It is whether the rating belongs to the relevant switch and specifies conditions that can be compared with the actual circuit. Without that context, a number alone cannot establish application suitability.

A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement and is commonly used for position or limit detection. That general function does not establish that every dishwasher door latch contains one, or that any particular switch arrangement is present. The design must be established for the appliance in question before applying a component-rating comparison.

## Read the rating with its electrical conditions

Keep each stated electrical condition attached to the rating value. Voltage and current type matter: use only a rating explicitly stated for the applicable AC or DC condition. An AC rating does not establish a DC rating, and a DC rating does not establish an AC rating. A value for one condition cannot be transferred to another merely because the same contacts or switch body are involved.

The load type must also match. If a specification states a rating for a particular load, that value does not establish performance with a different load. Do not assume that a general-looking electrical number covers every kind of circuit. Likewise, do not treat a rating as applicable at an unstated voltage, current type, load, or test condition.

A useful reading keeps the full documented condition beside the value: the applicable current type, voltage, load, and test conditions. Check the applicable specification sheet for the rating’s scope. If a condition is absent, unclear, or does not correspond to the circuit under consideration, treat that part of the comparison as unresolved rather than filling the gap by inference.

This distinction matters even when a label appears to match one circuit parameter. A matching voltage, for example, does not establish that the current type, load, and test conditions also match. A rating is not a free-standing approval for every use below or near a printed value; its meaning remains bounded by what is actually specified.

## Compare the rating with the actual latch circuit

A meaningful comparison starts with the specific dishwasher design, not an assumption about dishwashers generally. Establish whether that design uses a micro switch in its door-latch arrangement. Door-latch designs and control arrangements vary, so the presence of a micro switch cannot be inferred from the fact that an appliance has a door latch.

Next, identify what the switch contacts actually control in that design. The latch mechanism may move the actuator, but the electrical effect of a contact change depends on the actual circuit and control arrangement. Do not infer from the latch alone whether a contact change permits operation, interrupts a circuit, or produces another response. Those details require design-specific evidence.

Then compare the documented circuit conditions with the complete stated rating conditions. Relevant details include the actual switched load and the applicable voltage, AC or DC current type, and rating test conditions. The comparison is meaningful only where the evidence identifies both sides: what the circuit presents to the contacts and what conditions the rating covers. If either side is missing, a numerical resemblance is not enough to establish a match.

This is an evidence check, not a conclusion drawn from the appliance name. A circuit may be associated with a latch without its switched load or electrical conditions being known. If documentation does not establish those details, the rating cannot be confidently applied to that circuit. The appropriate conclusion is that suitability has not been verified, not that the switch is necessarily unsuitable or suitable.

## What a contact rating cannot establish

A contact rating alone cannot establish that a particular switch is a compatible replacement. It does not identify the correct switch for a given dishwasher, prove that terminals are identified correctly, or establish that the installed arrangement operates as intended. Those are separate questions that require evidence specific to the component and appliance design.

Nor does the presence of a latch switch establish a universal door-state response. Whether a change in contact state affects starting, running, or another control function depends on the actual circuit and control design. Avoid treating a general description of a door latch as a wiring diagram or as proof of a particular interlock behavior.

The decision boundary is straightforward: compare only documented, matching electrical conditions. If the switch’s applicable rating, the circuit’s actual load, or the relevant design details are unknown, the available evidence does not establish suitability. Do not resolve that uncertainty by borrowing a rating from a different load or AC/DC condition, or by assuming that all dishwasher latch arrangements are alike. Unresolved electrical or safety questions should be left to qualified personnel rather than addressed through improvised tests.

In short, the dishwasher door latch micro switch contact rating is a conditional statement about contact use, not a blanket claim about the appliance. It can be compared with a particular latch circuit only after the switch’s role, the controlled load, and the circuit conditions are established and matched to the rating’s stated conditions. Where that information is absent, the conclusion remains limited: compatibility and suitability are unverified.
