## What NO and NC mean on a lawn mower seat interlock micro switch NO and NC terminals

Normally open (NO) and normally closed (NC) describe contact states relative to a specified reference condition for the switch actuator. At that reference condition, the NO contact is open and the NC contact is closed. When actuator movement changes the switch state, the contacts may change accordingly, but the labels alone do not specify the reference condition or establish how a particular switch is arranged. The switch documentation must define the applicable states and contacts.

“Normally” is not a universal synonym for “seat unoccupied,” “seat occupied,” or “mower off.” Those are equipment or user conditions, not necessarily the documented actuator reference state. A seat can move without directly moving the switch actuator in a predictable way; a lever or linkage may transmit movement, and different arrangements can use different mechanical states. Therefore, do not assign NO or NC to a seat position until the switch’s reference condition and the mechanism connecting the seat to the actuator are established for that equipment.

The terms also do not state the mower’s safety outcome. An open or closed contact is a component-level electrical condition. Whether that condition permits or interrupts a circuit function depends on the surrounding circuit and its design. A terminal marked NO is not, by itself, a promise that the mower starts when the seat is occupied; NC does not, by itself, promise that the mower stops when the seat is unoccupied. Those conclusions require equipment-specific information beyond the terminal labels.

Use the exact switch’s connection diagram and terminal markings to identify terminals. Do not infer terminal identity from a terminal’s physical location or from another switch that looks similar. The documented circuit function and permissible load also matter when interpreting how a switch is used. If the documentation for the exact switch or mower is unavailable or unclear, the contact meaning in that installation remains unresolved rather than safely inferable from a generic NO/NC description.

## Why terminal labels do not reveal the mower’s interlock response

NO and NC describe contact states at the switch, not the complete safety logic of the mower. A contact change may be an input to a wider circuit, and that circuit determines what follows. The labels alone do not establish whether a particular mower permits starting, stops an engine, affects blade operation, or produces an alarm under any seat condition. Do not infer any of those outcomes without the design-specific circuit information.

The switch’s role must also be distinguished from the surrounding equipment. A seat, linkage, switch actuator, electrical terminals, wiring, and control circuit are different parts of the system. A problem or behavior at one boundary does not automatically identify a condition at another. For example, knowing the isolated contact state does not establish whether the terminal connections in the mower are correct or what the complete interlock does. Similarly, knowing the mower’s apparent response does not, by itself, identify the switch’s contact state.

To interpret the full arrangement, consult the connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. A generic physical-layout inference is not a substitute. If documentation does not map the actuator state, contact terminals, and circuit role, leave that mapping unresolved. This avoids turning a common use of miniature switches for position detection into an unsupported claim about every mower or seat interlock.

The practical conclusion is limited but useful: establish the documented actuator reference state, identify the terminals from the exact switch information, and describe any verified contact state only for the conditions tested. Then use mower-specific circuit documentation to determine what that contact change means to the equipment. **Lawn mower seat interlock micro switch NO and NC terminals** do not, on their own, reveal whether the seat is occupied or what the mower will do; those conclusions require the relevant mechanical and electrical design information.
