A **leaf blower throttle micro switch continuity test** checks whether a documented contact path changes as the isolated switch actuator moves between defined states. It applies only to a leaf-blower throttle assembly that actually contains a micro switch; some throttle designs may not. The test can assess the switch contact path under the stated conditions, but it cannot establish that the throttle assembly, wiring, or leaf blower works correctly.

Before measuring, confirm that the equipment is shut down and that the switch is electrically isolated. Identify the relevant terminals and actuator states from documentation for the actual switch or equipment. Do not guess which terminals to probe or what reading a particular throttle position should produce. If isolation is unclear, stop and seek help from a qualified technician rather than improvising disassembly or testing.

## What this leaf blower throttle micro switch continuity test can establish

A micro switch is an electromechanical component that can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement. Micro switches are commonly used for position or limit detection, but that general use does not establish that a particular leaf blower has one in its throttle assembly. The assembly design must be confirmed first.

A continuity test is a limited check of a contact path. Its meaning depends on measuring the correct terminals while the switch is isolated and the actuator is in a defined state. A reading is not meaningful as a switch assessment if the terminals are guessed, the reference state is unclear, or other circuit paths remain connected.

Even a reading that matches the documented behavior establishes only the tested contact path in the tested state. It does not establish that the switch is correctly connected in the equipment, that the linkage moves as intended, or that the leaf blower’s control logic and other components operate properly. Likewise, an unexpected reading does not by itself identify a failed switch. It may indicate that the test conditions, terminal identification, or expected behavior need review, or that another issue requires diagnosis.

The procedure below therefore begins with confirmation and isolation, not with a probe placement or an assumed throttle position. It does not cover testing a switch while connected to powered equipment, diagnosing every no-start condition, or repairing or replacing a throttle assembly.

## Make the throttle micro switch safe to test

Shut down the equipment before beginning. The switch must be electrically isolated from the surrounding circuit before a continuity reading can be interpreted as a check of its own contact path. Do not use a meter’s continuity mode on an energized circuit, and do not treat a reading taken through connected equipment as a measurement of the isolated switch.

This article does not provide a leaf-blower-specific disconnection procedure. Use applicable equipment instructions to determine whether and how the switch can be isolated. If those instructions do not make the safe isolation method clear, do not improvise by opening or dismantling the throttle assembly. Leave the task to a qualified technician.

For electrical work in the United States workplace context, the cited rule generally requires exposed live parts to be deenergized before work. It also requires a qualified person to verify the deenergized condition with test equipment, and limits work on energized parts to qualified persons. Verify all applicable requirements. These general work-practice boundaries do not replace equipment-specific instructions or establish that a particular leaf blower has been made safe to test.

Do not proceed to continuity measurement unless the equipment is shut down and the switch is isolated under an understood, appropriate procedure. If those conditions cannot be established, the test has not reached a safe or interpretable starting point.

## Identify terminals and actuator states before measuring

Before connecting the meter, consult applicable documentation for the actual switch or equipment. Use it to identify the contact terminals intended for the test and the actuator reference states associated with expected readings. The correct terminal pair and expected state behavior depend on the switch design; neither can be inferred merely from the fact that the switch is in a throttle assembly.

Do not assume that the switch has a particular terminal label, contact arrangement, or normal state. Do not assume that a particular throttle position means the actuator is operated or released. Instead, use the documentation to determine which actuator state is being checked and how that state is defined. Record the state and contact pair before taking a reading so that each result has a clear reference.

If the documentation does not identify the terminals or expected behavior, a continuity reading cannot be interpreted reliably from this procedure alone. Guessing at terminals may test the wrong path; guessing at a throttle position may make a valid reading appear unexpected. Resolve those uncertainties using applicable documentation or qualified assistance rather than drawing a conclusion from an unreferenced measurement.

## Take and interpret the continuity readings

With the switch shut down, electrically isolated, and its test terminals and actuator states identified, place the continuity meter across the documented contact pair. Take a reading for each documented actuator state. Keep the state attached to the result: a reading without a record of the actuator position or condition cannot be compared meaningfully with the expected behavior.

Compare each result only with the behavior documented for that particular switch design and state. Do not apply an assumed universal pattern, infer a contact arrangement from a sound or feel, or transfer expectations from another switch. If the readings match the documented behavior under the specified conditions, the test supports only that the measured contact path behaved as expected in those tested states.

If a reading differs from the documented behavior, or changes inconsistently when the same defined state is checked, treat it as a reason for further diagnosis, not proof of a specific fault. First keep the boundary of the evidence clear: the observation concerns the isolated contact path as measured. It does not alone establish why the result occurred or whether the switch is the cause of an equipment symptom.

A click, if one is noticed, is not a substitute for the meter reading or the documented expected behavior. Mechanical movement alone does not establish electrical continuity. This procedure does not specify a meter setting beyond continuity mode, a numerical threshold, or a particular result pattern because none is established for the unspecified switch design.

## Separate a switch reading from a throttle or leaf-blower fault

An isolated contact reading does not verify the connector, wiring, linkage, control circuit, or overall operation of the throttle assembly or leaf blower. A plausible reading may show that the tested contact path behaved as documented in the measured actuator states, while leaving those other parts untested. It cannot prove that the equipment will start, run, or respond correctly.

A poor or changing reading also has more than one possible interpretation. The terminal pair or actuator state may have been identified incorrectly, the expected behavior may not apply to the actual design, or there may be a contact-path issue. The evidence in this procedure does not distinguish among these possibilities or justify a particular repair. Do not replace, adjust, or disassemble components based solely on an unexplained reading.

The useful conclusion is narrow: compare an isolated switch’s measured contact path at documented actuator states with the expected behavior for that design. Keep any system-level symptom separate from that result. If isolation, documentation, or interpretation remains uncertain, leave further electrical diagnosis to qualified personnel. A continuity test can inform a bounded check; it is not a complete diagnosis of a leaf blower.
