Straight lever micro switch adjustment should begin with the external mechanism, not with bending the switch lever. The aim is to make the external part actuate and release the switch within the limits documented for that design. A lever that clicks is not, by itself, proof that the contacts work or that the installed equipment operates correctly.

First distinguish the switch’s actuator from the cam, linkage, or other part that presses it. Inspect how those parts meet and move before changing their position. Then compare the movement with the switch’s documented operating and release points and any permitted travel limits. If those limits or the allowed adjustment method are unknown, do not guess or reshape the lever.

## What straight lever micro switch adjustment can and cannot change

Adjustment should address the external mechanism’s position or contact geometry, where the design permits it. A mounting position, linkage, or contacting part may be adjustable; that does not establish that the switch’s lever is intended to be bent or reshaped. The actuator is the part of the switch moved by the external mechanism. The contacting part is outside the switch, even when it presses directly on the actuator.

The practical boundary is the design documentation. Use it to identify permitted adjustment points, the intended contact location, and applicable operating or travel limits. Without that information, there is no supported universal lever angle, gap, contact point, travel target, or adjustment method. Do not substitute an improvised bend for a documented adjustment.

An external mechanism that appears misaligned is a reason to inspect the actuation path, not proof that the switch itself is defective. Conversely, apparent lever movement does not establish that the internal contacts change state correctly. Keep these questions separate: does the external mechanism move the actuator as intended, and does the isolated switch show the documented electrical behavior at the relevant actuator states?

## Inspect alignment and contact location before changing anything

Begin with the mounting and movement path. Check whether the switch appears securely mounted and whether the external cam, linkage, or contacting part reaches the lever at the location shown in design information, if available. Look for an approach that is consistent with the mechanism’s intended motion. A contact point or direction that differs from the documentation calls for checking the external setup; it does not justify forcing the lever into a new shape.

Observe the linkage through its movement, within the documented access and isolation procedure. Note whether it moves freely and approaches the actuator consistently, or whether its path shifts, binds, or misses the intended contact region. These observations can help distinguish a problem in the external movement path from a question about the switch’s internal electrical condition. They do not, on their own, establish the cause of a fault.

Do not loosen or reposition parts merely because a different position seems likely to produce a click. Use documented adjustment points and limits. If the mounting method, intended contact location, or safe way to inspect the mechanism is not known, stop until that information is available. Instructions for accessing or isolating the host equipment must come from the relevant design or equipment documentation; do not improvise an access procedure.

## Relate operating point, release point, and available travel

Operation and release are distinct events. The operating point is where the actuator reaches the position associated with operation; the release point is where it returns through the position associated with release. The separation between those events is movement differential. A mechanism that reaches the operating point has not necessarily demonstrated that it can also return and release as intended.

Travel before operation and travel after operation are also different. In the terminology used by MIL-PRF-8805K sections 6.5.14 and 6.5.16, pretravel runs from free position to operating position. Overtravel runs from operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. Overtravel is relevant only where the design permits that additional motion. These definitions do not supply a universal travel amount for a particular switch or installation.

Compare the external mechanism’s movement with the specific switch and installation documentation. Use documented operating and release points, travel limits, and mounting tolerances where they are provided. Do not infer an acceptable margin from a click, estimate a target distance from another switch, or continue the mechanism’s motion beyond a documented limit. If the available travel cannot be evaluated because the required values or limits are missing, the adjustment remains unresolved; guessing can exceed the design boundary.

## Verify mechanical movement separately from electrical state

A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism. It does not prove continuity, correct wiring, correct terminal identification, or suitability for the equipment’s load. Treat the sound or feel as one mechanical observation, not as an electrical test or a conclusion about the entire assembly.

If continuity testing is appropriate and can be performed within the documented safe procedure, test the switch while it is isolated from the circuit. Identify the terminal arrangement and expected contact states from documentation for that switch; do not assume terminal labels or states that have not been established. Check at a defined actuator state, such as the documented free, operated, or release condition, and compare the reading with the documented expected behavior for that state. An unspecified actuator position or undocumented terminal arrangement makes a reading difficult to interpret.

An isolated continuity result applies to the switch under the tested conditions. It does not establish that the installed wiring is correct, that the equipment circuit is safe, or that the host equipment functions as intended. If the switch passes an isolated check but the installed system does not behave as expected, the cause may lie elsewhere in the actuation path or equipment. Keep component verification and system diagnosis separate rather than treating one test as proof of both.

## When to stop rather than force or reshape the lever

Stop when the permitted adjustment method, terminal arrangement, or relevant travel limits are unknown. Stop as well if the mechanism appears to require force or deformation beyond documented instructions. There is no general basis here for recommending that a straight lever be bent, forced, or modified. Use the design-specific instructions, or leave the adjustment unresolved until those instructions are available.

Several fault paths remain possible: external alignment or mounting, movement of the linkage, the switch’s internal contact condition, terminal identification, wiring, or another problem in the equipment. A click addresses only a limited mechanical observation. A continuity check of an isolated switch addresses only its defined test condition. Neither result alone identifies every other fault path or proves the installed assembly safe or functional.

If the mechanism cannot reach and release the actuator within documented limits, or if the available evidence does not distinguish the possible causes, do not force a result. Work involving unsafe access, energized equipment, or unresolved electrical hazards belongs with qualified personnel. The decision boundary is straightforward: adjust only the external parts and by the method the design permits, verify only against documented states and limits, and stop when those boundaries cannot be established.
