NO and NC on range hood damper micro switch NO and NC terminals describe contact states at a defined reference condition. They do not, by themselves, say whether a damper is open or closed, or what the range hood will do. To interpret the labels, first identify the switch’s specified reference condition and actuator position, then consult the contact diagram and the equipment’s circuit information.

A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement. Some range hood damper designs may use one, with a damper, lever, or linkage moving the actuator. That possible mechanical relationship does not establish a universal damper position for either contact label. Keep four things distinct: damper position, actuator position, switch contact state, and the range hood’s response.

## What NO and NC mean on a range hood damper micro switch NO and NC terminals

NO means normally open, and NC means normally closed, but “normally” must be tied to the reference condition specified for the particular switch. The labels identify contact states under that condition. They are not unconditional promises about current flow in an installed circuit, nor do they independently describe the range hood’s operating mode.

A generic changeover contact may also have a common terminal, often identified as COM. In that arrangement, NO and NC name the two contact paths relative to the documented reference condition. However, not every switch should be assumed to have that arrangement. The exact device marking or schematic controls; a generic terminal pattern is not enough to establish terminal function.

Actuator position matters because the switch responds to mechanical movement. If an actuator moves from the specified reference condition, the contact state may change. Thus, an NO contact that is open at the documented reference condition may be in a different state after actuation, and an NC contact may likewise change state. The precise states and movement sequence must come from the specific switch documentation. Do not infer them solely from the words “open” and “closed.”

The reference condition is not automatically “damper closed,” “damper open,” “fan off,” or “hood off.” Those are positions or equipment states, not definitions of NO and NC. A range hood design could place a switch actuator against a damper or linkage in different ways, or use no micro switch in that role. The label alone cannot establish which position corresponds to the switch’s reference condition.

## How damper movement may actuate a micro switch

Where a range hood damper design uses a micro switch, damper movement may be transferred through a lever or linkage to the switch actuator. The actuator is the part moved by the external mechanism. If that mechanism moves the actuator through the switch’s operating motion, the switch can change its contact state. This describes a conditional mechanical path, not a claim about every hood or damper assembly.

The sequence is important: the damper or linkage moves; the mechanism may move the actuator; the switch may change its contact state; and the connected circuit may then respond according to its design. Each step needs its own evidence. Seeing a damper move does not establish that the actuator moved, and actuator movement alone does not establish which terminals changed state without the documented contact arrangement and reference condition.

The switch’s travel terminology can help describe motion when the applicable design documentation defines it. Pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These definitions do not supply travel values for a particular switch or establish how far a range hood damper moves. Do not use them to infer a damper position from an NO or NC marking.

A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, correct terminal identification, correct wiring, or suitable operation in the hood circuit. The mechanical event and the electrical result are related but distinct. Even if the switch contact changes as intended, the range hood’s circuit response remains a separate question.

## Why terminal labels do not identify the hood’s response

NO and NC describe contact states at the switch’s specified reference condition. They do not specify what the range hood’s control circuit does with those contacts. The effect depends on the actual wiring and control design, including which contact path is used and how the circuit interprets it. Without that information, the labels cannot establish whether the fan runs, stops, or signals a condition when the damper moves.

This boundary prevents several common assumptions. A damper that appears open does not necessarily place the actuator in a particular switch state. An actuator that has moved does not identify which contact path the equipment uses. And a contact change does not, by itself, prove that the connected control system received or acted on a signal. These are separate observations, not interchangeable descriptions of one event.

The contact arrangement also matters. A generic changeover contact may provide common, NO, and NC terminals, but the exact switch might have a different marking or arrangement. Even where COM, NO, and NC are documented, a terminal’s name should not be treated as a complete wiring instruction or as a statement about the hood’s response. Use the exact switch diagram and the equipment’s circuit information rather than inferring function from physical terminal layout.

Accordingly, do not conclude from NO or NC alone that a particular damper position enables the fan, interrupts operation, or causes an alarm. Those outcomes are not inherent in the terminal labels. They require evidence about the actual switch, mechanical actuation, wiring, and control logic. If any of those details are unavailable, the system behavior remains unresolved.

## What information is needed to identify the terminals in a specific design

Start with the exact switch’s terminal markings or connection diagram and the reference condition it specifies. Confirm whether the documentation actually identifies a common, NO, and NC arrangement, and note the actuator condition associated with the stated contact states. Do not substitute a generic layout or a presumed damper position for this information.

Then consult the range hood equipment documentation for the circuit function and permissible load associated with the switch. The switch diagram identifies contact relationships; the equipment information establishes how those contacts are used in that design. Together, these documents may clarify the intended relationship among actuator movement, contact change, and circuit response. If they do not specify that relationship, do not infer it from the labels alone.

An isolated switch check and an installed-circuit conclusion are different things. A continuity result, if obtained under a defined actuator state and suitable test conditions, concerns the isolated switch contact being checked. It does not establish that the hood wiring is correct or that the assembled equipment responds as intended. Likewise, a click is not an electrical verification. Neither observation proves the installed range hood circuit is safe or functioning correctly.

For work involving identification or testing, follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. Use the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment rather than relying on generic physical-layout inference. Electrical work beyond a supported procedure belongs with qualified personnel. The bounded conclusion is simple: range hood damper micro switch NO and NC terminals name contact states at a documented reference condition; relating those states to a damper position or hood response requires the specific switch arrangement, actuator condition, and wiring information.
