The **elevator landing door travel limit micro switch operating force** describes two different kinds of switch parameter: position-related travel and the force applied at the switch actuator. A travel limit identifies a position boundary; it does not, by itself, say how much force is needed to operate the switch. These terms matter only for a landing-door design that actually uses a micro switch. They do not describe every elevator landing door or establish how a particular door system behaves.

The distinction is also important because force may be discussed at different places in a mechanism. Force at a switch actuator is not automatically the same as force applied to a door, lever, or linkage. The relationship depends on the actual arrangement, and the available evidence does not establish a conversion between those measurements. Likewise, switch position parameters do not determine the door’s travel endpoints or the control circuit’s response.

## What does elevator landing door travel limit micro switch operating force describe?

The phrase brings together two separate questions. **Travel** concerns actuator position or movement. **Operating force** concerns force associated with actuating the switch. Neither parameter substitutes for the other: knowing a position limit does not provide an operating-force value, and knowing an operating force does not specify a travel boundary.

The terminology in MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.5 distinguishes actuating force, releasing force, free position, operating position, movement differential, pretravel, and overtravel. Those are distinct characteristics, not alternate names for one general “limit.” The specification’s terminology identifies categories of switch behavior, but the supplied information gives no numerical force or travel values for a particular switch or elevator-door installation.

The scope is conditional. A landing-door mechanism may use a micro switch, but the evidence does not establish that every landing door does. Where one is used, the switch is a component within a larger mechanical and electrical arrangement. Its force and travel characteristics alone do not identify the door’s mechanical endpoints, the switch’s contact state in a given condition, or what the surrounding control system does.

A “travel limit” also needs a defined reference. It could refer in ordinary discussion to a door’s movement boundary or to a position associated with the switch actuator, but those are not automatically the same point. The accepted terminology defines switch positions and travel characteristics; it does not specify a landing door’s endpoint or how that endpoint corresponds to a switch position. Without an identified reference and arrangement, the phrase should not be treated as a complete specification.

## Where does operating force act in a landing-door mechanism?

To interpret force, trace where it is applied. In a possible arrangement, landing-door movement may be transmitted through an actuator or linkage to the micro switch’s actuator. The force at the switch actuator is the component-level quantity to distinguish from force applied at the door or elsewhere in the linkage. A stated switch operating-force characteristic should not be relabeled as the force a person or mechanism applies to the door.

The distinction matters because the door, linkage, and switch actuator occupy different points in the mechanical path. The supplied claims do not document a particular landing-door arrangement, linkage geometry, measurement location outside the switch, or a relationship that converts force at the door into force at the switch. It is therefore not possible to infer a numerical door force from a switch force, or the reverse, from these terms alone. Actuating force and releasing force are separately identified in the cited terminology. They should not be collapsed into a single operating-force figure. ## How do travel limits, operating point, and release point differ?

Switch travel terms describe actuator movement and positions, not a door’s overall travel range. The cited specification terminology distinguishes free position, operating position, and movement differential, along with pretravel and overtravel. These terms mark different aspects of the switch’s operating behavior. They do not, without design-specific evidence, define where a landing door begins or ends its movement.

**Operating position** is the switch position associated with operation in the cited terminology. **Release position** is a separate event: the position at which the switch releases as the actuator returns. The supplied claims do not provide a formal release-position definition or numerical position values, so the exact behavior and location in a particular switch remain unknown. The two events must not be treated as one point merely because both occur during actuator motion.

**Pretravel** and **overtravel** are also distinct. The source identifies both as separate force-and-travel terms but does not supply a pretravel definition. It does define overtravel in MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.5.14 as the distance or angle between the operating position and the extreme position to which the actuator may be moved. Thus, overtravel is measured beyond the operating position, within the actuator’s permitted movement; it is not a door-travel limit or a force rating.

The cited terminology also distinguishes **movement differential**, but the evidence pack does not provide its definition or a value. It would be unsupported to give a precise formula or to assign it a particular relationship to operating and release positions from this material alone. The safe interpretation is limited: it is a separately named switch characteristic, and any exact meaning or value must come from applicable documentation.

These distinctions matter when a door position is described as a “limit.” A mechanical endpoint of the door is not automatically the switch’s operating position, release position, or extreme actuator position. A linkage could place those events at particular points, but no arrangement is documented here. Without that information, do not equate a door endpoint with a switch travel term or assume how much actuator movement occurs before or after switch operation.
