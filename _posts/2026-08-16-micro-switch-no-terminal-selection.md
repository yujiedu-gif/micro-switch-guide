A micro switch NO terminal selection should begin with the switch’s documented contact state, not with a guess based on where a terminal sits or what color its wire is. “Normally open” describes a contact relationship at a specified reference condition. It does not, by itself, say whether current flows in an installed circuit. Identify the terminal from the exact switch’s markings or documentation, then use an isolated continuity check only if the contact arrangement and expected behavior are documented. That check can confirm an observation about the switch in a defined state; it cannot establish that the installed wiring is correct, that the switch suits a load, or that the surrounding system is safe.

## What NO means in micro switch NO terminal selection

NO, or normally open, is a contact-state designation. Its meaning depends on the reference condition stated in the specific switch’s documentation. Read that condition before interpreting the label. Do not substitute an assumed resting position, a particular actuator position, or the switch’s state in an installed appliance for the documented reference condition.

At that reference condition, an NO contact is documented as open. “Open” describes the contact relationship inside the switch; it is not a universal statement about current flow in the surrounding circuit. The effect of a contact state on a circuit depends on how the switch is connected and on the circuit itself. Terminal identification therefore answers which documented contact is NO, not what an appliance or control system will do when that contact changes state.

A generic changeover contact may use labels such as common, normally closed, and normally open. That general naming pattern is not proof that a particular switch has those terminals or that its labels appear in a particular arrangement. The exact device marking or schematic controls. If the documentation does not specify a reference condition or contact arrangement, do not fill in the missing information from a familiar switch shape or a general convention. Treat the terminal identity as unresolved until the exact documentation clarifies it.

Keep the terms tied to what they establish. A documented NO label identifies a contact state under a specified reference condition. It does not establish a voltage or current rating, load compatibility, wiring correctness, or an application’s operating logic. Those are separate questions and require their own applicable documentation.

## Find the NO terminal from the specific switch documentation

Start with the exact switch’s markings and documentation. Look for a terminal designation or schematic that identifies the NO contact and states the condition used to define it. Match the documentation to the device being examined; do not assume that another switch with a similar housing, actuator, or appearance uses the same terminal arrangement.

If documentation shows a common terminal and an NO terminal, use those documented labels to identify the contact pair. A generic changeover arrangement may include COM, NO, and NC, but that pattern should not be treated as a universal layout. In particular, do not identify NO by physical position, terminal order, wire color, or the position of a nearby terminal. Those clues do not establish the contact identity without device-specific documentation.

A schematic can convey the contact relationship, but interpret it according to the reference condition shown or described. If the drawing does not make that condition clear, the NO designation cannot safely be translated into an assumed actuator state. Likewise, if the switch has markings that conflict with available documentation, or the documentation cannot be matched to the specific device, stop short of assigning a terminal based on inference.

The practical decision is documentation-led: identify the terminal only when the exact device’s marking or schematic supports that identification. If those records are missing, incomplete, or ambiguous, the terminal remains unknown. Do not use a continuity check to invent an undocumented pinout; at most, a check can compare an observed contact state with an expected behavior that is already documented.

## Verify the contact state on an isolated switch

A continuity check can provide a bounded observation of the switch, provided the switch is isolated and the exact terminal arrangement and expected behavior are documented. First identify the relevant terminals from that documentation. Then use the documented reference condition to determine the actuator state in which the NO contact is expected to be open. Do not assume that an unactuated position is the reference condition unless the documentation says so.

With the switch isolated, check continuity across the documented contact pair in that specified state. Compare the result with the documented behavior. For an NO contact defined as open at that reference condition, the expected observation is no continuity across that pair in that condition. If the documentation also specifies how the contact changes when the actuator moves, a second observation may be compared with that stated behavior. Do not assume a particular state change or actuator position where the documentation does not provide it.

Keep the test conclusion narrow. The observation concerns the isolated switch, the identified terminals, the tested actuator state, and the contact behavior described by the documentation. A result that differs from the documented expectation is a discrepancy to investigate, not by itself a complete diagnosis of an appliance or circuit. A result that agrees with the expectation confirms only that measured observation under the stated conditions.

Do not treat an isolated continuity reading as evidence that installed wiring is safe. The cited United States workplace rule generally requires exposed live parts to be deenergized before work, requires a qualified person to verify the deenergized condition with test equipment, and limits work on energized parts to qualified persons. Verify all applicable requirements. The isolated-switch check described here does not replace those work practices or establish that an installed system is deenergized.

## What terminal identification does not establish

Knowing which terminal is documented as NO answers a limited identification question. It does not show that the switch is wired correctly in an appliance, that the circuit uses the contact as intended, or that a particular state will trigger a specific system response. Those conclusions depend on the surrounding circuit and its documentation, which are outside terminal identification alone.

Nor does a NO label establish suitability for a load or application. Electrical rating, load requirements, and other installation constraints must be assessed from applicable documentation. Do not infer them from the contact label, a continuity result, or the presence of a compatible-looking terminal arrangement. If the relevant rating or application information is unavailable, suitability remains unresolved.

The decision boundary is straightforward: use exact switch markings or documentation to identify NO; use an isolated continuity check only to compare a documented contact expectation in a documented actuator state; and keep the result separate from judgments about installed wiring, load suitability, or system safety. If the reference condition, terminal arrangement, or expected behavior is not documented, do not claim that a terminal has been verified as NO. A micro switch NO terminal selection is complete only as a terminal identification when the exact device evidence supports it, not as approval of the connected circuit.
