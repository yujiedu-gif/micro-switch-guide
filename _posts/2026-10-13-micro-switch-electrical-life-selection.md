Electrical endurance evidence can help compare micro switches, but only when its test conditions correspond to the intended use. Micro switch electrical life selection therefore starts with the documented load and operating conditions, not with a cycle count treated as a universal promise. A stated electrical operation count describes performance within a defined test scope; it does not, by itself, predict how long a switch or the equipment containing it will operate in service.

The key is to keep the evidence attached to its conditions. Distinguish electrical endurance from mechanical life, identify the load and test sequence behind any electrical-life claim, and note where the intended application differs or remains unspecified. If relevant conditions are missing or cannot be aligned, the figure may not support a direct selection comparison.

## What electrical life means in micro switch electrical life selection

Electrical life is an endurance measure for a switch operating under specified electrical conditions. A published electrical operation count is meaningful only alongside the load, operating sequence, and test conditions associated with that count. It is not a free-standing service-life value that can be applied to any circuit or use.

Mechanical life is a separate measure. A mechanical-life result does not establish how long the switch will endure electrical operation under a particular load. Conversely, an electrical-life result does not establish mechanical endurance outside the test conditions used to obtain it. Keep the two measures distinct when reviewing evidence; one cannot be substituted for the other to fill a gap.

Neither measure alone guarantees the service life of an installed switch, host equipment, or complete assembly. The switch endurance result describes a test scope. The operating life of a real assembly is a different question, and the evidence here does not establish a conversion from a test count to a field interval or equipment replacement schedule.

For a selection review, first identify what the stated life measure actually represents. If the evidence identifies electrical endurance, retain its load and test qualifiers. If it reports mechanical life instead, do not treat that as proof of electrical endurance. If the measure or its conditions are unclear, mark the comparison as unresolved rather than supplying an assumed interpretation.

## Check whether the electrical-life conditions match the intended load

Begin with the evidence attached to the electrical-life figure. Identify the documented load type, electrical conditions, operating sequence, and other stated test conditions. Then compare those details with the conditions relevant to the intended application. A useful comparison depends on knowing what was tested as well as what is being considered.

Do not transfer a result from one load type to another unless the evidence explicitly supports that comparison. Likewise, do not assume that a result established for AC applies to DC, or that a DC result applies to AC. If the available claim does not specify AC or DC, that distinction remains unknown; it should not be filled in by inference.

A practical evidence check is:

1. Record the stated electrical-life measure and its operation count, if one is provided.
2. Record the load type and electrical conditions attached to it, including any stated AC/DC distinction.
3. Note the operating sequence and other test conditions that accompany the result.
4. Set those documented conditions beside the known conditions of the intended application.
5. Mark each condition as aligned, different, or unavailable in the evidence.

This sequence is an evidence comparison, not a prediction of field life. If an application condition is not known, the evidence cannot establish a match for that condition. If the test uses a different load or operating sequence, do not silently treat the result as equivalent. If the source leaves a relevant condition unstated, preserve that uncertainty rather than assuming a typical value.

A figure with missing or mismatched conditions is insufficient for a direct application comparison. It may still describe the result of its stated test, but the limits of that result must remain visible when considering selection.

## Compare evidence without turning a life figure into a guarantee

Compare electrical-life figures only when the relevant conditions and measurement basis are sufficiently aligned in the available evidence. A larger count is not, by itself, evidence of longer service in a different application. Before interpreting two figures against each other, check whether each is tied to a documented load, operating sequence, cycle count, and environmental or other test conditions, and whether those conditions permit a meaningful comparison.

Record unresolved differences instead of assuming they do not matter. For example, if the evidence provides a load for one result but not the other, or describes different operating sequences without explaining their comparability, the figures do not establish a like-for-like comparison. The same boundary applies when relevant test conditions are absent. Do not bridge such gaps with an assumed equivalence or an unsupported adjustment.

Endurance requirements and results are bounded by their test scope. A durability result applies only with its specified electrical load, operating sequence, cycle count, and environmental or test conditions. Keep those qualifiers next to the result in notes or a comparison table; separating the number from its conditions can make the evidence appear broader than it is.

An endurance count is not a promised field life. It also does not define a replacement interval for the host equipment or assembly. The result cannot establish how long the complete system will operate, because the evidence concerns the stated switch endurance conditions, not a guarantee for every installed use. Do not present an operation count as a calendar duration or whole-equipment service-life estimate unless separate, relevant evidence supports that conclusion.

The disciplined conclusion may be that one result is relevant within its documented scope, while a direct comparison remains unresolved. That is more accurate than ranking figures whose conditions do not align. Preserve both the evidence and its limits so the next decision does not rely on an unsupported reading of the count.

## Selection decision when electrical-life evidence is incomplete

Use electrical-life evidence as relevant to selection only to the extent that the documented conditions match the intended application. When the load, operating sequence, and applicable test conditions are stated and sufficiently aligned, the result can inform a bounded comparison within that scope. It still does not guarantee field life or the service life of the surrounding equipment.

If a key condition is absent or differs, do not infer suitability from the figure alone. Do not calculate a service-life estimate from an endurance count when the evidence does not establish a basis for that calculation. In particular, a missing load detail or an unresolved AC/DC distinction prevents a direct conclusion about that condition. A mechanical-life result also cannot fill a gap in electrical-life evidence.

To resolve an incomplete comparison, identify the missing evidence explicitly: the load type and electrical conditions, the operating sequence, the stated test conditions, and enough information to determine whether the compared results use a sufficiently aligned measurement basis. If those details are unavailable, the selection conclusion should remain limited. This boundary does not mean the switch will fail or succeed in the application; it means the supplied endurance evidence does not establish either conclusion.

Keep the switch result separate from the expected life of the host equipment or assembly. Electrical endurance may inform one part of a selection review, but it is not a whole-system service-life claim. The practical decision is therefore conditional: use the result only within its documented scope, and treat a comparison with missing or mismatched conditions as unresolved. That is the appropriate limit of micro switch electrical life selection when the available evidence does not match the intended use.
