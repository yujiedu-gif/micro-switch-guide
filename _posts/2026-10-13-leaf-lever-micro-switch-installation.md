For a leaf lever micro switch installation, position the switch so the external mechanism moves its leaf lever as intended, then verify mechanical action separately from contact state, wiring, and host-equipment behavior. The leaf lever is the switch’s actuator; a cam, tab, door, or linkage that presses it is a distinct part. The available evidence does not establish universal mounting dimensions, allowable deflection, or a particular contact arrangement, so those details must come from documentation for the actual switch and installation.

A useful check follows the mechanism in stages: establish the relationship between the external part and the lever, inspect mounting and movement, distinguish actuation from release, and verify contacts only with the switch isolated and its contact arrangement known. A click alone does not prove continuity or correct operation of the surrounding equipment. Electrical isolation and any work beyond an appropriate supported procedure require qualified handling.

## Leaf lever micro switch installation: establish the mechanical relationship

Identify the three relevant parts before positioning anything: the micro switch body, its leaf lever actuator, and the external mechanism that moves the lever. The external part may be a cam, tab, door, or linkage, but the presence of any one of these does not establish how the host equipment is meant to respond. The switch detects mechanical movement through its actuator; the surrounding circuit and control logic determine what the resulting contact change means.

Trace the intended motion from the external part to the leaf lever. Check which surface makes contact, where that contact occurs during the mechanism’s movement, and whether movement is transmitted to the lever rather than to another part of the switch body. The evidence does not specify a universal contact location or required approach direction. Use the applicable switch and equipment documentation to establish those details rather than inferring them from the shape of the lever.

The mechanical relationship should allow the external part to move the lever through the intended sequence without treating a particular host-system outcome as inherent. For example, a contact change does not by itself establish that a door is locked, a machine is stopped, or an alarm is active. Those behaviors depend on the actual circuit and design. If the required movement or control effect is unclear, installation checks alone cannot resolve that uncertainty; consult the documentation for the assembly.

## Mount and align the switch without forcing the leaf lever

Use the mounting features and orientation documented for the specific switch and installation. Do not assume that micro switches share hole spacing, a universal orientation, or a common alignment dimension. Position the switch body so the external mechanism can reach the lever along the intended path, while keeping the actuator distinct from the mounting arrangement. No allowable lever force, clearance value, or mounting tolerance is established here.

With the arrangement accessible and safe to move, inspect the mechanism across its intended movement. Check whether the external part reaches the lever, whether the contact is maintained where the design calls for it, and whether the mechanism can move away again. Look for interference between the lever and nearby parts, but do not infer a permitted clearance or deflection from appearance alone. If the assembly’s intended movement or limits are not documented, do not resolve that gap by pushing the lever farther.

The mounting check concerns position and mechanical interaction, not terminal identification or wiring. A switch can appear well aligned while its terminals are misidentified or the host circuit is connected incorrectly. Conversely, a wiring symptom does not prove that the switch is mechanically misaligned. Keep these checks separate so a result in one area is not used as proof about another.

## Check actuation, release, and permitted travel

Observe the lever’s movement as a sequence rather than treating all motion as one event. Pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position. Operation is the event associated with reaching that operating position; release is a separate event as the mechanism moves back. The operating and release events should not be collapsed into a single position or inferred solely from the lever’s visible deflection.

Overtravel is the distance or angle between the operating position and the extreme position to which the actuator may be moved. It describes movement after operation, not the movement before the operating position. A design may permit movement beyond the operating position, but the existence of that definition does not establish an allowable amount for an unidentified switch. Do not push or adjust the lever on the assumption that a particular overtravel is available. Use applicable documentation for the actual design’s permitted movement.

During a mechanical check, note whether the external mechanism produces the intended lever movement and whether the lever can return as intended when the mechanism withdraws. A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, contact condition, terminal wiring, or host-system response. If actuation or release appears absent, that observation does not identify the cause by itself: the external mechanism may not be reaching the lever, the switch may not be operating, or another part of the assembly may be involved. Further conclusions require separate checks.

## Verify the isolated micro switch in defined actuator states

A continuity check is bounded to the switch under test. First establish the applicable contact arrangement and reference condition from documentation for the actual switch. Then check the isolated switch in defined actuator states, such as the documented free state and a specified actuated state, and compare the observed contact behavior with that documented arrangement. The evidence here does not establish which contacts should be open or closed for an unspecified switch, so do not assume that result from the presence of a leaf lever.

Use COM, NO, and NC only when the switch’s contact arrangement is documented. COM means common; NO and NC describe contact states at a specified reference condition. Those labels do not supply an unqualified promise about current flow in an installed circuit. If the reference state, terminal markings, or expected contact behavior is not established, the continuity reading cannot be interpreted reliably from the information available here.

Keep the switch isolated for this check and record which actuator state was tested. A reading in one state is evidence about that isolated switch state only. It does not establish that the switch is correctly mounted, that the external mechanism reaches the operating point, that the terminals are correctly connected, or that the host equipment is safe or functional. Do not use an isolated reading as a substitute for evaluating the installed circuit. Electrical isolation and work outside the supported procedure require appropriate qualified handling.

## Separate switch results from wiring and host-system behavior

Interpret the findings by category. Mechanical input concerns whether the external mechanism moves the lever as intended. Contact verification concerns the isolated switch in a defined actuator state. Terminal identification concerns whether the documented terminals have been recognized correctly. Wiring concerns the connections in the host circuit. Host-system behavior concerns the response of the surrounding equipment. A result in one category does not settle the others.

If the equipment does not respond as expected, avoid concluding immediately that the leaf lever switch is defective or that its installation is the sole cause. A mechanical input problem, an internal contact problem, uncertain terminal identification, a wiring problem, or another equipment issue can produce different symptoms. Distinguish them only with the relevant documentation and separate checks. The evidence does not specify a universal circuit, load, or response for equipment using a micro switch.

The installation check is complete only to the extent supported by the observations: the parts are identified, their mechanical relationship is inspected, permitted movement is established from applicable documentation, and any isolated contact check is interpreted in its documented states. It cannot prove overall wiring correctness, load suitability, or the host equipment’s control behavior. If those questions remain, they need assessment beyond the mechanical positioning and isolated switch check described here. A leaf lever micro switch installation should therefore be judged in separate mechanical, contact, and system-level steps, without treating one as proof of the others.
