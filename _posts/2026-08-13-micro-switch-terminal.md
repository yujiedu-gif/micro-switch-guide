A **micro switch terminal** should be identified from the markings and documentation for the particular switch, not from an assumed universal layout. A continuity check can provide evidence about an isolated switch in a defined actuator state, but it is not a diagnosis of the wiring or the wider equipment. The exact switch documentation also governs which contact arrangement is present and what its labels mean.

The key distinction is between identifying a terminal, checking a contact, and determining what an installed circuit does. Those are related questions, but evidence for one does not automatically answer the others. If markings, documentation, or test conditions are missing, treat the terminal function or result as unresolved rather than inferring it from physical position, a click, or the equipment’s apparent behavior.

## What a micro switch terminal label can, and cannot, tell you

Start with the connection diagram and terminal markings for the exact switch. Use its documentation to interpret them. Do not assume that every micro switch has the same number of terminals, the same physical arrangement, or the same labels. A generic diagram or a terminal’s location relative to the actuator is not enough to establish its electrical identity when the specific device information is unavailable.

The documented terminal identity is also distinct from the terminal’s role in the surrounding circuit. A marking can identify a contact connection on the switch, but it does not, by itself, show how the equipment uses that connection or what will happen when the contact changes state. Circuit function must be established from the relevant equipment documentation, not inferred from the switch’s physical form.

For a particular switch, consult the documentation that applies to that device and compare it with the markings actually present. If they are absent, illegible, or do not match the available diagram, the identification remains uncertain. Do not resolve that uncertainty by assigning a familiar label based only on where a terminal sits or how the switch looks.

This boundary matters because a terminal label is not a complete wiring instruction. The connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment all matter to applicability. A generic layout cannot establish those conditions, and terminal identification alone does not establish that a proposed connection is appropriate.

## Micro switch terminal names and contact states

Some micro switches use a documented changeover contact arrangement labeled common, normally closed, and normally open. In that arrangement, COM means common, while NO and NC describe contact states at the specified reference condition. These names apply only when the particular switch’s marking or schematic documents that arrangement; they are not a promise that every micro switch has all three terminals.

Read NO and NC with the reference condition stated in the applicable documentation. The labels describe contact state relative to that condition, not an unqualified promise about current flow in every installed circuit. If the reference condition or actuator state is not specified, do not silently supply one. Likewise, do not treat the actuator’s movement or the switch’s position in equipment as proof of which state the documentation defines as the reference.

A contact-state description also does not identify the equipment’s response. What a contact change means to an appliance or control circuit depends on that circuit and its design. A terminal label alone cannot establish whether the host equipment starts, stops, signals, or remains unaffected. Those outcomes are outside what the contact name itself proves.

If the exact switch documentation uses different labels or shows a different arrangement, follow that information rather than translating it into COM, NO, and NC by assumption. If the diagram and physical markings appear inconsistent, pause at the identification question: the available evidence has not established which terminal is which. Do not use a guessed contact arrangement as the basis for a wiring conclusion.

## How to verify a micro switch terminal with an isolated check

A continuity check is a separate way to examine an isolated switch. Its result is meaningful only in relation to the specific switch, the actuator state being checked, the measuring method, and the expected behavior stated in the applicable documentation. A general terminal diagram does not supply a complete test procedure. When the documentation does not establish those details, consult it rather than improvising a procedure or assuming an expected reading.

Keep the check’s conclusion narrow. A reading under documented test conditions can provide evidence about the isolated switch’s contact behavior in that state. It does not independently identify undocumented terminals, establish how the switch is wired into equipment, or show what the complete circuit will do. If the test state or expected behavior is unclear, the reading cannot resolve the uncertainty by itself.

Terminal identification and continuity checking answer different questions. Markings or a schematic identify the documented connections. A continuity check evaluates contact behavior under its stated conditions. Neither should be substituted for the other: a reading without an established terminal reference may be hard to interpret, while a diagram alone does not establish the observed contact condition of a particular isolated switch.

Do not extend an isolated-switch check to an installed-circuit diagnosis. The wiring and circuit function are separate from the switch’s isolated contact behavior, and the available evidence here does not provide a procedure for diagnosing them. Any work beyond the applicable switch documentation and safe, qualified scope remains unresolved by this check.

## What a terminal check does not establish

A click may indicate mechanical movement, but it does not prove continuity, correct terminal identification, correct wiring, or suitability for a load. Hearing or feeling a click therefore cannot replace documentation or a properly bounded electrical check. Nor does the absence or presence of an apparent change establish what the wider equipment is doing.

An isolated reading also does not establish that the switch is correctly connected in the installed circuit. It cannot, on its own, show that the circuit uses the intended contact, that the equipment responds as expected, or that the whole assembly is safe. Those conclusions require evidence about the actual circuit and equipment, beyond the isolated switch result.

For applicability, rely on the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. If any of these remain unknown, do not infer compatibility or load suitability from the terminal names or a continuity result. Follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements; this discussion does not supply an installed-circuit procedure.

In short, identify terminals from the exact switch’s markings and documentation, interpret contact labels only at their specified reference condition, and treat any isolated continuity result as evidence limited to its documented test conditions. If those details are unavailable, the conclusion remains uncertain. A **micro switch terminal** check does not, by itself, establish correct wiring, load suitability, or whole-equipment safety.
