A visible bend does not, by itself, show whether a micro switch lever is damaged or suitable for a mechanism. For micro switch lever deformation selection, compare the lever’s actual geometry and motion with the documentation for the specific switch and the mechanism that will actuate it. A lever may have an intentionally shaped form, or its shape may have changed through damage, mounting, or wear; appearance alone does not distinguish these possibilities.

The relevant question is whether the documented lever and actuator interface can operate within the specified motion limits and produce the required switching behavior under the applicable conditions. If those limits or conditions are unavailable, do not substitute an assumed bend threshold or a visual judgment for them.

## What micro switch lever deformation selection can and cannot establish

A bend visible in a lever is an observation, not a diagnosis. It does not establish that the lever was deformed, that the switch will operate incorrectly, or that the part is compatible with a particular mechanism. Some lever shapes are part of the design. To determine whether a particular shape is intended, compare the switch with its design-specific documentation rather than with an unrelated switch or a general picture.

If the documentation does not resolve whether the observed geometry is intended, the uncertainty remains. Damage, mounting, or wear may be relevant possibilities, but none can be confirmed from the visible shape alone. Avoid treating a slight bend as automatically acceptable or a pronounced bend as automatically defective without applicable limits and evidence about function.

Selection requires more than identifying the lever’s outline. The specific design’s dimensions or shape, the location where the host mechanism contacts it, permitted actuator motion, and operating and release behavior all matter. Applicable ratings and operating conditions also need to be checked where they are relevant to the intended use. If a required value or condition is not documented, it is unknown; this article does not supply a universal deformation allowance.

The decision boundary is therefore evidence-based: proceed only when the documented design and the actual interface can be compared, and unresolved geometry or limits call for further documentation or qualified assessment. A visual match alone is not proof of compatibility.

## Compare lever geometry with the mechanism that will actuate it

Assess the lever as an interface between the switch actuator and the external mechanism. Check the documented lever shape and dimensions, if provided, against the host mechanism’s actual approach, contact location, and motion. The mechanism must contact and move the actuator as intended for that particular design. A lever that appears similar may meet the mechanism at a different location or move differently, so appearance alone cannot establish an equivalent interface.

Keep the component distinct from the equipment around it. A micro switch may be actuated by a mechanism, but what happens electrically depends on the specific switch and circuit. The presence of a lever does not establish a contact arrangement, normal state, or control outcome. Those details must come from applicable documentation for the switch and the actual circuit, not from the lever’s visible shape.

Do not infer interchangeability from another switch’s lever or from an arrangement used in another application. The evidence here provides no universal dimensions, bend tolerance, or compatibility rule. If the mechanism’s contact point or direction of motion cannot be compared with the documented interface, the fit remains unresolved rather than proven by a near match.

## Verify motion limits and switching behavior for the specific design

Where applicable documentation is available, use it to assess actuator motion rather than relying on appearance or a click. MIL-PRF-8805K defines pretravel as the movement from free position to operating position, and overtravel as the movement from operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position (sections 6.5.14 and 6.5.16). These definitions identify distinct portions of travel; they do not provide a universal allowable lever bend or a limit for every switch.

Operating and release behavior are also separate points to check against the specific design’s documentation. Under MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.5.17, release force is the force or torque to which force on the actuator must be reduced to permit the contacts to return to the unoperated position after operation. This definition does not establish a value for an undocumented switch. Use documented values and conditions where available, and do not assume that values for one design apply to another.

Observed lever movement or an audible click may indicate movement, but neither establishes contact continuity or suitability for the intended load. Switching behavior must be considered with the applicable switch documentation and circuit conditions. If operating or release behavior, permitted travel, or relevant ratings and conditions are unknown, do not fill those gaps with an assumed value based on the lever’s shape.

## Separate lever deformation from other causes of a mismatch

A poor fit or unexpected operation can have more than one cause. The lever’s designed geometry or possible deformation is one question; mounting alignment and the point where the host mechanism makes contact are separate questions. Wiring and internal contact condition are also distinct from mechanical fit. A visible bend cannot identify which, if any, of these explains a mismatch.

Check the available evidence in the context of each possibility. Compare the actual contact location and mechanism motion with documented interface and travel information. Consider wiring and contact behavior using the applicable circuit and switch documentation. Do not conclude that a lever is defective merely because the installed equipment behaves unexpectedly, or that the mechanism is correctly aligned merely because the lever moves.

A test of an isolated switch has a limited scope: it concerns that switch in a defined actuator state. It cannot, by itself, establish that the installed mechanism, wiring, circuit, or equipment is correct. Likewise, a movement observation does not establish electrical continuity. Keep these conclusions separate so that one observation is not used to diagnose a different part of the assembly.

## When the available evidence is insufficient for selection

Do not select a switch on the basis of an assumed allowable bend, an undocumented substitute, or a visual resemblance. First seek the design-specific information needed to compare the lever’s geometry, contact position, permitted motion, operating and release behavior, and applicable ratings or conditions with the actual mechanism and intended use.

If those details are missing, inconsistent, or cannot be matched to the installation, the selection remains unresolved. Documentation or qualified assessment is the appropriate boundary; an invented deformation threshold cannot settle it. The same applies when the visible form might be either intentional geometry or a change caused by damage, mounting, or wear and there is no evidence to distinguish them.

In short, micro switch lever deformation selection is a comparison of documented design and actual interface, followed by checks of motion and switching behavior. A visible bend can prompt that comparison, but it cannot replace it or prove damage, function, or compatibility.
