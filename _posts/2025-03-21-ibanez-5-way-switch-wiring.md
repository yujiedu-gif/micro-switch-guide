## Separate switch positions, terminals, and contact states

A **switch position** is the selected mechanical setting. A **terminal** is an electrical connection point on the switch. A **contact state** describes whether a particular pair of terminals is electrically connected or not connected in a particular position. These are separate pieces of information: a position is not a terminal, and a terminal’s location does not by itself reveal its contact state or circuit function.

A multi-position switch can connect different terminal combinations in different positions. To describe what is observed, identify the position and the terminal pair being checked, then record whether continuity is observed between that pair under the test conditions. Repeat the record for relevant positions and pairs rather than assuming that one observation describes every position.

Use neutral identifiers for terminals when their markings are unknown, such as “A” and “B” in a written observation. These labels are only a way to track the points being tested; they do not assign a function, polarity, input, output, or instrument connection. If the switch has markings, record them accurately, but do not infer that a marking means a particular circuit role without documentation for the exact switch.

A continuity record is also not the same thing as a wiring diagram. It describes observed contact behavior; it does not say which external circuit should attach to each terminal, what load is permissible, or whether the switch is suitable for a particular application. Those conclusions require supporting documentation. This distinction helps prevent a physical sketch or a limited set of observations from being mistaken for a verified connection map.

## Verify the diagram against the exact switch and instrument

First check what the documentation actually identifies. It should identify the exact switch variant and the relevant instrument configuration, rather than relying only on a broad description. Then compare the terminal labels and documented contact states with the observed record. A mismatch, missing identification, or unclear correspondence leaves applicability unverified.

Keep the evidence categories distinct. The switch’s connection diagram and terminal markings can identify documented connections; its circuit function and permissible load define relevant limits. The equipment documentation must also correspond to the configuration being considered. A visual resemblance between two switches, or agreement in one observed position, does not establish that they are the same variant or interchangeable.

If the documentation does not resolve a discrepancy, leave the connection question open rather than filling gaps from physical layout or convention. Do not assume that terminals in matching locations have matching functions, or that a diagram for one configuration applies to another. The appropriate conclusion may simply be that the available evidence is insufficient to verify the diagram.

In short, determine applicability by matching exact identifying information and documented contact behavior, not by guessing from the search phrase or appearance. The phrase “ibanez 5 way switch wiring” is a starting point for locating a question, not evidence of a terminal map.
