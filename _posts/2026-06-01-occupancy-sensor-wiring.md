# Reading an Occupancy Sensor Wiring Diagram

An **occupancy sensor wiring** diagram can help explain how a documented device and circuit are intended to connect, but it is not a universal wiring template. Terminal labels, circuit relationships, permissible loads, and configurations depend on the exact sensor and equipment. A diagram’s physical appearance alone cannot establish what a terminal does or whether a connected load is suitable.

This guide explains how to read a device-specific diagram and identify information that must be verified before wiring work. It does not provide terminal assignments, wire-color rules, or installation instructions. Use the documentation for the exact device and equipment, and do not infer missing details from a generic drawing. If identification is uncertain or work presents a hazard, consult a qualified electrician and follow applicable requirements.

## What occupancy sensor wiring diagrams can and cannot tell you

A wiring diagram documents connections for a particular device, equipment, or configuration. Its applicability depends on what the documentation actually covers. A drawing for one sensor should not be treated as the diagram for another sensor merely because their cases, labels, or apparent terminal arrangements look alike. Nor does a generic diagram establish how a particular sensor connects to a particular circuit or load.

The diagram and terminal markings must be read together. A line or symbol in a drawing can communicate a documented circuit relationship, but the drawing alone does not establish that an unlabeled physical terminal has a particular function. Do not translate a position shown in a schematic into a physical terminal assignment unless the exact-device documentation makes that correspondence clear. Do not infer a connection from a terminal’s location, a conductor’s color, or another device’s diagram.

This distinction matters because a diagram is evidence only within its stated scope. It may describe a device under a particular configuration or equipment arrangement. If the documentation does not identify the relevant configuration, terminal, circuit relationship, or load condition, the missing detail remains unresolved. Do not fill the gap with a common-looking convention or an assumption about how occupancy sensors are generally wired.

No universal terminal assignment or wire-color rule is asserted here. A diagram can guide interpretation, but it cannot by itself establish that a proposed connection is permitted for an exact sensor, circuit, and load. The controlling reference is the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. Where those sources do not provide enough information, stop short of treating a generic explanation as connection directions.

## occupancy sensor wiring: What to verify in the device diagram

First, match the documentation to the exact device and to any configuration or variant the documentation identifies. Check that the diagram actually applies to the equipment being considered. Similar appearance or a familiar layout does not prove that a drawing applies. If the exact device or relevant configuration cannot be confirmed, its terminal identity and connection requirements remain unresolved.

Next, read the diagram for the supply, switched-load, and control relationships that it explicitly documents. Use the drawing’s labels and the device’s terminal markings together. Do not assign a function to an unlabeled connection, infer an unshown relationship, or convert a schematic position into a physical terminal location without documentation. The goal is to understand what the exact documentation states, not to complete a drawing using assumptions.

Then check the documented circuit conditions and permissible load. Do not assume that a load is compatible based only on the fact that the device is an occupancy sensor, or because another sensor appears to have a similar diagram. The documentation must support the relevant device, circuit, and load relationship. If it does not state the necessary information, compatibility has not been established by the generic diagram.

Keep the evidence boundary clear: a diagram may show a connection relationship, while other device or equipment documentation may be needed to identify the exact applicable ratings or conditions. Use those sources together rather than relying on terminal appearance. If the diagram, markings, and documentation do not agree or do not resolve the intended configuration, do not guess which source or connection applies. Seek clarification from a qualified electrician or another appropriate source for the exact equipment.

## Questions to resolve before wiring work

Before any wiring work, identify the circuit and connected load from reliable documentation. Do not identify either solely from conductor color or physical position. A wire’s appearance does not establish its circuit function, and a terminal’s location does not establish its electrical role. If the circuit or load cannot be identified reliably, that uncertainty needs to be resolved before proceeding.

Verify the exact device instructions, relevant electrical ratings, and the requirements that apply to the work and location. The device documentation must support the connection and permissible load for the equipment in question. Applicable local requirements must also be considered. A general diagram-reading explanation cannot establish those device-specific or location-specific facts.

In the United States workplace context covered by the cited rule, exposed live parts generally must be deenergized before work. A qualified person must verify the deenergized condition with test equipment, and work on energized parts is limited to qualified persons. This is a boundary on work practice, not a substitute for exact-device documentation, circuit identification, or applicable requirements. Verify all requirements that apply to the situation.

Do not proceed on an uncertain identification or an unresolved compatibility question. If the information is missing, inconsistent, or difficult to interpret, seek help from a qualified electrician. This article does not prescribe a wiring procedure; it identifies the documentation and context that must be clear before anyone relies on a diagram for wiring work.

## When a generic wiring explanation is not enough

A generic explanation cannot establish the terminal identity of a particular device, the configuration covered by its diagram, or whether its documented permissible load matches a particular circuit and connected load. Those are exact-equipment questions. A familiar drawing or physical layout cannot resolve them unless the exact-device documentation supports that interpretation.

For an unresolved question, return to the exact device and equipment documentation and the applicable requirements. If those sources do not establish the needed connection or suitability, ask a qualified electrician rather than extrapolating from a general diagram. This is especially important when circuit identity, terminal function, or load compatibility is uncertain.

The boundary here is deliberate: this guide addresses how to interpret a documented diagram and what to verify, not how to wire a specific sensor, install or replace one, or troubleshoot a particular load. It also does not identify a model, assign terminals, or provide a conductor-color convention. **occupancy sensor wiring** should be treated as device- and circuit-specific; without the exact supporting information, a generic explanation cannot justify a connection.
