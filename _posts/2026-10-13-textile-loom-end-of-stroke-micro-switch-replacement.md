A textile loom end of stroke micro switch replacement is justified only when evidence points to the switch itself, not merely because the loom fails to stop or respond at the expected point. This diagnostic applies only to loom designs that actually use a micro switch at the end-of-stroke mechanism. The same symptom could arise because loom motion does not reach the switch actuator, because the switch contact does not change as documented, or because of a terminal, wiring, or control-circuit problem.

Trace the mechanical input first, then assess the switch separately from the installed circuit. An isolated continuity check can provide evidence about contact behavior in a defined actuator state, but it does not establish that the wiring, controls, or machine as a whole are functioning safely.

## textile loom end of stroke micro switch replacement

At the end-of-stroke position, the diagnostic question is whether the switch fails to change contact state when it is correctly actuated, or whether the cause lies elsewhere. The loom’s actual arrangement matters: not every textile loom uses a micro switch for this function, and the presence of one does not by itself establish what the control system will do when its contacts change.

The causal chain to examine is loom movement, mechanical input to the switch actuator, contact behavior, and the host control response. A fault or interruption at any point can produce a symptom at the end of stroke. For example, a symptom may prompt an inspection of the input path, but it does not prove that the switch has failed. Nor does a switch contact change alone establish that the loom will stop, since that response depends on the actual equipment and circuit.

Keep five possible fault areas distinct:

- **Mechanical input:** Loom motion may not reach or move the switch actuator as intended.
- **Internal contact behavior:** The contact may not behave as the applicable documentation specifies when the actuator is in a defined state.
- **Terminal identification:** The terminals used for a check may not be the documented terminals for the intended contact function.
- **Wiring and connections:** An issue outside the isolated switch may interrupt or alter the installed circuit.
- **Downstream control:** The control circuit may not respond as expected even if the switch contact changes.

These are diagnostic categories, not conclusions about a particular loom. Use the loom and switch documentation to identify the intended mechanical input, contact behavior, terminals, and system response. Do not infer them from a generic switch layout or from the symptom alone.

## Trace the end-of-stroke input before blaming the micro switch

Start at the moving part of the loom and follow the mechanical path toward the switch. Depending on the documented arrangement, loom motion may act through a cam, lever, linkage, or another mechanism. Do not assume which one is present. The relevant question is whether the movement that should trigger the end-of-stroke function reaches the switch actuator and moves it as intended.

Compare the observed motion with the applicable service information. If the input path does not move the actuator as intended, the switch may not be receiving the required mechanical input. That is different from evidence that the switch contact fails under correct actuation. The input mechanism and the switch are separate parts of the diagnostic chain, even when they work together during normal operation.

The definition of overtravel can help distinguish an actuator position from the permitted further movement of an actuator, but it does not establish the required travel for a particular loom or switch. In MIL-PRF-8805K 6.5.14, overtravel is “the distance or angle between the operating position and the extreme position to which the actuator may be moved.” Use that definition only as a general term. Do not use it to infer a loom’s end-of-stroke setting, a switch’s required movement, or whether an observed actuator position is acceptable without applicable documentation.

A click is not a sufficient diagnosis. It may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but does not prove that the contact changed, that the correct terminals were tested, or that the installed circuit responded. Conversely, a symptom at the loom’s end-of-stroke point does not establish that the mechanical input is correct. Keep observations about loom motion and actuator movement separate from electrical conclusions about the switch.

## Check the micro switch separately from loom wiring and controls

An electrical check should follow an appropriate isolation and test procedure supported by the applicable service information. Before interpreting a result, identify the documented terminals and the actuator state being tested. The expected contact behavior must come from documentation for the exact switch and equipment; do not assume that a particular terminal arrangement, COM, NO, or NC contact is present. Leave hazardous or undocumented work to qualified personnel.

For an isolated continuity check, record what was tested: the switch separated from the installed circuit as required by the applicable procedure, the terminals identified from documentation, and the defined actuator state. Compare the result with the documented behavior for that state. A result that does not match may be evidence of a switch contact problem, provided the isolation, terminal identification, test method, and actuator state are correct. It is not a substitute for confirming those conditions.

A continuity reading describes the isolated switch under the conditions tested. It does not diagnose installed wiring, connections, control logic, or the loom’s overall safety. If the switch behaves as documented in the tested states, that finding does not rule out a fault elsewhere in the equipment. If the result is unexpected but the terminals or state are uncertain, the diagnosis remains unresolved rather than proving switch failure.

Terminal identification and permissible load are also specific to the documented switch and equipment. Use the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment rather than inferring them from physical layout. Do not extend a switch-level check into a conclusion about how the loom is wired or what its controls should do.

## Decide whether replacement is supported by the evidence

Replacement is supported when the mechanical input is adequate and a properly isolated, state-defined check shows that the documented switch behavior is not met. The conclusion should rest on both parts of that evidence: correct actuation and an unexpected switch-level result under the documented test conditions. A stopping or end-of-stroke symptom by itself is not enough.

If the switch behaves as documented during the isolated check, do not treat replacement as established. The remaining diagnosis may involve terminal identification, connections, installed wiring, or downstream control. Investigate those areas separately under appropriate service procedures, using the documentation for the loom and switch. Do not infer a specific control response from the presence of a micro switch.

If the mechanical input cannot be confirmed, or the applicable terminal information, actuator state, isolation procedure, or expected contact behavior is unavailable, the evidence does not yet support a definite replacement decision. Resolve those uncertainties through appropriate equipment documentation or qualified service rather than guessing at a terminal, circuit, or test condition.

The practical decision boundary is narrow: replace only when documented switch-level evidence points to the switch after adequate mechanical actuation has been established. A failed input path points away from an internal contact diagnosis; a switch that passes its documented isolated check leaves the installed circuit and controls to be assessed separately. This approach supports a textile loom end of stroke micro switch replacement decision without claiming compatibility, ratings, fit, or a machine-level diagnosis that the available evidence does not establish.
