When a design includes one, the painting robot guard interlock micro switch location is best identified by tracing how guard movement reaches the switch actuator, rather than by assuming a standard mounting point. Finding the component is only a physical identification task. The guard, actuator or linkage, switch, wiring, and host control logic are separate parts of the arrangement. Seeing a switch or observing actuator movement does not prove its electrical state, wiring, or effect on the robot’s control system. ## Painting robot guard interlock micro switch location: trace the actuation path

First establish from the robot or guarding documentation whether the particular design uses a micro switch. A guard interlock may use another arrangement, so the equipment name is not enough to identify the component or its location. If documentation is unavailable or unclear, treat the presence and identity of a micro switch as unresolved rather than assuming one is installed.

When a micro switch is documented, trace the mechanical path from the moving part of the guard toward the switch actuator. Depending on the documented arrangement, movement may be conveyed by a guard edge, hinge, cam, or linkage. Follow only the parts that apply to that design. Look for the point where the mechanical movement reaches an actuator associated with a distinct switch body.

This is a way to search, not a claim about a universal mounting point. The switch could be mounted separately from the visible guard panel, with a bracket or linkage connecting the two. Conversely, the visible hardware near the guard may not be the switch. Do not conclude that the component must be on a particular side, hinge, or panel based on the general function of an interlock.

Compare what you can see with the equipment documentation, which may identify the component or inspection location. If the documented path cannot be matched to visible parts, or the component is obscured, do not infer its location from appearance alone. The documentation and the actual mechanical arrangement must agree before treating a visible part as the identified switch.

## Tell the micro switch apart from the guard and its actuator

The guard is the movable barrier; it is not the micro switch. The actuator is the part moved by the external mechanism. A lever, plunger, or other moving part may be associated with a switch design, but the available evidence does not identify which actuator form a particular painting robot uses. Identify the switch body separately from the part that contacts or moves its actuator.

A bracket, hinge, cam, or linkage may transmit guard movement, but such hardware is a mechanical interface, not the switch itself. It can help explain how the guard movement reaches the switch actuator without being part of the electrical contact mechanism. Do not label the whole guard assembly, or every nearby piece of hardware, as the micro switch.

Visual identification can be difficult when parts are enclosed or when several components occupy the same area. In that case, use the robot and guarding documentation to confirm which item is designated as the switch and how it relates to the guard. Do not assign an identity, contact arrangement, or electrical role to an ambiguous component based only on its position or shape.

Keeping these boundaries clear also makes the inspection more useful: the guard moves, a mechanical interface may carry that movement, the switch actuator receives it, and wiring connects the switch to the host system. This describes distinct roles, not a confirmed configuration for every robot. The actual design and control logic remain specific to the equipment.

## What location and movement can, and cannot, verify

Finding a documented switch and observing its mechanical relationship to the guard can support a physical location identification. It does not establish that the contacts changed state, that the wiring is correct, or that the host control system responds as intended. Those are separate verification questions, and the component’s visible position cannot answer them by itself.

An audible click is not proof of electrical continuity, correct wiring, or suitability for a safety function. It may indicate movement of a mechanism, but the sound alone does not establish the electrical state. Likewise, seeing the actuator move does not establish the state of the switch contacts or the behavior of the robot’s control system.

Do not infer a particular contact arrangement or control response from the fact that a micro switch is present. The result of a contact change depends on the design and circuit. Without equipment-specific evidence, the expected state and the robot’s response remain unknown. Location verification must not be described as proof that the guard interlock works or that the robot is safe to operate.

Keep the questions separate: Is the identified component physically the documented switch? Does the documented mechanical path reach its actuator? What electrical state and control response are specified for this design? The first two concern identification and physical arrangement; the last requires the applicable equipment procedure and qualified verification. A visual inspection or click cannot substitute for that process.
