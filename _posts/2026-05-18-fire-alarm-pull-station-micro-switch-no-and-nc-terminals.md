The **fire alarm pull station micro switch NO and NC terminals** describe contact relationships at a specified reference condition. They do not, by themselves, tell you whether a pull station will signal an alarm, produce a supervisory indication, or behave in any other particular way. Those outcomes depend on the pull station’s design and installed circuit, so terminal labels should not be read as a complete description of system behavior.

A micro switch is an electromechanical component, not another name for the pull station or its control circuit. Some pull-station designs use a micro switch; others may differ. To interpret the markings, identify the switch’s documented reference condition and contact diagram, then keep the component-level contact description separate from the host equipment’s function.

## What do fire alarm pull station micro switch NO and NC terminals mean?

NO and NC identify contact relationships at the documented reference condition for the particular switch. “Normally” is not a universal statement about the position of a pull station, the state of a fire alarm system, or an unqualified condition that can be assumed without documentation. The reference condition must be established for the switch in question.

At that stated condition, NO identifies a contact relationship designated normally open, and NC identifies one designated normally closed. These terms describe the contact state at the reference condition. They do not establish which contact will change during a particular pull-station operation, how the device is mechanically actuated, or what an attached panel will interpret. Those details require the specific switch and equipment documentation.

COM means common when the documented contact arrangement includes a common terminal. Do not assume that a particular switch has a COM terminal, or infer which physical terminal is NO, NC, or COM from terminal position alone. A marking or diagram must support the identification. If the actual arrangement is not established, leave it unresolved rather than assigning terminal names by appearance.

This distinction matters because a contact label is not an alarm instruction. NO does not inherently mean “alarm,” and NC does not inherently mean “normal system operation.” Either interpretation would go beyond what the labels establish. A terminal designation describes a switch contact under a defined condition; system meaning belongs to the documented circuit and equipment design.

For example, a reader might see NO and NC beside terminals and want to infer what happens when a pull station is operated. The labels alone cannot answer that. First establish the actuator reference condition and the documented contact relationship. Then consult the pull station’s documentation for how its mechanism and circuit use that contact. Without both pieces, the result of the operation remains unknown.

## How the micro switch relates to the pull station

The micro switch is a component within a larger device when that pull-station design uses one. The pull station is the host equipment; its mechanical parts, electrical connections, and surrounding circuit determine how the component is used. A contact-state description for the switch should not be expanded into a claim about the behavior of the complete device.

An external mechanism may move the micro switch’s actuator. If the mechanism changes the actuator state, the switch’s contacts may change according to its documented operation. But the existence of that possible mechanical relationship does not identify the pull station’s particular mechanism or establish the resulting circuit response. Designs can differ, and the supplied terminal labels do not specify how a particular device is arranged.

The contact change’s effect depends on the actual circuit and design. A pull station may be connected to equipment that interprets a contact state, but NO and NC markings alone do not reveal which terminals are used, how the circuit is configured, or what indication follows. Do not infer alarm, supervisory, or other behavior from a terminal label in isolation.

This boundary prevents two different questions from being conflated:

- **What contact relationship does the micro switch identify at its reference condition?** The switch diagram and markings can answer this when they are documented.
- **What does the pull station or connected system do with that contact?** The specific equipment documentation and circuit determine that; the labels alone do not.

Some pull-station designs use a micro switch, while others may use a different arrangement. Therefore, a description of one design’s internal relationship cannot be generalized to every pull station. Even where a micro switch is present, its terminal names do not establish the host device’s operating sequence or system-level result.

Keep any interpretation tied to the exact equipment documentation. If it does not identify the switch’s reference condition, contact diagram, or role in the pull station, the missing detail should remain unknown. Do not fill it in using a generic assumption about how pull stations are designed or how fire alarm circuits respond.

## What a terminal check can and cannot establish

A continuity observation is meaningful only when it concerns an isolated switch and a defined actuator state. The isolation condition and state must be known, and the expected contact relationship must come from the applicable switch documentation. An observed reading under those conditions is limited to the switch and state that were checked.

That observation does not verify the pull station’s installed wiring, the connected panel’s interpretation, or the operation of the complete system. It also does not establish that a terminal was identified correctly if the terminal diagram or markings were uncertain. A click or apparent mechanical movement, by itself, would not resolve those electrical or system-level questions.

If the expected state is undocumented, a continuity result cannot be interpreted reliably by assuming that NO or NC corresponds to a particular pull-station position. The reference condition is essential. Likewise, an isolated contact observation should not be treated as proof that the installed circuit is correct or that the pull station will produce a particular indication.

Electrical work has a defined safety boundary. Under the cited United States workplace rule, exposed live parts generally must be deenergized before work; a qualified person must verify the deenergized condition with test equipment; and work on energized parts is limited to qualified persons. Verify all applicable requirements. This boundary does not provide a procedure for testing or servicing a complete fire alarm system.

For an unclear terminal identity, reference condition, or expected contact relationship, use the applicable device documentation or seek qualified service. Do not turn an isolated-switch observation into wiring directions, a system test, or a conclusion about alarm performance. Those are separate questions that require the specific equipment and circuit information.

## What to confirm before interpreting the markings

Start with the documentation for the exact switch and equipment. Confirm the switch’s connection diagram, terminal identification, and specified reference condition. The diagram and markings, rather than a generic physical-layout inference, are the basis for identifying terminals. If they do not establish the contact arrangement, do not assume that COM, NO, or NC is present or located where expected.

Then keep the component and host-device questions separate. The switch documentation can describe contact relationships at a stated condition. The pull-station documentation must establish how its mechanism and circuit use the switch, if one is present. System interpretation remains tied to the specific equipment and circuit, not to the NO or NC label alone.

If checking continuity is relevant, the conclusion must stay within the documented, isolated switch and defined actuator state. If terminal identity or expected state is uncertain, the check cannot settle that uncertainty. If the question concerns installed wiring, panel interpretation, or system operation, an isolated reading is not a substitute for the applicable equipment documentation or qualified service.

The decision boundary is straightforward: interpret NO and NC as documented contact relationships at a stated reference condition. Do not treat those labels as a universal description of pull-station position or alarm behavior. For a **fire alarm pull station micro switch NO and NC terminals**, the markings alone do not establish what the complete device or system will do.
