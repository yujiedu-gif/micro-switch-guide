# Microswitch Tact: Understanding the Difference

The phrase **microswitch tact** may be used when someone is trying to distinguish a microswitch from a tact switch. The names point to different switch categories, but neither name alone establishes a component’s electrical suitability, physical fit, or compatibility with equipment. The practical distinction starts with how the switch operates, then moves to its documented actuator, contacts, mounting, and ratings.

A microswitch is generally understood as a switch with an actuating mechanism that changes contact state. Many designs use snap action, but devices described as microswitches are not all identical. A tact switch is generally a momentary push-operated switch intended to provide a tactile response. These broad descriptions help with identification, but the exact construction and behavior must be confirmed for the component in question.

## What does “microswitch tact” mean?

Here, “microswitch tact” is treated as a comparison between a microswitch and a tact switch, not as the name of a single switch type. The categories are useful starting points, not complete specifications. Labels may describe a general mechanism or intended form, but they do not by themselves establish a switch’s contact arrangement, terminal function, rating, or suitability for a particular circuit.

A microswitch is commonly associated with an actuator that operates the contacts when moved. A tact switch is commonly associated with a button that is pressed to make a momentary change and released to return. These descriptions are general: a category label cannot tell you every detail of how a specific part behaves.

When identifying a part, look for its stated switch type and operating action in the component documentation. Then check the contact description, actuator information, and drawing. If the documentation does not resolve a detail, do not infer it from the category name or appearance alone.

## Microswitch tact: how the operating mechanisms differ

A key distinction is the operating mechanism. In a snap-action mechanism, contact speed is relatively independent of the speed of the actuating mechanism. This definition describes the contact action, not a guarantee that every switch called a microswitch has identical operation or feel. The cited standard defines snap action in MIL-PRF-8805K, section 6.5.21.

A tact switch generally operates momentarily when pressed and returns when released. That description should not be treated as a universal specification for every device sold or described under that category. Confirm the operating action and contact behavior in the exact component’s documentation.

The terms “microswitch” and “tact switch” also do not, by themselves, specify whether contacts are normally open, normally closed, or arranged in another way. Nor do they establish whether a switch is suitable for a particular electrical load. Identify contact states and permitted use from the relevant documentation rather than relying on a category name.

## How do feel, travel, and installation commonly differ?

A tact switch is often recognized by a small button and a tactile response during a press. A microswitch may have a lever, plunger, or another actuator form. These are common cues, not fixed rules: actuator shape, operating force, feedback, and overall travel depend on construction. Physical feel alone is not a reliable way to determine a switch’s electrical behavior or exact operating point.

Travel terminology can make documentation easier to compare. Under MIL-PRF-8805K sections 6.5.14 and 6.5.16, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These terms describe actuator movement; they do not establish dimensions or fit for a particular part.

Mounting methods and dimensions also vary. Compare the exact component drawing with the available space and the equipment’s mechanical requirements. Do not assume that two switches fit or can replace one another because they look similar or share a broad category label.

## Where might each switch type be used?

As general examples, a tact switch may serve as a momentary input on a control interface, while a microswitch may be used where movement of an actuator is intended to change a circuit state. These examples describe possible functions only. They do not establish that a particular switch can handle a stated voltage, current, load, operating environment, or mechanical duty.

The task matters more than the label. A control input may call for a particular momentary action and tactile feedback. Another mechanism may need a specific actuator form or contact action. In either case, the exact component’s documented behavior must match the task. An example of where a switch type might be used is not evidence of compatibility with any particular equipment.

## How can you identify the switch type from its documentation?

Use a repeatable check rather than appearance alone:

1. **Find the stated category and operating action.** Look for whether the documentation identifies the device as a microswitch or tact switch, and whether it describes snap action, momentary operation, or another behavior.
2. **Check actuator and travel terms.** Review the actuator description and any stated free, operating, and extreme permitted positions. Use the drawing to understand physical movement and mounting.
3. **Read the contact description.** Confirm the documented contact function and state references. Do not infer terminal roles from physical position or a generic diagram.
4. **Review ratings in context.** Rating values are specification-sheet requirements tied to stated load and test conditions, not universal switching capacities. MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.1.1 and the applicable specification sheets are relevant to this distinction.
5. **Verify terminals and circuit function.** Use the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. Do not assume that a generic layout identifies a terminal or its role.

If documentation is incomplete, leave the uncertain characteristic unresolved. A broad category label or visual resemblance does not fill in missing electrical or mechanical information.

## What should you verify before choosing between them?

Start by describing the required action: should the switch change state momentarily when pressed, respond to a moving actuator, or meet another documented behavior? Then compare the exact component’s operating action, actuator, contact description, travel information, mounting drawing, and electrical ratings with the requirements of the equipment.

Check voltage, current, load type, mechanical fit, environmental conditions, and actuation method against documentation for the exact switch and application. Ratings depend on their stated load and test conditions, so a value cannot be treated as a universal capacity. Terminal functions and permissible connections likewise require the exact switch and equipment documentation. Follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements when examining or working with a circuit.

For a replacement, treat compatibility as an exact-part and application-specific question. Similar names, button shapes, or actuator styles do not prove that two components share dimensions, contact functions, ratings, or suitability. The most dependable distinction in a **microswitch tact** comparison is therefore the documented operating mechanism, checked alongside the component’s physical and electrical requirements.
