A motorized valve may use a micro switch to indicate home position, but only when that design includes the switch and a mechanism that actuates it. Motorized valve home position micro switch wiring is therefore specific to the valve’s mechanical arrangement and documented circuit, not something that can be inferred from the switch’s appearance or the word “home.” The switch provides a contact change; the surrounding circuit determines what that change means to the control system.

To interpret the wiring, trace the documented path from valve movement to the switch actuator, from the actuator to the contact change, and from the contact to the receiving circuit. Use the equipment-specific diagram to identify terminals and reference states. Without that documentation, the switch’s contact labels do not establish whether the valve is at home, whether a controller is energized, or what action the circuit takes.

## What motorized valve home position micro switch wiring describes

The phrase describes a wiring arrangement in which a micro switch contact is used as part of a home-position indication path. It applies only to designs that include such a switch. It does not describe every motorized valve, and it does not by itself identify a valve position, terminal, or control response.

There are three distinct parts to keep in view. The valve moves through its mechanical travel. An actuator or linkage, if provided in the design, transfers relevant movement to the micro switch’s actuator. The switch then changes its contact condition, and a receiving circuit may use that condition as an input. The switch is a component in this chain, not the valve controller and not the whole position-indication system.

The direct answer is that home indication depends on the particular mechanism and wiring diagram. A diagram may document how a contact change relates to the valve’s home indication, but the phrase “home position” alone does not tell you which contact is used or what its state will be. Nor does the presence of a micro switch establish how the controller responds. Those details must come from documentation for the actual valve and circuit.

This distinction matters because a mechanically actuated contact and a controller’s interpretation of that contact are separate stages. The switch can change state when moved by its mechanism; the receiving circuit determines what that signal represents or does. Do not treat an assumed valve position or controller action as a property of the switch itself.

## How valve movement changes the micro switch contact

In an arrangement that uses a micro switch for home indication, the causal path begins with valve movement. A mechanical part associated with that design moves the switch actuator; actuator movement can then change the switch contact condition. The receiving circuit may interpret the resulting signal, but its interpretation depends on the circuit design. This describes the relationship, not a universal valve mechanism or a guaranteed controller response.

The exact arrangement is not established by the general term “motorized valve.” The available information does not identify where a switch is mounted, what part moves its actuator, or which point in valve travel operates it. Those details must remain open unless the equipment documentation supplies them. Do not assume a particular mechanical connection or operating point from the fact that a valve has a home position.

Actuator travel terminology can help when a specification documents it. Under the cited standard’s terminology, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These terms describe actuator motion. They do not, by themselves, identify the valve’s home position or show how the receiving circuit interprets a contact change.

A contact change also does not establish whether the valve is open or closed, whether the motor is energized, or whether the controller considers the valve to have reached home. Those relationships require design-specific evidence. The signal’s meaning belongs to the documented mechanism and circuit together, not to the micro switch in isolation.

## Read the wiring diagram before identifying terminals

Use the wiring diagram for the exact switch and equipment to identify the documented terminals, circuit function, and permissible load. Do not infer terminal identity from physical layout, wire color, terminal position, or a generic diagram. Where a diagram documents COM, NO, and NC, treat those as terminal labels tied to the diagram’s stated reference condition. The labels alone do not tell you which state corresponds to valve home.

In particular, do not equate NO or NC with “home,” “open,” “closed,” “energized,” or a guaranteed current-flow condition unless the equipment documentation makes that connection. Contact labels describe a documented contact arrangement and reference state; the valve’s mechanical position and the control circuit’s logic are separate questions. A diagram or specification must connect those details before the terminal state can be interpreted as a home-position signal.

Keep the switch-terminal path distinct from the controller input and logic. The diagram may show how the switch is connected to a receiving circuit, but the effect of the signal depends on that circuit. A contact change alone does not establish whether the controller records an indication, permits another action, or responds in some other way. Do not infer such behavior from the switch terminal labels.

For any wiring interpretation, rely on the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. If those sources do not resolve a terminal or reference state, the correct conclusion is that it remains unknown, not that a familiar-looking arrangement must apply. Questions about the controller’s input or logic also require the relevant equipment documentation; switch documentation alone may not establish them.

## What a contact check can and cannot establish

A contact check is meaningful only when its test condition is defined. The available claims do not specify an isolation procedure, measuring method, or actuator state for a continuity check on this valve arrangement. For that reason, no particular continuity test or expected reading can be prescribed here. The applicable specification and test procedure must provide the method, connection points, and limit for a contact-resistance measurement; do not substitute an assumed value or an unspecified meter reading.

Even a documented measurement has a bounded result. An isolated switch reading concerns the switch under the stated test conditions. It does not establish the safety of an installed circuit, confirm the controller’s interpretation, or prove that the full valve assembly reaches or indicates home correctly. Mechanical actuation, switch contacts, wiring, and receiving-circuit behavior are distinct parts of the path.

Electrical work also has a defined boundary. Under the cited United States workplace rule, exposed live parts generally must be deenergized before work; a qualified person must verify the deenergized condition with test equipment; and work on energized parts is limited to qualified persons. Applicable requirements must be verified for the work context. This does not provide a valve-specific test procedure or authorize an improvised check.
