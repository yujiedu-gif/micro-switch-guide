Actuator travel is the movement of the micro switch’s actuator, not the total movement of a dishwasher door, latch, or linkage. Dishwasher door latch micro switch actuator travel therefore cannot be determined from latch movement alone: the specific mechanical arrangement must show how motion reaches the switch and where the actuator moves. The two motions may be related, but their relationship is not established without design-specific evidence.

## What dishwasher door latch micro switch actuator travel describes

A micro switch actuator is the part moved by an external mechanism. In a dishwasher door-latch arrangement, the latch or a connected part may provide that input, but actuator travel describes movement at the switch itself. It does not name the door’s full movement, the latch’s movement, or the travel of every part in the mechanism.

That distinction matters because the mechanical path can affect what motion reaches the actuator. A latch or linkage may transmit, limit, or redirect movement. Without documentation or direct evidence for the particular design, it is not possible to establish whether latch movement corresponds to the same amount of actuator movement, or to infer a fixed ratio between them.

The practical interpretation is bounded: a reported latch position or movement is not, by itself, a measurement of actuator travel. To relate the two, the applicable design information needs to identify the relevant parts and how their motion is coupled. If that relationship is not available, the actuator’s travel remains unknown even when the latch’s movement can be seen.

Nor does a description of travel establish a switch’s electrical state. Mechanical position and contact behavior are separate questions. A travel term can explain where an actuator moves in relation to defined switch events, but it does not establish how a dishwasher’s control circuit responds to those events.

## How operating, release, and other travel events differ

Operating position and release position refer to distinct events. The operating point is where the switch operates as the actuator moves in the operating direction; the release point is where it releases as the actuator moves back. They should not be treated as one point or assumed to occur at the same actuator position. The difference between those events is related to differential travel, but a particular amount cannot be stated without design information.

Pretravel and overtravel describe other portions of actuator motion. Under MIL-PRF-8805K sections 6.5.14 and 6.5.16, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position. Overtravel runs from the operating position toward the extreme permitted actuator position. The standard defines overtravel as the distance or angle between the operating position and the extreme position to which the actuator may be moved.

These terms refer to positions and movement at the switch actuator. They do not describe how far a dishwasher door swings or how far a latch travels. Applying them to a latch requires evidence connecting latch motion to actuator motion; otherwise, the terms should remain tied to the switch.

No universal travel dimension or allowable actuation condition for a dishwasher door-latch switch follows from these definitions. Values and limits are design-specific. The applicable switch and mechanism documentation is needed to interpret a stated value or determine what actuator movement is permitted. Without it, do not infer a travel value from the appliance’s visible movement or from terminology alone.

## What the latch mechanism can and cannot establish

A door latch may be part of a mechanical path that moves a micro switch actuator, but its position alone does not reveal the actuator’s position. The path could involve connected parts that change how motion is transmitted or constrained. Which parts move, in what order, and how they relate to the actuator must be established for the specific arrangement rather than presumed for dishwashers generally.

Evidence useful for relating the two motions would identify the switch actuator and the latch or linkage that contacts or moves it, and describe their relationship. Applicable drawings or design documentation may provide that information. Direct observation can establish that a particular part appears to move, but it does not establish an exact travel relationship unless the relevant positions and measurement basis are known.

Keep the conclusion proportional to the evidence. If documentation identifies latch positions and corresponding actuator positions, those documented positions can be compared. If it only identifies latch movement, the actuator travel remains unresolved. If it identifies actuator movement but not the switch’s operating or release events, movement alone does not show whether either event has occurred.

This boundary also prevents assumptions about normal state or appliance behavior. The presence of a micro switch in a latch arrangement does not establish a particular contact arrangement, whether the switch is open or closed at a given door position, or what the dishwasher’s control logic does. Those details depend on the actual switch and circuit documentation, not on the word “latch.”

## How to interpret movement without overclaiming switch function

Visible actuator movement can support a limited mechanical conclusion: the actuator moved. A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but neither observation proves electrical continuity, correct wiring, or the dishwasher’s response. Do not treat a sound, latch position, or apparent actuator movement as a complete functional test.

Electrical contact behavior is a separate verification question. A continuity check, if appropriate to the available documentation, concerns the isolated switch in a defined actuator state and a documented contact arrangement. The reference state and expected contact behavior must be known before interpreting a reading. Without those details, a reading cannot establish that the switch is behaving as intended.

Even a meaningful isolated-switch result does not establish how the installed wiring or dishwasher operates. An installed-system response depends on the actual circuit and other equipment conditions. Likewise, a switch result cannot by itself prove the latch mechanism’s travel relationship or the condition of every part in the appliance.

The defensible conclusion is narrow: actuator travel is switch-actuator movement, and latch movement does not establish it unless the particular mechanical relationship is documented or otherwise established. Operating, release, pretravel, and overtravel describe distinct switch travel events, with applicable limits tied to the design. Movement or a click may show mechanical activity, but it does not prove contact operation or host-circuit behavior. For dishwasher door latch micro switch actuator travel, keep those mechanical, electrical, and appliance-level questions separate.
