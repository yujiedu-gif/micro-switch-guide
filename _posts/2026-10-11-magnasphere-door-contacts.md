## How magnetic and mechanically actuated door sensing differ

At a general conceptual level, magnetic sensing responds to a magnetic condition associated with the positions of a door and a sensing arrangement. The phrase alone does not establish which components are present, how they are positioned, or what contact state results as the door moves. Those details must be confirmed for a documented device and the system using it.

Mechanical actuation instead involves physical movement of an actuator. A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement and is commonly used for position or limit detection. This is general background, not a statement that any particular door contact contains such a switch or is suitable for a door-position task. The exact keyword provides no evidence that a mechanically actuated device is present.

The practical distinction is the input that produces the change: a magnetic condition in magnetic sensing, versus physical actuator movement in mechanical actuation. That conceptual difference does not establish which approach is more appropriate. Door construction, required signal behavior, equipment requirements, operating conditions, and documented device characteristics all remain relevant. Without evidence about those factors, neither sensing principle can be declared suitable for a particular door or system.

Do not turn a general description into an assumption about wiring or electrical behavior. ## What to verify for a door-position sensing task

Start by documenting the task rather than choosing from a phrase. Identify what the system needs to detect, which door positions or movements matter, and what signal behavior the equipment requires in each relevant state. The required behavior should come from the system’s documentation or other applicable evidence; do not assume that “open” or “closed” corresponds to a particular electrical state.

Next, identify the exact device under consideration and consult documentation that applies to it. Verify its stated sensing principle and relevant specifications, including electrical ratings, terminal functions, and operating behavior, where those details are needed. Confirm compatibility with the equipment and procedures that govern the task. If exact identity or supporting documentation is absent, leave those matters unresolved rather than filling gaps from the search wording, appearance, or a general description.

For environmental questions, distinguish enclosure protection from overall suitability. An IP code states defined enclosure-protection characteristics. It does not by itself establish electrical rating, chemical compatibility, corrosion resistance, vibration endurance, condensation performance, or complete application suitability. Evaluate those application requirements separately, using evidence appropriate to the specific device and conditions. The code alone cannot answer whether a device is appropriate for a particular installation.

A neutral verification checklist is:

- What position or movement must the system detect, and what signal behavior does it require?
- Is the exact device identity established by documentation, rather than inferred from wording?
- Does the documentation specify the sensing principle and the relevant contact behavior?
- Are electrical ratings and terminal functions documented and appropriate for the equipment, with compatibility verified?
- Are the operating conditions and any needed environmental characteristics addressed separately, rather than assumed from an IP code?
- Are installation and wiring details being taken from applicable documentation and procedures, not guessed from appearance or a generic description?

If an answer is missing, treat it as an open verification item. A general explanation of magnetic sensing or mechanical snap action can clarify what questions to ask, but it cannot certify a device, establish a rating, or confirm compatibility.
