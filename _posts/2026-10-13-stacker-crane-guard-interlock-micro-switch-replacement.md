A stacker crane guard interlock micro switch replacement is suitable only when the replacement matches the documented mechanical, electrical, and system requirements. Appearance or a similar size alone does not establish compatibility. Some guard interlock designs use a micro switch, but equipment behavior depends on the actual mechanism and circuit; the presence of a switch does not reveal how the interlock responds.

Treat the switch as one component in a larger arrangement. The guard may move a linkage, cam, or other mechanism that acts on the switch actuator, and the resulting contact change may feed a control circuit. Each relationship must be checked against equipment-specific documentation. A switch-level check cannot, by itself, validate the complete guard-interlock safety function.

## What replacement means for a stacker crane guard interlock micro switch replacement

Replacement means establishing that the candidate meets the documented requirements at each relevant interface, not merely finding a component that looks similar or fits the available space. Physical fit is only one part of the comparison. The mounting, actuator geometry and motion, contact arrangement, terminal identification, and electrical requirements all need supporting documentation.

A micro switch is an electromechanical component, not the entire guard interlock. Some guard interlock designs use one; others may use a different arrangement. Even where a micro switch is present, the equipment’s response to guard movement depends on the mechanism and circuit. Do not infer a particular control response, contact state, or safety behavior from the component name or its location.

Keep four events distinct when tracing the arrangement: the guard moves, a mechanism transfers that motion to the actuator, the actuator changes the switch contact state, and the equipment circuit responds. A candidate can appear to fit while failing to reproduce the required actuation or electrical interface. Conversely, identifying a switch contact change does not establish how the surrounding equipment uses it.

Before treating a candidate as suitable, locate the exact equipment documentation and the documentation for the switch being considered. The evidence must support the mechanical interface and electrical requirements, as well as the equipment’s safety-related requirements. If any of those are missing or unclear, compatibility remains unresolved rather than established by appearance.

## Trace how the guard actuates the micro switch

Start with the documented guard-to-switch relationship. Identify the guard and, if present, the linkage, cam, or other mechanism that contacts or moves the actuator. Confirm from equipment documentation that the candidate’s mounting position and actuator direction correspond to the intended arrangement. Do not assume that a similar-looking actuator will meet the same mechanical requirements.

Compare the required movement and switching events, not just the resting appearance. Operating point and release point are distinct events. The cited MIL-PRF-8805K provisions define pretravel as motion from the free position to the operating position, and overtravel as motion from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These terms describe travel portions; they do not provide the required values for a particular stacker crane or replacement.

Use the equipment and switch documentation to determine what movement is expected, including the relevant operating and release events. A click or visible actuator movement does not establish that the correct contact state occurs at the required point, or that the guard interlock performs its intended function. The mechanical path must be considered as a design-specific interface, rather than inferred from a generic switch layout.

If mounting details, actuator direction, or the required operating and release behavior are unavailable or conflicting, do not conclude that the candidate is mechanically equivalent. Those gaps prevent a documented comparison. Resolve them through the equipment-specific information and qualified personnel before treating the part as a replacement.

## Compare documented electrical requirements before selecting a replacement

Confirm the required contact arrangement and terminal identification from documentation for the exact switch and equipment. Do not assume COM, NO, or NC labels, or infer terminal function from physical position. A terminal name or arrangement is useful only when it is actually documented and corresponds to the equipment’s circuit requirements.

Next, compare the documented electrical requirements. MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.1.1 and applicable specification sheets treat rating values as requirements tied to stated load and test conditions, not as universal switching capacities. The candidate’s documented voltage, current, and load conditions therefore need to be compared with the requirements for the actual circuit. Do not transfer a rating from one load or test condition to another, or treat a similar rating label as proof of suitability.

The connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment are the basis for the comparison. Matching terminals alone does not prove that the candidate meets the circuit requirements. Likewise, a matching or higher-looking rating does not establish equivalence when the relevant load or conditions differ or are unspecified.

If the equipment documentation does not establish the required contact arrangement, terminal identification, or electrical conditions, the electrical comparison is incomplete. Leave suitability unresolved until the missing requirements are identified. Do not supply an assumed pinout or substitute a generic terminal layout for the documentation.

## Verify the replacement without confusing a switch check with a safety validation

Safety-related installation and validation should follow an authorized, equipment-specific procedure and be performed by qualified personnel. This article does not provide a replacement procedure or live-work instructions. Use the equipment documentation to identify the applicable requirements and verification process; do not improvise a test from a switch’s appearance or a general description.

Keep a switch-level check separate from testing the installed circuit and the complete guard-interlock function. If an isolated continuity check is authorized by the applicable procedure, its meaning depends on the defined actuator state and the expected result documented for that switch. A result under one state does not establish behavior under another state, correct installation, or the response of the equipment circuit.

MIL-PRF-8805K’s insulation-resistance requirement and referenced test method evaluate insulation resistance between specified electrically isolated points using the applicable method and conditions. This is a defined test interpretation, not a general continuity check or an independent validation of an installed interlock. Do not apply its meaning beyond the specified points, method, or conditions.

A favorable switch-level observation cannot alone prove that the guard mechanism actuates correctly, that the circuit is wired as required, or that the complete safety function works correctly. Complete-system validation is a separate responsibility governed by the equipment-specific procedure and requirements. Where that validation is not documented or cannot be completed by the appropriate personnel, do not declare the replacement or interlock validated.

## When replacement compatibility remains unresolved

Do not declare equivalence when mounting, actuator movement, contact arrangement, terminal identification, electrical ratings or conditions, or safety requirements are missing or conflict. A candidate that fits physically, produces a click, or has a similar rating label may still lack evidence for one or more required interfaces.

Use the equipment documentation to resolve the requirements, and refer unresolved identification or safety-related validation to qualified personnel. Keep the decision limited to what the available evidence establishes: mechanical correspondence, documented electrical correspondence, and compliance with the equipment’s system requirements must each be supported. A switch check is not a substitute for validation of the guard-interlock function.

The decision rule for a stacker crane guard interlock micro switch replacement is therefore straightforward: treat it as suitable only when the documented mechanical, electrical, and system requirements are all verified. If any part remains unknown, compatibility has not been established.
