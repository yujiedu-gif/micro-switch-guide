A **how to wire a micro switch** guide should begin with identification, not a universal wiring diagram. Micro switches can differ in their terminal markings, contact arrangements, and role within the equipment. A terminal’s physical position or appearance does not establish its function. Before considering any change, identify the exact switch, consult the documentation for that switch and equipment, and confirm that the work is within your qualifications and the applicable safety procedure.

This article explains how to interpret common contact labels and what to verify before changing connections. It does not provide a terminal-position map, prescribe conductor connections, or replace equipment-specific instructions. If the circuit, isolation procedure, or terminal identification is unclear, stop and seek qualified help.

## How to wire a micro switch safely starts with identification

There is no universal terminal layout or connection diagram that applies to every micro switch. Even where two devices use familiar labels, you should not assume they share the same physical arrangement, contact behavior, ratings, or intended role in the equipment. A drawing for one switch or application is not evidence for another.

Start by identifying the equipment and the exact switch configuration. Read the device markings and find the connection diagram and instructions that apply to that specific switch and equipment. Use those sources to establish terminal identity and circuit function. Do not infer a terminal’s purpose from its position, shape, color, or resemblance to a terminal on another switch.

A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement and is commonly used for position or limit detection. That general description does not tell you how a particular switch is connected in a particular machine. The equipment’s circuit function and the documented permissible load also matter. A familiar switch shape or an apparent match is not enough to establish that a connection is appropriate.

This guide is limited to identification and verification. It does not substitute for the equipment procedure, establish that a circuit is safe to access, or authorize electrical work. For equipment-specific work, follow the applicable instructions and qualification requirements. If you cannot identify the switch or determine the required procedure, do not proceed based on a generic diagram or guesswork.

## How to wire a micro switch: what COM, NO, and NC mean

On a generic changeover contact, **COM** refers to the common contact connection. **NO** means normally open, and **NC** means normally closed. “Normally” refers to the switch’s unactuated state: the NO contact is open and the NC contact is closed in that state. When the actuator moves, the contact state can change according to the specific switch design. These terms describe contact relationships, not a universal physical layout.

A switch may have different markings or a different contact arrangement, and a marking should not be interpreted apart from the device’s own diagram. Confirm the meaning of each terminal from the exact switch documentation and the equipment connection diagram. If a label is missing, unclear, or inconsistent with the documentation, treat terminal identity as unresolved rather than guessing.

Do not decide that the upper, lower, left, or right terminal is COM, NO, or NC based on appearance. Nor should you assume that the same label occupies the same position on two switches. The exact device marking or schematic controls. If the device and equipment documents do not let you confirm the terminal functions, stop and ask a qualified person to identify them.

Understanding the labels is not the same as deciding which contacts a circuit requires. That depends on the equipment’s circuit function and documented configuration. This article does not provide a general connection pattern or tell you which terminal to use for any appliance or machine. Keep contact terminology separate from application-specific wiring decisions.

## What to verify before changing micro switch connections

Before considering a wiring change, use a cautious verification process:

1. **Identify the equipment and switch.** Confirm which equipment the switch belongs to and locate the documentation for the exact switch and configuration. Do not rely on a similar-looking component or an unrelated diagram.
2. **Confirm terminal identity and circuit function.** Compare the switch’s markings with its connection diagram and the equipment instructions. Establish what each documented terminal does in that circuit. If you cannot reconcile the information, stop.
3. **Check the applicable work procedure.** Follow the equipment instructions and applicable electrical safety requirements. Do not assume that a control being off, a switch being open, or a machine appearing inactive establishes a deenergized condition.
4. **Record existing connections only when permitted and safe.** If the equipment procedure permits it and the work can be done safely, make a clear record or photograph of the existing connections before any change. A record can help preserve what was present, but it does not verify that the existing wiring is correct or that a proposed change is suitable.
5. **Stop when an essential detail is uncertain.** If the circuit, isolation procedure, terminal identification, documentation, or required qualification is unclear, do not proceed. Seek guidance from the applicable equipment documentation or a qualified professional.

In the United States workplace context, the cited workplace rule generally requires exposed live parts to be deenergized before work, requires a qualified person to verify the deenergized condition with test equipment, and limits work on energized parts to qualified persons. Verify all applicable requirements for the work and jurisdiction. This summary is not a substitute for the applicable procedure or a determination that a particular task is safe.

## When a general micro switch wiring guide is not enough

A general explanation of COM, NO, and NC cannot establish whether a switch is suitable for a specific circuit. Circuit function, permissible load, switch ratings, and equipment requirements are application-specific. Use the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. Do not transfer a rating, terminal interpretation, or wiring arrangement from a different switch or application.

The same limit applies to enclosure information. An IP code states defined enclosure-protection characteristics. It does not by itself establish electrical rating, chemical compatibility, corrosion resistance, vibration endurance, condensation performance, or complete application suitability. Evaluate those requirements separately using applicable evidence; an enclosure code alone cannot settle them.

For mains, hazardous energy, or work that depends on equipment-specific procedures, use the applicable instructions and qualified help. If documentation is absent or conflicting, or you are unsure whether the work is within your qualifications, stop rather than attempting to adapt a generic diagram. The key question in **how to wire a micro switch** is not where a terminal appears to be, but whether its identity and role are confirmed by the exact device and equipment documentation before any change.
