## What does robot vacuum bumper micro switch actuator travel describe?

Actuator travel describes movement at the micro switch actuator. Bumper displacement describes movement of the bumper. These are different measurements at different parts of a mechanism; one cannot be substituted for the other without knowing how the particular design transfers motion.

When a robot vacuum uses a micro switch in its bumper arrangement, bumper movement may be transmitted to the switch actuator through a mechanical connection. The actuator then moves relative to the switch’s operating characteristics. The exact relationship depends on the bumper mechanism, any intervening linkage or contact feature, and the switch design. The available evidence does not establish a standard bumper-to-actuator ratio or a travel value for robot vacuums generally.

This distinction matters when describing or checking operation. Observing that the bumper moves establishes only that the bumper moved. It does not establish how far the switch actuator moved, whether it reached an operating point, or whether the switch’s contacts changed state. Those questions require evidence about the particular arrangement and the relevant switch characteristics.

A micro switch is an electromechanical component, not a generic name for every switch or sensor in a robot vacuum. Some designs may use one in a bumper arrangement; the presence of a bumper alone does not establish that they do. The discussion here applies only where that component and mechanical relationship are documented.

## How bumper motion reaches a micro switch actuator

For an arrangement that uses a micro switch, the mechanical sequence can be considered in separate stages: the bumper moves, a linkage or contact feature may transmit that motion, and the switch actuator moves. The parts are not interchangeable in description. Bumper travel is motion at the bumper; actuator travel is motion at the switch actuator.

The motion reaching the actuator depends on the particular mechanical path. A linkage, contact feature, alignment, or mechanical limit may affect how much movement is transmitted. These are factors to investigate in the design, not evidence that every robot vacuum uses the same construction. This separation also helps avoid a misleading inference: visible bumper displacement does not by itself show that the actuator received sufficient movement. Without it, a numerical comparison or a claim that the bumper should move a particular distance would be unsupported.

The switch’s travel terms refer to positions and movement at the switch actuator. Keeping the stages distinct makes it possible to discuss mechanical input without assuming that the switch operated or that the host equipment responded in a particular way.

## How operating and release travel relate

In the terminology of MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.5, the specification distinguishes free position, operating position, movement differential, pretravel, and overtravel, as well as actuating force and releasing force. These terms describe switch characteristics, not a general robot vacuum bumper travel specification.

Pretravel runs from the switch actuator’s free position to its operating position. The operating position is the point associated with operation in the applicable switch specification. Overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These definitions distinguish movement before operation from additional permitted movement after operation. They do not state how far a bumper must move, or how much movement a particular linkage transfers.

The operating point and release point are distinct events. Movement differential describes the difference between the positions associated with operation and release. Thus, a switch may operate as the actuator moves in one direction and release at a different position as it returns. The specification’s terminology does not supply a numerical value for a robot vacuum arrangement without evidence for the specific switch and reference conditions.

This matters when interpreting motion. Reaching an operating position and returning through a release position are not the same event. A description that says only that the bumper moved, or that the actuator clicked, does not identify either position. Nor does it establish the amount of pretravel, overtravel, or movement differential for the installed design.

No numeric travel target is available from the evidence here. Do not infer one from generic terminology or transfer a switch specification to bumper displacement. Any comparison with actual motion would need applicable design-specific travel data and a defined reference condition.

## When bumper movement does not establish switch operation

If the bumper moves but the expected equipment behavior is absent, insufficient actuator movement is one possible mechanical explanation, not a conclusion. The bumper may move without transmitting enough motion to reach the switch’s operating position. Whether that is happening depends on the particular linkage, alignment, limits, and switch design; no universal symptom-to-cause rule is established.

A separate possibility is an internal contact issue. Even if the actuator moves and the mechanism clicks, the sound alone does not prove that the contacts changed state or have electrical continuity. Mechanical movement and electrical contact behavior are related but distinct observations.

Terminal identification or wiring may also be involved. A switch’s contact arrangement and the connections used in a particular circuit must be established for that design. Bumper movement or a click does not verify that terminals are identified correctly or that wiring is connected as intended. No terminal arrangement or wiring scheme for a robot vacuum is specified here.

Finally, the cause may be elsewhere in the equipment. A contact change, if one occurs, does not by itself establish what the host system should do; that depends on the actual circuit and control logic. The bumper’s movement cannot prove that the equipment received, interpreted, or acted on a signal. Avoid treating one visible or audible symptom as a complete diagnosis of the switch or the whole robot vacuum.
