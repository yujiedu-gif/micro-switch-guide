An **antenna rotator closed position micro switch replacement** is appropriate only when the rotator actually uses a micro switch to detect its closed limit. Designs vary, so first confirm the mechanism rather than assuming that a particular switch, wire color, or visible component controls that position. A miniature snap-action switch can change electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement, and such switches are commonly used for position or limit detection. That general function does not establish that a given switch is suitable for a particular rotator.

The replacement should preserve the original point at which the mechanism actuates the closed limit. Before removing anything, distinguish an electrical switching failure from a cam or mounting problem that prevents the switch from being operated. Record the existing wiring and geometry, test the switch with the circuit isolated, and verify mechanical engagement separately. If you cannot secure the equipment or access it safely, stop and use an appropriate service procedure.

## Confirm the antenna rotator uses a closed-position micro switch

Consult the equipment’s service information or inspect the limit mechanism to establish how the closed position is detected. Some designs may use a different sensing method. Do not identify a switch by its location alone: trace or verify which mechanism corresponds to the closed position. Wire color is not enough to establish a switch’s function.

Before opening the mechanism, disconnect power and prevent unexpected rotation. Follow the equipment’s safe access procedure, and make sure the antenna and rotator are secured against movement. If access requires unsafe work at height, or the equipment cannot be kept secure, do not proceed with an exposed mechanism.

Once access is safe, identify the switch and the mechanical part that operates it, such as the relevant cam or actuator. Check the service information where available, and compare the mechanism’s operation with the closed-position function. Do not assume that a switch is defective merely because the rotator does not stop where expected. A misaligned or damaged cam, loose mounting, wiring fault, or controller problem can produce a similar symptom.

The scope here is limited to a closed-position switch in a design that uses one. It does not establish how to replace an open-position switch or repair the motor, control box, or antenna system generally. If the mechanism does not clearly match the closed-limit function, resolve that uncertainty before removing a component.

## Antenna rotator closed position micro switch replacement: record and test first

With power isolated and unexpected motion prevented, document the original arrangement before disturbing it. Photograph or label the terminal connections, and record the switch’s mounting position, actuator orientation, and the cam’s position relative to the actuator. These notes provide a reference for restoring the original wiring and trigger geometry; they do not prove that the existing geometry is correct if a cam or mount is damaged.

Test the switch with the circuit isolated. A multimeter continuity or resistance check can establish whether the electrical contact state changes as the actuator is operated. Compare the state with the actuator released and operated, without assuming a particular terminal arrangement or contact configuration. If the state does not change, the switch may be faulty, but first ensure that the test is made across the appropriate contacts and that the actuator is actually moving.

Check mechanical engagement independently of the electrical test. Observe whether the cam physically reaches and moves the switch actuator at the intended point. Look for a damaged cam, loose mounting, or misalignment that could keep a working switch from changing state. A switch that changes state when operated by hand may still fail to engage in the mechanism if the cam does not reach it. Conversely, visible engagement does not by itself establish that the electrical contacts change state.

The distinction matters because replacing a functioning switch will not correct a failed cam, unstable mounting, or wiring problem. If the switch changes state during an isolated test and the cam operates it, but the rotator still does not stop, do not assume that the switch itself is the cause. Preserve the observations and investigate the other parts of the limit path before replacement.

## Replace the closed-limit micro switch without changing its trigger point

Choose a replacement only by confirming its electrical and mechanical specifications against the original. Appearance alone cannot establish suitability, and no rating, terminal arrangement, or compatibility can be inferred from a switch’s size or shape. If the needed specifications are unknown, leave the replacement choice unresolved until they can be verified.

Keep power isolated while removing the original switch. Use the wiring record or photographs to transfer conductors to corresponding terminals, and check that each connection is secure. Do not rely on wire color as a substitute for the recorded terminal mapping. Mount the replacement at the documented position, maintaining the original orientation and alignment as far as the verified mechanism allows.

Before closing the mechanism, check that the cam operates the actuator through the intended travel. Compare the engagement point with the record made before removal. The goal is to preserve the original closed-stop actuation point, not to create a new one by bending the actuator. Bending or repositioning the actuator can mask a damaged cam or misaligned mount and change the point at which the limit is detected.

If the cam or mounting is visibly damaged or cannot operate the replacement consistently, stop rather than compensating with actuator adjustment. A replacement switch cannot correct those mechanical faults. Likewise, if the wiring cannot be matched confidently to the recorded connections, do not guess at terminal placement. Resolve the wiring or component uncertainty before restoring power.

## Verify closed-position switching and stopping safely

Reassemble the mechanism and clear the area before restoring power. Restore power only as required for a cautious functional check, following the equipment’s safe access procedure. Observe the mechanism from a safe position, and avoid placing yourself where unexpected motion could cause harm.

Approach the closed limit cautiously. Confirm both that the switch changes electrical state at the intended point and that the rotator stops at the expected closed position. Repeat the check to determine whether the cam engages the actuator consistently and the stop point is repeatable. Compare the result with the original record rather than treating a single successful stop as proof of consistent operation.

Do not force the mechanism against its stop or overdrive it to test the limit. If motion continues past the expected point, disconnect power and investigate before further operation. If the switch’s electrical state changes but the rotator does not stop, the fault may lie in the wiring or control path rather than in the switch. A damaged or misaligned cam, loose mounting, wiring fault, or controller problem can require attention independently of the replacement.

A successful result requires both reliable switch-state change and repeatable stopping at the expected closed point. If either result is uncertain, do not treat the replacement as verified. The **antenna rotator closed position micro switch replacement** is complete only after the mechanism has been checked without forcing the stop and the original trigger geometry has been preserved or its unresolved mechanical condition addressed.
