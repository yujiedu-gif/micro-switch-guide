Suspected spring fatigue in a micro switch should be treated as a mechanical question, not diagnosed from a click or a single electrical result. How to check micro switch spring fatigue means observing repeatable actuator movement and return under a suitable, design-specific procedure, then keeping those observations separate from any continuity check. Neither check alone proves that the spring is the cause of a problem or that the complete assembly is functioning correctly.

The useful evidence is comparative: what the actuator does in a defined setup, whether it returns consistently, and how those observations compare with a documented baseline for that design. If no baseline or applicable procedure is available, an observation may flag abnormal behavior, but it cannot quantify fatigue. A restriction elsewhere in the mechanism can also imitate a weak or slow return.

## How to check micro switch spring fatigue: what the check can establish

The check is a screen for abnormal spring-related mechanical behavior. It does not directly measure fatigue unless a design-specific method and baseline define what is being measured. Do not infer spring condition from a single actuation, a click, or a continuity result alone. A click can accompany actuator movement, but it does not establish how the actuator returns or whether the contacts have the expected electrical behavior.

Start by identifying the specific switch and the procedure that applies to it. The procedure needs to establish the permitted actuator movement, the relevant reference conditions, and what normal return behavior looks like. If it specifies a force, travel, or other threshold, use that specification only within its stated conditions. Do not substitute an assumed value or compare observations made under different conditions.

A useful assessment separates three questions:

- Does the actuator move and return consistently under the documented mechanical check?
- Could a linkage or other external restriction account for the observed behavior?
- If electrical behavior also needs checking, can it be assessed separately using documented terminals, defined actuator states, isolation, and an appropriate procedure?

Each question has a different evidentiary limit. Mechanical observations can flag behavior for investigation. They do not establish contact condition. A continuity result can report the isolated switch's electrical behavior at a defined state, but does not identify spring fatigue as its cause. Neither result by itself diagnoses the installed circuit or the surrounding equipment.

## Observe actuator movement and return under controlled conditions

Only observe movement under an appropriate, documented procedure. With appropriate isolation and the required procedure in place, move the actuator through its permitted range and release it without forcing it beyond the design travel. The purpose is to note what happens, not to challenge the mechanism or create a test threshold that the design does not provide.

Record observations in descriptive terms. For example, note whether the actuator returns fully, whether its return appears delayed, or whether comparable releases produce inconsistent results. These are observations that may warrant investigation; they are not proof of fatigue. Do not translate them into a force, travel, fatigue level, or remaining-life estimate unless an applicable design-specific specification supplies a method and threshold.

Repeat comparable cycles only if the documented procedure permits. Keep the setup and conditions alike from one observation to the next. If the setup changes, the comparison may no longer distinguish a change in the switch from a change in the way it is being actuated. A result that cannot be repeated under comparable conditions is weaker evidence than a consistent pattern, but even a repeatable return problem does not establish its cause.

Do not force an actuator that appears restricted or continue beyond the procedure's permitted movement. If the required procedure, safe access, or normal behavior is unknown, the observation has reached its limit. The appropriate conclusion is that the behavior remains unverified, not that the spring is fatigued.

## Separate spring behavior from binding or misalignment

A weak-looking or slow return can be produced by more than one mechanical path. A lever, cam, or other linkage may impose restriction or alter how force reaches the switch actuator. Misalignment or another mechanical issue may also affect observed movement. These alternatives can make a switch appear to return poorly even when the observation does not isolate the switch's internal mechanism.

Where the setup permits and it is safe to do so, distinguish movement of the switch actuator itself from movement or resistance imposed by the surrounding mechanism. The aim is to learn whether the observed restriction is present at the actuator or arises from the external arrangement. Do not assume that a problem belongs to the spring merely because it occurs while the switch is installed in a linkage.

This distinction has a firm limit: the available evidence does not define how to separate external linkage binding from the internal switch mechanism in every design. If the actuator cannot be assessed independently under an approved procedure, report the limitation rather than attributing the behavior to the spring. Do not dismantle the switch or improvise internal repair steps to make the distinction.

An abnormal click does not settle this question. It may be an observation of movement, but it cannot show whether the restriction is internal or external, whether the actuator returns correctly, or whether the contacts behave as required. Likewise, an abnormal return pattern identifies a behavior to investigate, not a specific failed part.

## Use continuity checks only as a separate, conditional check

A continuity check addresses electrical behavior, not spring condition. It concerns the isolated switch at a defined actuator state. Use such a check only if a documented procedure identifies the terminals, the relevant actuator state, the expected contact behavior, and the required isolation. Do not infer terminal identities or expected readings when the switch documentation does not provide them.

Keep the mechanical and electrical observations distinct. If the actuator has an unusual return and an isolated continuity check also differs from the documented expectation, the two findings may both warrant investigation. The electrical result does not prove that spring fatigue caused the return behavior or the contact result. A continuity anomaly may relate to a contact or terminal issue; it does not, by itself, identify the spring as the cause.

A normal result at one defined state is also limited. It reports only what the check establishes for that isolated switch, terminal identification, and state under the stated procedure. It does not prove the switch behaves correctly at other states or under installed conditions. Do not use an isolated reading to infer wiring correctness, circuit operation, or the safety of the surrounding equipment.

If the contact arrangement, terminals, test state, isolation, or procedure is uncertain, do not guess. Stop short of interpreting the reading and obtain the information or qualified assistance needed to perform the check. The mechanical assessment can remain a separate observation, but it cannot fill in missing electrical documentation.

## When observations are inconclusive or require qualified assessment

Without a documented baseline or design-specific procedure, observations can flag abnormal behavior but cannot quantify spring fatigue, establish its cause, or predict remaining life. A force or travel value is meaningful only when the applicable specification and its conditions are known; an assumed threshold is not evidence. A click, a single actuation, or a continuity result cannot substitute for that baseline.

The assessment can support a cautious description of what was observed: for example, whether the actuator returned consistently in the permitted check, whether an external restriction could be distinguished, and whether any separate isolated electrical check followed documented requirements. It cannot establish that the switch, linkage, wiring, or complete equipment assembly is good or bad as a whole.

If safe isolation, terminal identification, access, or interpretation is uncertain, stop and refer the work to qualified personnel. Do not dismantle the switch or attempt an internal spring repair without an approved procedure. When the available evidence cannot distinguish external restriction from internal behavior, state that uncertainty and seek a design-specific assessment rather than naming spring fatigue as a confirmed fault.

The practical decision boundary for how to check micro switch spring fatigue is therefore narrow: compare permitted, repeatable mechanical behavior with a documented design baseline, investigate whether the surrounding mechanism could be imposing resistance, and treat any properly conducted continuity check as separate evidence. Without those conditions, report an unresolved abnormal observation, not a measured fatigue condition or a diagnosis of the whole assembly.
