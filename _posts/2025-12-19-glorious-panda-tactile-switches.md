A typist may notice a bump, a brief increase or decrease in resistance, or another change in the motion of the key. ## How to interpret a tactile bump during a keystroke

When evaluating a key by feel, describe the sequence without presuming what causes it. For instance, a typist might report feeling a distinct change in resistance during downward movement. That observation communicates a personal experience. It does not, by itself, reveal the point of electrical contact, the amount of force involved, or how far the key has traveled.

A tactile event and electrical actuation are different concepts. The first refers to a perceived change in key feel; the second refers to a change in electrical state. They could be related in a particular switch, but the relation cannot be inferred from the word “tactile,” from a search phrase, or from the sensation alone. Without exact, relevant evidence, avoid saying that the bump occurs before, at, or after actuation.

Sound should also be kept separate from feel. A person may notice a sound while pressing a key, but hearing a sound does not establish the switch’s electrical state or identify its mechanism. Nor does the phrase provide a supported sound profile. Any description of a particular switch’s sound would need to be grounded in evidence for that exact item and the conditions under which it was assessed.

A careful description stays observational: what the hand perceives, at what point in the press the perception occurs, and whether the observation is repeatable. It avoids converting a sensation into an unsupported specification. If the exact switch is not established, numerical force or travel values and claims about where actuation occurs should remain unresolved.

## Which parts of tactile feel are measurable, and which are subjective?

Some aspects of switch behavior can be measured, provided the item is identified and the method is clear. Examples include force or travel measurements and the electrical state under specified conditions. These are not interchangeable with a typist’s shorthand such as “crisp,” “soft,” or “pronounced.” Such words can help communicate an impression, but people may use them differently, and they do not supply a numerical value or test result.

A comparison is meaningful only when the compared switches are identified precisely and assessed using consistent methods. Otherwise, differences in description may reflect different products, variants, test conditions, or individual perceptions. The phrase alone does not resolve those possibilities. It is not enough to support a force curve, a travel figure, a sound comparison, or a claim that a particular tactile event corresponds to electrical actuation.

Subjective preference is separate from measurable behavior. Two typists can experience or describe the same key differently, and neither account establishes a universal standard for which feel is better. A statement that a bump feels noticeable may be valid as a report from one person, but it should not be presented as a verified product characteristic unless supported by suitable evidence. Likewise, a measured value does not determine whether a reader will find the feel comfortable or desirable.

When reading a description, ask what kind of claim it makes. Is it a personal impression, a measured result, or a statement about electrical operation? For a measurement, look for the exact switch identity and the method or conditions. For an electrical claim, look for documentation tied to that exact identifier. For an impression, treat it as an individual observation rather than a guarantee of another typist’s experience. This separation helps keep subjective wording from being mistaken for a specification.
