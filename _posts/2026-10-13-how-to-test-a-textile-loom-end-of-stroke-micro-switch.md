Testing an end-of-stroke input begins with identifying the actual component and making the machine safe. How to test a textile loom end of stroke micro switch depends on whether that loom uses a micro switch for the input at all. The procedure below is limited to an isolated contact check; it does not establish how the loom’s control system responds or prove that the end-of-stroke function works in service.

Do not probe an energized control circuit or bypass the switch. Before any isolated test, follow the applicable machine procedure to make the loom safe and electrically isolate it. If isolation cannot be verified, stop and have qualified personnel handle the diagnosis.

## How to test a textile loom end of stroke micro switch safely

This procedure applies only to a textile loom design that actually uses a micro switch as an end-of-stroke input. A micro switch is a component, not the complete end-of-stroke system. Other designs may use a different arrangement, so do not assume that a device at the end of a loom’s travel is a micro switch or that every loom uses the same mechanism.

Start with the machine-specific procedure for making the loom safe and electrically isolated. An isolated contact test is meaningful only when the switch is separated from the loom circuit under a procedure appropriate to that machine. If you cannot confirm that the relevant electrical source has been isolated, do not proceed with the test. Stop and refer the work to qualified personnel.

Do not defeat guards, bypass contacts, or probe an energized control circuit as part of this check. The procedure is limited to observing actuator movement and checking the isolated switch contacts at defined states. It is not a procedure for adjusting or repairing the loom mechanism, diagnosing live control logic, or determining what the loom should do after the input changes.

## Identify the micro switch and its actuator before testing

Locate the component using machine documentation rather than assuming that a particular loom arrangement is universal. Where it is documented, or can be observed without unsafe access, identify the cam, lever, or linkage that moves the switch actuator. The relevant observation is whether that mechanical input engages and releases the actuator at the end-of-stroke event. Do not infer from the component’s location alone that it has been correctly positioned or that the loom’s control system assigns a particular response to it.

Before checking continuity, identify the switch terminals and the expected contact states from reliable documentation for the actual component. Record the documented terminal identities and the reference condition for each state. Do not infer COM, NO, or NC from terminal appearance, a click, or the switch’s mounting position. Those labels and state expectations are useful only when they are documented for the switch being tested and tied to a specified reference condition.

If terminal identities, the actuator reference state, or the expected contact behavior are unknown, the readings cannot be interpreted reliably. Stop rather than assigning a generic terminal pattern or assuming which contacts should be continuous. Uncertainty about identification is not evidence that the switch has failed.

## Check the isolated contact response at defined actuator states

Proceed with a continuity check only if the switch has been isolated from the loom circuit and the documented terminal arrangement and applicable safe procedure support the test. Use the defined terminal pair and compare its response at the documented unactuated reference state with its response at the documented actuated state. Keep the actuator state explicit when recording each observation. If the mechanism permits, observe and record release as a separate event rather than treating it as the same observation as engagement.

Interpret the readings only against the expected behavior for that documented contact arrangement. There is no universal terminal pattern or resistance threshold established here, so do not invent one. If the documentation does not specify the relevant terminals and reference states, a continuity reading alone cannot tell you whether the result is correct.

The test answers a narrow question: under the tested conditions, did the isolated contacts show the documented response as the actuator moved between the defined states? It does not establish that the switch is correctly installed, that the loom wiring is correct, or that the end-of-stroke input produces the intended behavior in the machine.

## Separate a switch response from a loom end-of-stroke fault

Observe mechanical engagement and release separately from the electrical readings. Visible actuator movement is evidence of movement. A click may also indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but neither a click nor visible movement proves contact continuity. Conversely, a continuity observation without confirmed terminal identities and actuator reference states may be uninterpretable.

If the isolated contacts do not show the documented change, the observation does not identify a single cause. The actuator may not have reached the defined state; terminal identification may be wrong; or there may be an internal contact issue. These are distinct possibilities, and the isolated result alone cannot determine which one applies. Do not turn the absence of a reading change into a definitive diagnosis without confirming the test setup and the relevant mechanical states.

If the isolated contacts do show the documented change, that supports only the conclusion that the contacts changed state under the tested conditions. It does not prove that installed wiring carries the signal correctly or that the loom’s control system responds as intended. A fault elsewhere in the wiring or control system remains outside the scope of this isolated test. The contact change also does not establish whether the loom should stop, reverse, or signal a fault; that behavior depends on the actual machine design and control logic.

## Know when to stop and hand off the diagnosis

Stop if safe isolation cannot be verified, if terminal identities or expected contact behavior are uncertain, or if the actuator cannot be observed without unsafe access. Do not compensate for missing information by bypassing the switch, probing an energized circuit, or assuming the loom’s control response. The isolated test has a limited conclusion, even when its readings match the documented expectation.

Leave energized circuit checks, adjustments, and repairs to qualified personnel following machine-specific procedures. If an isolated result is unexpected, it is a reason to have the test setup and the separate mechanical, contact, wiring, and control-system possibilities evaluated, not proof of one particular failure. If the result matches expectations, it still does not prove the end-of-stroke function works when installed or during loom operation.

In short, how to test a textile loom end of stroke micro switch safely means confirming the component and documented states, isolating it before checking continuity, and keeping mechanical observations distinct from electrical results. Treat the outcome as evidence about the isolated contact response only. The broader end-of-stroke function remains unverified until the installed machine is assessed under its own procedures by qualified personnel.
