## What the end-of-stroke micro switch detects

“End of stroke” describes the limit of a mechanism’s intended travel. In a binding machine, the relevant motion is that of a binding component during its cycle. The location question is therefore a motion-tracing problem: determine which assembly moves, where its intended travel ends, and whether a switch senses that boundary.

When a micro switch is used for this purpose, a moving feature, such as a cam or lever, actuates its lever or plunger as the mechanism reaches the relevant position. The switch provides a sensing function. It should not be confused with the hard mechanical stop, which physically limits movement. The sensing point and the mechanical stop may be near each other, but they are not the same component or function.

The switch’s state is meaningful in relation to the mechanism’s position. A component that changes state before the assembly reaches its travel boundary may be sensing an intermediate position instead. Conversely, a nearby physical stop does not by itself show that a switch is present or identify what it senses. Confirm the relationship among the moving part, switch actuator, and intended endpoint rather than inferring purpose from proximity alone.

The term “micro switch” also should not be treated as a guaranteed design feature. A particular machine may use another sensing technology, or may not have a separate switch at the endpoint. The machine’s mechanical and electrical documentation is needed to establish which design applies.

## How to tell the endpoint switch from nearby parts

Compare the suspected switch’s actuator position with the moving assembly at the travel boundary. Ask whether the moving feature actuates the switch at that boundary, or whether the switch appears to respond at a different point in the mechanism’s travel. The endpoint relationship, supported by documentation, is the basis for distinguishing an end-of-stroke signal from intermediate-position sensing.

Keep the electrical sensing point separate from the hard stop in your identification. A hard stop is the physical limit on motion; a switch senses position. Their proximity may make them easy to confuse during a visual inspection, but one does not establish the presence or function of the other. Do not treat a bracket, stop, or other nearby component as the switch unless its identity and role can be confirmed.

A component that appears to be a switch may also serve another point in the travel sequence. Its appearance alone does not show whether it detects the endpoint. Check the actuator-to-motion relationship and compare it with the machine’s documented sequence. If the suspected switch changes state at an intermediate position rather than at the intended travel boundary, that evidence does not support identifying it as the end-of-stroke switch.

Some designs use another sensing technology or no separate micro switch at the endpoint. If the documentation does not show a micro switch, do not reclassify another sensor based only on its shape or location. The correct conclusion may be that the machine’s design does not use the component being sought.
