A **dock leveler end of stroke micro switch location** cannot be identified by assuming that every design puts the switch in the same place. The specific equipment documentation must identify the switch and its relationship to the moving mechanism. The switch may be mounted away from the leveler’s physical travel endpoint, with a mechanism connecting the two.

To locate it, start with the drawing, parts identification, or service documentation for the particular dock leveler. Then trace the documented mechanical path to the switch and distinguish the component’s mounting point from the position or event that actuates it. Identification does not establish what the control system does in response, or prove that the switch or leveler is functioning correctly. If the documentation is missing or safe access is uncertain, do not guess or search around moving or energized equipment.

## What “end of stroke” means for a dock leveler micro switch

“End of stroke” describes a travel condition only when documentation for the specific equipment identifies the micro switch with that function. The phrase alone does not establish which position is involved. It should not be taken to mean that the leveler is fully raised, fully lowered, or at any other particular position unless the applicable documentation says so.

Three locations or events need to be kept distinct:

- **Switch mounting point:** where the switch is physically attached to the equipment.
- **Actuation point:** where the mechanism causes the switch actuator to operate.
- **Travel endpoint:** the physical limit of the leveler’s movement, as defined for the equipment.

These may be related without being the same point. A moving part could operate a cam, lever, or linkage, which in turn moves the switch actuator. That is a possible mechanical arrangement, not a universal dock leveler design. The available evidence does not establish a standard mounting point or a fixed distance between the switch and the leveler’s travel endpoint.

The distinction also matters when interpreting travel terminology. In the stated definitions from MIL-PRF-8805K sections 6.5.14 and 6.5.16, pretravel runs from the actuator’s free position to its operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. Those terms describe actuator movement; they do not, by themselves, identify where a dock leveler switch is mounted or which leveler position it detects. Do not use them to infer a mounting location or a particular end-of-stroke condition.

## dock leveler end of stroke micro switch location: trace the documented actuation path

Begin with documentation for the specific dock leveler: its drawing, parts identification, or service documentation. Look for the switch and the mechanism identified as acting on it. Do not substitute a diagram or instructions from another design, even if nearby equipment appears similar.

Next, follow the documented mechanical path from the moving leveler structure toward the switch. The path may include a cam, lever, or linkage, but these are design-dependent possibilities. Identify the parts only as the equipment documentation does. The aim is to understand which moving part leads to the switch actuator, not to infer a location from a general arrangement.

Use the documented mounting context and actuator contact to distinguish the switch from nearby components. A switch, sensor, limit device, or unrelated electrical part may be near the same mechanism; proximity or appearance alone does not establish which component performs the documented end-of-stroke function. If the drawings do not identify the component or its mechanical relationship clearly, treat its identity and location as unresolved rather than guessing.

When comparing the drawing with the equipment, keep the three reference points separate: where the switch is mounted, where its actuator is operated, and where the leveler’s travel ends. A linkage can connect these points while placing them in different locations. The documented mechanism, not the phrase “end of stroke” by itself, is what connects the switch to a travel condition.

## Verify the location without confusing it with the control response

Location verification should match the documented switch position and mechanical path to the specified travel condition. Use only an observation method authorized for the equipment and safe under its applicable procedures. If a safe observation cannot establish the relationship, that is a limit on what can be verified, not a reason to reach toward the mechanism or move the leveler to search for the component.

Keep separate records of what is established: the switch’s mounting point, the mechanism that acts on its actuator, and the travel condition associated with that actuation. Do not treat the control system’s response as evidence of mounting location unless applicable documentation establishes the relationship. Whether a contact change stops, signals, or locks out the leveler is a separate question that cannot be answered from the switch’s location alone.

A click or apparent actuator movement is also limited evidence. It does not alone prove electrical operation, correct wiring, or correct operation of the leveler assembly. Likewise, identifying a switch in a drawing does not prove that the installed component is in the documented position or that the equipment functions as intended. Location identification and functional verification are distinct tasks.

The conclusion should stay narrow: documentation and an authorized observation may establish where the switch is mounted and how the mechanism actuates it. They do not establish an undocumented control response or prove the condition of the full assembly.

## When documentation or safe access is missing

Do not guess a mounting point or move the leveler to search for a switch. If the equipment documentation does not identify the part or its actuation path, the location remains unresolved until the applicable information is obtained. Record the equipment identification and consult documentation for that specific design; do not apply instructions from a different dock leveler.

Do not reach into the mechanism or test equipment that is moving or energized. Follow the equipment-specific safety procedures. If identification requires access to hazardous motion or energized components, stop and use qualified service personnel. This is a boundary for locating the component, not an adjustment, replacement, wiring, or troubleshooting procedure.

The **dock leveler end of stroke micro switch location** is therefore specific to the documented design. Identify it by tracing the documented mechanism from the moving structure to the switch actuator, while keeping mounting point, actuation point, and travel endpoint distinct. If that path cannot be established safely and from applicable documentation, do not infer the location.
