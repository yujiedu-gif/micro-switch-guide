Contact welding is an electrical contact-separation failure to consider when choosing a micro switch for a particular load. Micro switch contact welding selection therefore starts with the application’s actual electrical conditions, not with a generic switch label or a judgment about overall product quality. A suitable conclusion depends on whether the documented rating applies to the current type, load, and switching conditions in the application.

The electrical review is only one part of selection. Keep it separate from whether an actuator can interact with the mechanism as intended, whether the switch’s permitted motion fits the installation, and whether the terminals and surrounding circuit are understood. If essential application or switch-specific evidence is missing, suitability remains unresolved. A rating match alone does not establish that the complete system is safe or suitable.

## What contact welding means in a micro switch

For this selection task, contact welding means a failure in which the contacts do not separate as intended. It is an electrical contact condition, not a description of an actuator that fails to move or of an external wiring problem. This narrow definition identifies the concern without implying that welding is inevitable for any particular load.

The available evidence defines snap action as contact action in which the speed of the moving contacts is relatively independent of the speed of the actuating mechanism. That definition describes the relationship between contact movement and actuator movement. It does not establish the condition of the contacts under every electrical load or show that a particular application is protected from welding.

A click or visible actuator movement is not enough to confirm electrical contact condition. Those observations concern mechanical movement; they do not, by themselves, establish that contacts separate correctly during operation. Likewise, a symptom at the host equipment does not identify contact welding by itself. The cause might lie in the switch, the actuator interaction, wiring, or another part of the equipment, and this selection framework does not diagnose an installed fault.

Keep the question focused: can the documented electrical rating be applied to the actual load and switching conditions? Do not turn that question into an unsupported claim about a specific switch, expected service life, or the likelihood of welding. Those conclusions require evidence for the particular conditions being considered.

## Micro switch contact welding selection starts with the actual load

Begin by recording the load type and whether the application uses AC or DC. These are essential distinctions because a rating stated for one current type, load, or test condition does not establish a rating for another. Do not transfer an AC rating to DC service, or the reverse, and do not assume that two loads are equivalent because their labels appear similar.

Next, identify the switching conditions that the applicable specification sheet uses to state a rating. The rating values are requirements tied to stated load and test conditions, not universal switching capacities. Keep each condition beside the value it qualifies. If the application’s actual conditions do not match, or cannot be compared with, the stated conditions, the rating’s relevance has not been established.

The application record should capture the information needed to make that comparison, including the current type, load type, and switching conditions called out by the applicable evidence. Do not estimate an unstated load characteristic or fill a gap with a general assumption. If the available documentation does not identify a required condition, mark it as unknown and withhold a suitability conclusion until the gap is resolved.

This step maps the electrical question; it does not establish mechanical fit or diagnose the host equipment. Nor does identifying the load by name alone prove that a particular rating applies. The decision depends on the stated scope of the rating and the actual conditions in which the switch is expected to operate.

## Match documented ratings to the application conditions

Compare the application with the rating explicitly stated for the applicable current type, load, and test condition. Consult the applicable specification sheet and check whether its stated conditions cover the intended use. Treat a value outside those conditions as unverified, even if the value appears relevant or familiar.

Keep AC and DC evidence distinct throughout the comparison. A value provided for AC does not establish a DC rating; a DC value does not establish an AC rating. Apply the same rule to load type and test conditions: a rating for one stated load or test does not prove suitability for a different one. If a specification sheet presents several values, assess each only within its own documented scope.

A matching documented rating is evidence for that electrical comparison, not a guarantee against contact welding. It also does not establish service life, prove that every operating event falls within the stated conditions, or certify the surrounding equipment. Avoid converting a rating into a promise of performance beyond the evidence that defines it.

If a necessary rating or condition is absent, record the gap rather than substituting a nearby value or making a generalization. The appropriate result may be that the available information is insufficient to determine suitability. That is a more accurate conclusion than claiming the switch is suitable or unsuitable without the conditions needed to support either judgment.

## Keep electrical suitability separate from mechanical fit

After the electrical conditions are documented, assess physical interaction as a separate selection dimension. The actuator is moved by the external mechanism, so the relevant question is whether the application’s mechanism interacts with the documented switch design and its permitted motion. Use only evidence that applies to the particular design. If permitted motion or actuator interaction is not documented, do not infer it from a general description of micro switches.

Mechanical fit cannot establish electrical suitability. A mechanism that moves an actuator as intended does not show that the contacts are rated for the application’s load. Conversely, an applicable electrical rating does not show that the actuator can be reached or moved appropriately in the installation. Keep these checks distinct rather than treating success on one as proof of the other.

Terminal identification is another separate issue. Do not infer terminal arrangement or function from a rating, physical fit, or the general term “micro switch.” Use terminal information only when it is documented for the switch being considered. The contact arrangement and the surrounding circuit’s response depend on the actual design; they cannot be inferred solely from the presence of a switch.

This separation prevents a selection decision from blending contact-welding concerns with installation assumptions. Evaluate electrical rating scope, mechanical interaction, and the documented terminal arrangement on their own evidence. If any needed dimension is unresolved, the overall selection remains unresolved as well.

## When the evidence is not enough to select

Withhold a suitability conclusion when the application’s AC or DC status, load type, switching conditions, or relevant rating scope is unknown. The same applies when the applicable specification sheet does not state a condition needed for comparison, or when the switch-specific evidence does not establish the mechanical interaction or permitted motion required by the installation. These are evidence gaps, not grounds for guessing.

The decision boundary is straightforward: select only when the documented electrical rating applies to the actual current type, load, and stated switching conditions, and the separate mechanical requirements are supported by relevant evidence. If those comparisons cannot be made, the evidence does not support a selection conclusion. Have unresolved electrical conditions or safety implications reviewed by a qualified person rather than treating a general rating statement as a substitute.

This framework is for selection, not repair, field diagnosis, or certification of the host equipment. A rating match alone does not prove that the complete system is safe or suitable, and a selection review does not establish that an installed switch has failed. Micro switch contact welding selection remains evidence-based: apply each rating only within its documented conditions, keep electrical and mechanical questions separate, and state plainly when the available information is insufficient.
