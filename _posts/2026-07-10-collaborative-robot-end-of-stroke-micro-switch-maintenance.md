A collaborative robot end of stroke micro switch maintenance plan should begin with the robot’s documented service procedure, not with assumptions about how every robot detects motion. Some collaborative-robot mechanisms use a micro switch to detect a mechanically defined end-of-stroke event; others use a different sensing arrangement. Where a micro switch is present, its condition is only one part of the system. The mechanism must engage it, the wiring must carry the resulting state, and the robot’s control design determines how that state is interpreted.

Maintain and verify the switch by checking access and identification, inspecting its mechanical engagement, and performing only the tests allowed by the applicable robot and switch documentation. Keep observations about switch movement separate from electrical results and equipment-level behavior. A single click or continuity reading cannot establish that the installed robot system operates correctly or safely.

## collaborative robot end of stroke micro switch maintenance

First establish that the mechanism actually uses a micro switch for end-of-stroke detection. Identify the switch and its actuator in the equipment documentation rather than inferring their purpose from location or appearance. A micro switch is an electromechanical component; it does not, by itself, define the robot’s end-of-stroke response. That response depends on the actual mechanism, circuit, and control design.

Use the robot-specific maintenance procedure and the switch’s documentation to determine what inspection and verification are permitted. Do not assume a universal contact arrangement, terminal layout, service interval, adjustment dimension, or test state. Maintenance should distinguish three questions: Does the mechanism reach and engage the actuator as documented? Does the isolated switch show the documented electrical state under the specified conditions? Does the installed robot respond as its authorized procedure specifies? Evidence for one question does not answer the others.

If the robot fails to detect an end-of-stroke event, treat that as a symptom, not a diagnosis. Possible areas for investigation include mechanical engagement, the switch itself, terminals or wiring, and the control system. Use the specified checks to narrow the fault path; do not bypass the switch or infer that a particular control response should occur.

## Before inspecting a collaborative robot end of stroke micro switch

Before accessing the mechanism or switch, follow the robot’s documented safe-stop and isolation process. The relevant procedure must account for the equipment and task. Do not assume that a robot’s stopped state alone establishes that its electrical circuit is isolated. If the required isolation steps, authorization, or circuit implications are unclear, stop and use the robot’s authorized service process.

Identify the actual switch, actuator, linkage, terminals, and applicable documentation. Confirm the switch identity from reliable equipment records or markings as directed by the service procedure. Do not assign terminal functions based on their physical position, a generic diagram, or the appearance of another switch. Use the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. The applicable requirements for deenergization and qualification remain in force.

Separate switch access from work on robot controls or safety-related circuits. This article does not provide a procedure for programming, repairing, or bypassing those systems. If the inspection would require work beyond the documented authorization or isolation boundary, defer it to qualified, authorized personnel.

## Inspect the micro switch and its mechanical engagement

With access established under the required procedure, inspect the switch and the surrounding mechanism for visible damage, loose mounting, obstruction, or other conditions that could interfere with engagement. Observe the actuator and linkage without assuming that a particular amount of travel, force, or clearance is acceptable. Compare the mechanism only with the equipment’s documented geometry and permitted movement; do not invent an adjustment value.

Consider the mechanical path as well as the switch body. The robot mechanism may fail to reach the switch, or a linkage may not engage it consistently, even if the switch itself is intact. Conversely, the mechanism may appear to reach the actuator while the switch has an internal condition that requires separate verification. Record what is directly observable and keep those observations distinct from conclusions about electrical or control performance.

A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove that the contacts changed state, that the terminals are correctly identified, or that the robot received the expected signal. Do not treat audible or tactile feedback as a substitute for the documented verification method. If actuator movement or engagement differs from the equipment documentation, use the robot-specific service procedure to determine the next step rather than making an undocumented adjustment.

## Verify the switch state using documented conditions

Perform electrical testing only if the applicable procedure permits it and the required isolation has been established. Identify the terminals using the exact switch documentation and equipment connection information. Do not assume that terminal positions or labels have a universal meaning, or that a contact arrangement documented for another component applies here.

If the procedure calls for an isolated continuity check, isolate the switch as directed and test only at the specified actuator states. Compare the reading with the documented contact arrangement and reference condition for that switch. A result is meaningful only in relation to the test setup and the state being checked; an unexplained reading, or a reading taken at an unspecified actuator position, cannot establish whether the switch meets its documented behavior.

An isolated continuity result is evidence about the switch under the tested conditions only. It does not verify installed wiring, terminal connections in the robot, control logic, or safe operation. Likewise, a switch that changes state when tested does not establish that the mechanism engages it at the correct point during robot motion. Keep the electrical result, mechanical observation, and equipment-level behavior as separate records.

## Use symptoms to decide what needs further diagnosis

Intermittent or absent end-of-stroke detection can arise from several paths: inconsistent mechanical engagement, a switch condition, terminal or wiring faults, or a control-system issue. The symptom alone does not identify which one applies. Compare observations with the authorized diagnostic procedure. For example, inconsistent physical engagement points toward further mechanism-focused assessment; a switch that moves but does not show the documented isolated state points toward switch-focused assessment. Neither observation alone establishes the cause of an installed-system failure.

If the isolated switch check matches its documented states but the robot still does not behave as expected, do not conclude that the complete assembly is good. The result leaves installed wiring, control interpretation, and other equipment behavior unresolved. If the switch does not meet its documented isolated-state check, do not infer that replacing it will resolve every equipment-level symptom; verify the applicable service path and preserve the distinction between component and system findings.

Do not bypass the switch to test a theory or to restore operation. Unresolved faults involving robot controls or safety-related behavior require the robot’s authorized service process. Escalate when observations conflict, when the documented test conditions cannot be established, or when interpreting the result would require work outside the authorized boundary.

## When to stop and what maintenance records should capture

Follow only the documented requirements for cleaning, adjustment, replacement, and periodic inspection. No universal maintenance interval or repair method can be inferred for all collaborative robots or micro switches. If the equipment documentation does not specify a permitted cleaning or adjustment method, do not improvise one. If a required test, isolation step, or result interpretation is unclear, stop and seek qualified, authorized assessment.

Record the switch and mechanism inspected, the observed actuator and linkage condition, and any visible obstruction or damage. For an electrical check, capture the test setup, isolation condition as required by the procedure, terminals tested, actuator states, and observed results. Also record equipment-level behavior separately, including anything that remains unresolved. This keeps component evidence from being mistaken for proof of correct robot wiring, control behavior, or safe operation.

The maintenance decision should follow the documented evidence: continue only within the approved procedure, route a component finding through the specified service path, and escalate unresolved mechanism or control issues. Do not claim a complete system diagnosis from a click, a visual check, or an isolated continuity reading alone.
