For a painting robot guard interlock, painting robot guard interlock micro switch adjustment should be based on the equipment’s documented mechanical arrangement and permitted settings. It is not a universal procedure. First establish that the guard design actually uses a micro switch; some interlocks may use another arrangement. Then trace how guard movement reaches the switch actuator and compare the observed movement with the specified limits. A change at the switch is only one part of the assessment. It does not, by itself, show that the installed interlock performs its documented safety function.

## What painting robot guard interlock micro switch adjustment can and cannot change

Adjustment concerns the documented relationship among the guard, any linkage, and the micro switch actuator. It can address a mechanical condition only when the equipment documentation identifies an allowed adjustment and method. It cannot establish the correct interlock logic, repair an unrelated circuit fault, or prove that the whole system provides its intended response.

A micro switch is a component that can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement. A miniature snap-action switch can be used for position or limit detection, but that general use does not establish that a particular painting robot guard uses one. Confirm the component and its role from the equipment-specific documentation rather than assuming that every guard interlock has the same design.

The guard and interlock system are not synonyms for the switch. Guard movement may operate a hinge, linkage, or other mechanical part before reaching the actuator. The switch event then interacts with the installed circuit and control system. The effect of that event depends on the documented design. Do not infer a contact arrangement, normal state, or system response from the presence of a switch alone.

Before adjustment, establish which guard states and outcomes the documentation specifies, and identify the approved procedure for assessing them. If the documentation does not identify the switch, its mechanical relationship, or permitted adjustment, the evidence is not enough to choose a setting. Do not substitute a generic gap, travel, or force value.

## Trace guard movement to the micro switch actuator

Follow the mechanical path in order: guard movement, any hinge or linkage that transmits it, the point where that mechanism meets the actuator, and the actuator movement itself. Use the equipment documentation to identify the intended contact point and motion. The actuator is the part moved by the external mechanism; movement of the guard does not automatically mean that the actuator receives the required movement.

Treat possible mechanical conditions separately. Misalignment concerns whether the moving mechanism reaches the documented contact point. An obstruction may interrupt or change the path. Insufficient actuator movement means the mechanism may not produce the specified switch event. Sustained loading is a different concern: the mechanism may continue to press on the actuator beyond the movement allowed by the design. These are possible observations to investigate, not diagnoses that can be made from a symptom alone.

Compare the observed motion with the equipment’s specified arrangement and limits. Do not infer required pretravel, overtravel, operating force, clearance, or a mounting dimension when the documentation does not provide it. A switch that appears to move is not necessarily operating within its permitted range, and a visible gap or apparent contact point is not a substitute for the stated adjustment criteria.

If the guard motion and actuator movement do not correspond to the documented path, record which part of the chain differs. That observation can help distinguish a linkage or alignment concern from a switch or circuit concern, but it does not prove the cause. Keep the switch, its actuator, and the surrounding mechanism distinct while assessing what is actually observed.

## Use the documented adjustment limits, not a generic setting

Locate the equipment-specific adjustment instructions before changing the mounting position or linkage. Establish the stated reference position, adjustment method, and permitted limits from that documentation. The required setting may depend on the particular guard and interlock design; no universal gap, travel, force, or adjustment direction is established here.

Do not move the switch or linkage beyond documented limits, or alter a part based on an assumed setting. If the procedure is missing, ambiguous, or does not cover the observed condition, stop rather than improvising. An adjustment that changes when the actuator is reached may also change how it is loaded during guard movement, so judge it against the documented arrangement, not merely whether a switch event can be produced.

Work involving the equipment or its circuit may present hazards. Follow the equipment’s prescribed safe procedure and have work performed by qualified personnel when the procedure or circuit presents a hazard. If a safe, equipment-specific procedure is not available, refer the matter to qualified personnel. This is not a general robot-cell safety procedure, and it does not authorize bypassing or defeating the guard interlock.

A durability result is meaningful only within its stated test scope. For example, a durability result under MIL-PRF-8805K qualification and conformance tests applies only with its specified electrical load, operating sequence, cycle count, and environmental or test conditions. Such a result does not supply an adjustment setting or establish suitability for a particular installation. Likewise, an IP code states defined enclosure-protection characteristics; by itself, it does not establish electrical rating, chemical compatibility, corrosion resistance, vibration endurance, condensation performance, or complete application suitability.

## Verify switch actuation separately from interlock function

Use the equipment’s documented, safe verification procedure and identify the guard states being assessed. Check the specified guard-open and guard-closed conditions as directed. Keep the conditions attached to any measurement: a continuity observation, for example, is meaningful only for the isolated switch and defined actuator state specified by the procedure. Do not treat an isolated reading as a test of the installed circuit.

A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, correct terminal identification, correct wiring, or suitable operation under the installed conditions. A measured contact-state change can establish that an electrical change was observed under the stated test conditions. It still does not establish how the complete interlock circuit responds.

Verify the complete interlock response only through the equipment’s prescribed functional test. Follow the specified procedure and expected outcomes; do not infer the control logic or assume what the system should do from the switch movement alone. If the documented system-level test cannot be performed or its result is unclear, the switch observation cannot close that gap. Refer the unresolved verification to qualified personnel.

Keep conclusions proportional to the test. A component-level observation addresses the switch under its test conditions. A system-level functional test addresses the documented interlock response under its prescribed conditions. Neither should be represented as proving more than was actually checked.

## When adjustment is not the demonstrated cause

If the switch does not change state as specified, consider distinct possibilities rather than concluding that the switch is misaligned. The mechanical input may not reach the actuator as documented; the internal contacts may not change state; terminal identification may be uncertain; wiring may be incorrect or faulty; or the cause may lie elsewhere in the equipment. Compare each observation with the applicable documentation and test conditions. A single symptom does not distinguish these causes.

If the switch state changes under the specified component-level check but the installed interlock response is wrong, the issue may be in the wiring, circuit, control system, or another part of the equipment. The observed switch event is not proof that the complete system is functioning as intended. Use the prescribed functional test and fault-isolation process rather than repeating a mechanical adjustment without evidence that the mechanical relationship is the problem.

Do not bypass or defeat the guard interlock as a diagnostic shortcut. Stop and escalate when the procedure, circuit, or test conditions are hazardous, when documentation is unavailable, or when the cause remains unresolved. The practical decision boundary for painting robot guard interlock micro switch adjustment is therefore clear: adjust only the documented mechanical relationship within stated limits, and assess the switch event separately from the complete system’s verified response.
