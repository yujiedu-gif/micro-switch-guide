# Replacing and verifying a travel-limit switch in an antenna rotator

An **antenna rotator travel limit micro switch replacement** is not just a matter of transferring wires to a similar-looking part. The switch must match the electrical contact arrangement and the mechanical way the rotator’s trip mechanism operates it. First determine whether the switch has actually failed; a misadjusted cam, damaged wiring, or control fault can produce similar symptoms. Rotator limit mechanisms and wiring arrangements vary, and not every rotator uses the same switch.

The procedure below focuses on identifying the fault, preserving the original setup, and checking both configured travel endpoints. It does not establish a replacement part for a particular rotator. Device-specific travel limits and other component requirements must come from the applicable specification, not from assumptions about the component category.

## Antenna rotator travel limit micro switch replacement: what the switch does

In designs that use a travel-limit switch, the switch changes circuit state at a configured endpoint. Depending on the design, that change interrupts motion or signals that the endpoint has been reached. The exact circuit behavior, trip mechanism, and wiring arrangement are design-specific, so do not assume that all rotators use the same switch or limit logic.

A switch operates through contact movement driven by its actuator. The rotator’s cam or other trip mechanism must engage that actuator in a way that produces the intended state change. If the cam misses the actuator, or the switch is positioned incorrectly, a functional switch may fail to stop motion at the intended point. Conversely, a switch that does not change state reliably may be defective even when its mechanical position appears correct.

Treat the electrical and mechanical interfaces as separate checks. Contact symbols or a schematic address which terminals connect in each state. Mounting and actuator geometry address whether the mechanism can operate the switch at the intended point. Appearance alone establishes neither. The switch’s device-specific travel limits are requirements of its applicable specification sheet and must not be inferred from the component category.

## Confirm the micro switch is the failed part

Before removing anything, identify the observed failure. A repeatable failure to stop at one endpoint, intermittent operation, or visible switch damage can point toward a switch problem, but none of these symptoms alone proves the switch is the cause. A cam that has shifted or no longer reaches the actuator can resemble a failed switch. Damaged wiring, a loose terminal, or a control fault can also affect the limit function.

Inspect the switch, its terminals and wiring, and the cam or other trip mechanism. Note whether the mechanism visibly operates the actuator and whether the symptom occurs consistently at one endpoint or intermittently. Observe available controller indications as part of the fault check, but do not treat an indication alone as proof of which component failed.

Where a suitable, safe continuity check is used to identify contacts, distinguish a contact-state problem from a mechanical trip problem. Terminal function must come from the device markings or exact schematic, not wire color. Avoid assuming that a switch is defective simply because the rotator travels too far: adjustment, circuit, and control causes remain possible. If the cause is not clear, resolve that uncertainty before replacement rather than changing multiple parts at once.

## Record the original switch and its connections

Before access, disconnect power and secure the equipment against movement. For workplace electrical wiring in the United States, the cited rule generally requires exposed live parts to be deenergized before work. A qualified person must verify the deenergized condition with test equipment, and work on energized parts is limited to qualified persons. Verify all applicable requirements for the work and jurisdiction.

Once access is safe, photograph or label each wire before disconnecting it. Record the terminal markings and which wire is attached to each terminal. If markings are unclear, use the exact device schematic rather than inferring terminal function from wire color. This record helps preserve the original circuit arrangement; it does not establish that the existing wiring is correct if a separate wiring fault is found.

Also note the switch orientation, mounting position, actuator style, and the point at which the cam or trip mechanism operates the actuator. Record how the actuator is approached and where the trip occurs in relation to the mechanism. These observations provide a reference for installation and adjustment. They do not supply a specification-sheet travel value or a universal endpoint setting.

## Match electrical and mechanical compatibility

Check electrical contacts and mechanical actuation independently. For generic changeover terminology, COM means common terminal, NC means normally closed terminal, and NO means normally open terminal. Verify those meanings against the exact device schematic and terminal markings. Do not identify contacts by wire color, physical position, or resemblance to another switch. If contact configuration cannot be established, compatibility remains unresolved.

Then compare the mounting arrangement and actuator geometry with the original setup. The actuator must be reached and moved by the rotator’s trip mechanism in the intended direction and at the intended point. Consider whether the replacement’s actuator reach and direction allow that engagement without binding. Also compare the operating force and travel requirements with the applicable device information and the mechanism. Do not infer numerical travel limits from the fact that a component is a micro switch.

Matching only the contact configuration is insufficient if the cam cannot operate the actuator correctly. Matching only the actuator shape is insufficient if the contacts do not provide the required circuit arrangement. A similar-looking component is not evidence of either kind of compatibility. If the electrical configuration, mounting, actuation, or relevant specification cannot be confirmed, do not assume the replacement fits the rotator.

## Replace and adjust the travel-limit micro switch

Use the recorded wire map and switch orientation to guide removal and installation. Transfer each wire to its corresponding contact only after confirming terminal functions against the markings or exact schematic. Secure the switch in the recorded orientation, while ensuring the cam or trip mechanism can reach the actuator without forcing it. Do not bend or push the actuator as a substitute for correcting an alignment problem.

Adjust the switch or trip mechanism so the intended state change occurs before the mechanism reaches a hard stop. The correct adjustment is specific to the rotator design; this procedure does not set a universal trip distance or travel value. Preserve the limit function. Do not bridge, bypass, or defeat the switch to make the rotator operate.

Before testing, review the wire placement, mounting, and actuator engagement. Confirm that the mechanism can operate the actuator through its intended movement without binding. If the replacement cannot be mounted or actuated as required, stop and recheck compatibility rather than forcing the part into position. A correct-looking installation is not yet verified until the endpoint checks confirm reliable operation.

## Verify both endpoints and return to service

Keep movement controlled and observe the mechanism during testing. Check each direction separately, with attention to the configured travel endpoint and whether the switch interrupts travel reliably. Both endpoints matter: a successful check in one direction does not establish that the opposite limit is adjusted or wired correctly. The test should confirm the switch’s operation in the installed circuit, not merely that its actuator can be moved by hand.

Stop if either endpoint fails to stop travel reliably, if the switch does not change state as expected, or if the actuator binds or the mechanism reaches a hard stop first. Recheck wire placement, contact identification, switch alignment, and cam engagement. If those checks do not resolve the issue, consider the wiring or control behavior as an alternative fault path. Do not resume normal operation on the assumption that one working endpoint is sufficient.

Return to normal operation only after both configured endpoints have been checked and the limit function is reliable. If the result is uncertain, leave the fault unresolved and obtain appropriate technical assessment rather than bypassing the limit. The goal of **antenna rotator travel limit micro switch replacement** is not simply to install a component, but to preserve the intended electrical and mechanical trip behavior at both ends of travel.
