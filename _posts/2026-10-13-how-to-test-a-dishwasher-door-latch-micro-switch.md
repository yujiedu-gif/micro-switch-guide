A meaningful continuity check tests the switch itself, not the dishwasher circuit: isolate the switch from circuit paths, identify its documented terminals, and compare readings in defined actuator states with the specified contact arrangement. How to test a dishwasher door latch micro switch depends on whether that particular latch design uses a micro switch and on the switch’s documentation; there is no universal terminal layout or assumed contact state.

## Direct answer: how to test a dishwasher door latch micro switch

First establish that the latch design actually contains a micro switch. If it does, a bounded continuity check requires the switch to be electrically isolated from the dishwasher wiring, the terminals to be identified from documentation, and the actuator state to be defined for each reading. Compare the observed readings with the documented contact arrangement and reference condition. Do not infer terminal identity or expected continuity from a generic latch description or physical layout.

An in-circuit measurement is not equivalent to an isolated-switch check. Other circuit paths can affect what a meter indicates, so an in-circuit reading does not establish the switch’s condition. Likewise, a switch-only result addresses only the tested contacts under the stated conditions. It does not establish that the latch, wiring, control logic, or dishwasher as a whole functions correctly.

## Before testing: isolate the dishwasher and identify the switch

Do not begin access or measurement until the dishwasher has been safely isolated from power. The isolation method, access requirements, and applicable qualifications depend on the appliance and its documentation; this article does not supply model-specific service instructions. If safe, verified isolation or access is outside your competence, leave the work to a qualified person. Do not treat a meter reading across an installed circuit as proof that the appliance is safe to work on.

Confirm from documentation that this latch design uses a micro switch. Dishwashers and latch designs are not interchangeable descriptions of one universal arrangement. Identify the exact switch’s documented terminals and contact arrangement before connecting a meter. Use the applicable connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load for the exact switch and equipment. Do not guess from terminal position, wire color, a generic diagram, or a description of another dishwasher.

The expected contact behavior must also be documented for the relevant actuator reference state. If the terminal identification, contact arrangement, or reference condition cannot be established, an observed reading cannot be interpreted reliably. Do not assign COM, NO, or NC labels unless those terminals are actually identified for the switch. This check concerns continuity interpretation only; it does not determine whether the switch is suitable for a particular electrical load.

## Check the latch actuator without treating a click as proof

Where the design permits observation, note whether latch movement physically reaches and releases the micro switch actuator. The actuator is the part moved by an external mechanism. A latch that fails to reach it, or does not release it as expected, presents a mechanical actuation question distinct from the electrical behavior of the switch contacts. Alignment, travel, or linkage concerns cannot be settled by a contact reading alone.

A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, correct terminal identification, correct wiring, or operation of the dishwasher circuit. Conversely, lack of an audible click by itself does not identify the cause. The relevant distinction is whether the mechanism moves the switch actuator through the states specified for that design, and whether the isolated contacts behave as documented in those states.

Do not force the latch or infer a particular normal position or contact state from the fact that it is a dishwasher door latch. If the actuator cannot be observed or its reference states are unknown, record that limitation rather than treating an assumed movement as a defined test condition.

## Measure continuity in defined actuator states

Only after safe isolation and identification, and with the switch isolated from circuit paths, use an appropriate continuity measurement across the documented terminals. Take readings in the specified released and actuated states, where those states are defined for the particular switch. Compare each reading with the documented contact arrangement and reference condition. The meter result has meaning only in relation to the terminals, actuator state, isolation condition, and expected behavior used for the check.

Do not assume which terminal pair should conduct when the actuator is released or actuated. Do not transfer a state description from a different switch or latch. If documentation does not identify the terminals or expected states, there is no supported basis here for declaring a reading normal or abnormal. A measured contact-resistance requirement, if applicable, must be interpreted using the method, connection points, and limit specified by the applicable specification and test procedure; a generic continuity indication is not a substitute for those requirements.

Keep the observations distinct: record which documented terminal pair was measured, whether the actuator was in the defined released or actuated state, and whether the switch was isolated from circuit paths. Do not use a result obtained in one condition as evidence for another. If the measurement setup or state is uncertain, resolve that uncertainty before drawing a conclusion about contact behavior.

## What the result can and cannot establish

If the isolated contacts change state consistently with the documented arrangement under the tested conditions, that supports only the conclusion that those contacts responded during this check. It does not validate latch alignment or full mechanical travel, the wiring, other dishwasher components, control logic, or dishwasher operation. It also does not establish that the installed circuit is safe.

If there is no change or a reading differs from the documented expectation, several explanations remain possible: the contacts may not be responding, the actuator may not have reached the defined state, the terminal pair may have been misidentified, or the test setup may be affecting the result. Separate those possibilities before concluding that the switch itself has failed. A switch-only reading cannot diagnose a control-board, wiring, wash-cycle, or other appliance fault.

The decision boundary is therefore narrow: interpret continuity only for an identified, isolated switch in a defined actuator state and against its documented contact arrangement. Treat latch actuation, installed wiring, and dishwasher behavior as separate questions. Neither a click nor a single continuity result proves that the complete latch or dishwasher is functional.
