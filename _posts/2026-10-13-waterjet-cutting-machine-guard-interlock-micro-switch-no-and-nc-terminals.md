A waterjet cutting machine guard interlock micro switch NO and NC terminals describe contact states at a defined reference condition; they do not, by themselves, describe what the machine will do when a guard moves. NO and NC are labels for contacts on the particular switch, while the interlock and control system determine how a contact change is interpreted. Confirm the terminal arrangement and reference condition from the exact switch documentation before drawing conclusions.

The mechanical and electrical chain also has distinct steps. A guard or linkage may move a micro switch actuator, causing a contact state to change. The machine’s wiring and control design then determine whether that change enables, inhibits, signals, or otherwise affects operation. Not every waterjet cutting machine uses a micro switch in its guard interlock.

## What NO and NC mean on a micro switch

A generic changeover contact may have terminals marked common, normally closed (NC), and normally open (NO). The words “normally closed” and “normally open” identify contact states at the reference condition specified for the switch. They should not be read as unconditional descriptions of current flow, an energized circuit, or the machine’s guarded or unguarded state.

In a documented changeover arrangement, the common terminal is the shared contact connection, and the NO and NC labels distinguish the two contact paths at that reference condition. The exact switch marking or schematic governs, however. Do not assume that every micro switch has all three terminals, or infer terminal identity from physical position or appearance. Confirm the device’s diagram and markings.

The reference condition matters because the actuator can move from that condition and change the contact arrangement. A terminal label alone does not say which actuator position is present on an installed machine. Nor does it establish whether the guard is open or closed, whether a circuit is energized, or whether the control system regards a condition as safe.

These distinctions keep the component description narrow: NO and NC describe contact states in relation to the specified switch reference condition. They do not define the interlock’s complete logic. For the actual terminal arrangement, use documentation for the exact switch and equipment rather than a generic terminal-layout assumption.

## How a waterjet cutting machine guard may actuate a micro switch

If a particular machine uses a micro switch as part of a guard interlock, the mechanical sequence may be: guard movement, movement of a linkage or other mechanism, actuator movement, and a resulting contact change. Each link in that sequence depends on the machine’s actual arrangement. The presence of a guard does not establish that a micro switch is used, or show how it is mounted or actuated.

The switch is one component in that possible chain. The guard and any linkage supply mechanical input; the switch responds through its actuator and contacts. The control system receives or otherwise uses the resulting electrical condition according to its wiring and design. These are separate functions, and a change at the switch does not by itself specify the system response.

For example, identifying a contact as NO does not reveal which guard position corresponds to the documented reference condition, or whether the contact changes when the guard moves in a particular direction. Those details require the switch and machine documentation. Do not generalize a mechanical arrangement from one waterjet machine to another.

The useful question is therefore not merely “Which terminal is NO?” It is also whether the exact switch is the component in the machine’s interlock, what reference condition its documentation specifies, and how the machine’s circuit uses the contact. Without those details, the mechanical chain and its effect remain unresolved.

## How to verify terminal identity and contact state safely

Begin with the exact switch documentation and the machine documentation. Use the switch’s markings or schematic to identify its terminals and the specified reference condition. Use the machine’s connection diagram and circuit information to understand how the component is incorporated. Do not identify terminals by physical layout alone or treat a generic diagram as proof of the installed arrangement.

An isolated contact check, when performed using an applicable procedure and measurement setup, concerns the switch itself in a defined actuator state. Interpret the reading only against the documented terminal arrangement and reference condition. The state being checked and the method used matter; an unexplained reading cannot establish which contact should be closed or open in a particular machine position.

An isolated reading does not show how the installed interlock circuit behaves. It cannot establish the machine’s control response or prove that the interlock is safe. Machine-level checks require the applicable approved procedure and qualified personnel. Follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements; do not improvise tests or wiring changes from terminal labels alone.

Keep the scope of each observation explicit: a terminal marking identifies a documented connection, while an isolated measurement concerns a switch under a defined condition. Neither, by itself, verifies the complete guard, wiring, control logic, or machine response. If the documentation does not establish the relevant reference condition or terminal identity, treat it as unknown rather than filling the gap by inference.

## What to conclude when terminal behavior and machine behavior differ

A discrepancy is not proof that the switch has failed, nor does it prove that the machine’s interlock is functioning correctly.

Compare observations with the exact switch and equipment documentation, including the relevant reference condition and circuit diagram.
