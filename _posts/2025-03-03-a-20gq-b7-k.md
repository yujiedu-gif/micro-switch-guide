# Verifying an Unfamiliar Component Marking

The marking **a 20gq b7 k** should be treated as an unverified identifier string unless exact, applicable documentation establishes what it identifies. A printed or observed code is evidence of the characters present, but it does not by itself establish a manufacturer, product family, component type, specification, or intended application. A similar-looking string is not enough to fill in those gaps.

A careful check keeps the observed marking separate from any interpretation. Record the characters as seen, note where they appear, and look for documentation that matches the complete identifier and relevant variant. Until that evidence is available, ratings, terminal functions, compatibility, manufacturer, and application remain unresolved. The same caution applies to claims about travel, durability, or other performance: such claims require documentation that establishes the relevant device and the conditions covered.

## What does a 20gq b7 k identify?

Keep the observation and the conclusion distinct. “The marking reads a 20gq b7 k” reports what was observed. “This is a particular switch” makes an identity claim that needs support. A code may appear on a component, a label, packaging, or a document, and those contexts do not necessarily establish the same thing. The location of the marking is useful context, not a substitute for an exact identification source.

In the absence of an exact, applicable match, these details remain unresolved:

- The manufacturer or product family.
- Whether the marked item is a micro switch or another kind of component.
- Electrical ratings, switching characteristics, and load type.
- Terminal arrangement and the function of any individual terminal.

## How to verify the a 20gq b7 k marking

Use a sequence that preserves what is known and makes gaps visible:

1. **Transcribe the marking exactly.** Preserve the characters, spacing, and case as observed. Do not silently correct a character, add punctuation, or substitute a similar-looking code. If part of the marking is unclear, record that uncertainty rather than guessing.
2. **Record its context.** Note where the string appears, such as on the component or on an associated label or document. Record any other visible markings separately. Context can help distinguish observations, but it does not prove identity on its own.
3. **Seek an exact match.** Compare the complete string with documentation that explicitly matches it and, where relevant, the marked variant. A partial match, similar code, or general description is not enough to establish that the documentation applies to the observed item.
4. **Check scope and conditions.** Read what the documentation actually covers. Confirm that a specification refers to the identified device or variant and includes the conditions relevant to the claim. Do not detach a rating or test result from its stated scope.
5. **Keep unsupported fields open.** If the source does not establish a rating, terminal function, compatibility, or application, leave that point unresolved. Do not fill it in by analogy with a familiar code or component category.

This process separates three kinds of information: the observed characters, the evidence linking those characters to an identity, and the specifications supported for that identity. A source may support one without supporting the others. For example, a document that repeats the marking without clearly identifying the relevant variant may not establish which ratings or conditions apply.

For terminal interpretation, use exact device documentation rather than a generic diagram or assumed convention. A generic changeover contact may be described with common, normally closed, and normally open terminals, but the exact device marking or schematic controls. If the marking cannot be matched to documentation that establishes the terminals, their functions remain unresolved.
