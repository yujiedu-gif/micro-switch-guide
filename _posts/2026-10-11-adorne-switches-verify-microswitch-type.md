A phrase used to describe a switch does not, by itself, establish the switch’s technical category. **adorne switches** should be treated as a search phrase unless exact documentation identifies the particular item and its switching mechanism. The words alone do not show that a switch is a microswitch, nor do they establish its ratings, terminals, dimensions, compatibility, or intended application.

To verify whether a specific switch is a microswitch, start with documentation that applies to that exact item and configuration. Look for an explicit switch-type or mechanism description, then check that the equipment documentation identifies the same item. If those records do not resolve the classification, leave it unresolved rather than inferring it from a label, appearance, or search result.

## What does “adorne switches” establish about switch type?

On its own, the phrase establishes no particular switch type. It may be a search term, a label, or wording used in a description, but those uses are not a technical classification. The phrase does not prove that the item is a microswitch, and it does not identify a specific product or establish any electrical or mechanical specification.

A switch category should be established by applicable technical documentation, not by how a phrase sounds or where it appears. A listing, informal description, or isolated label may not supply enough information to distinguish a microswitch from a switch classified in another way. Without authoritative information tied to the exact item, do not convert the phrase into a product identity or a claim about the switch’s construction.

A microswitch can be described using the definition in MIL-PRF-8805K, 6.5.21: snap action is contact action in which the speed of the moving contacts is relatively independent of the speed of the actuating mechanism. This definition explains snap action; it does not classify an unidentified switch. A reader would still need documentation showing that the particular switch has that mechanism or is explicitly identified as a microswitch. A definition cannot substitute for evidence about an item.

This distinction matters because a search phrase and a technical description answer different questions. The phrase can help someone find information, but it cannot establish what mechanism is inside a switch. Likewise, a visual impression or a general resemblance to another switch does not establish the category. Keep the item’s type unresolved until documentation specifically supports it.

## adorne switches: How to verify whether a switch is a microswitch

Begin by identifying the exact item under review, including any relevant variant or configuration shown in the equipment records. Then find documentation that clearly applies to that item. The evidence should explicitly identify the switch category or describe its mechanism. A statement that only repeats a search phrase or general label does not answer whether the mechanism is snap action.

Next, read the technical description in context. Check that it belongs to the item being assessed, rather than to a different switch, an accessory, or a broader group with multiple configurations. If the documentation uses the term “microswitch,” determine whether it is identifying the item’s switch type or merely repeating informal wording. If it describes a mechanism instead, compare that description with the relevant definition. The snap-action definition above can clarify terminology, but it cannot verify an undocumented item.

Confirm that the equipment documentation refers to the same switch and configuration. A description that appears relevant but cannot be matched to the item is not enough to establish identity. Where documentation is incomplete, conflicting, or too general to connect the mechanism to the exact item, the careful conclusion is that the type has not been verified.

A useful verification sequence is:

1. Record the exact item and configuration stated in the equipment documentation.
2. Locate technical documentation that applies to that item, rather than relying on the phrase alone.
3. Look for an explicit switch-category identification or a mechanism description that can be evaluated against a stated definition.
4. Check that the evidence and equipment records refer to the same item and configuration.
5. If the records do not establish the category, keep the classification unresolved.

This sequence verifies only the question of switch type. It does not establish whether the item can be used in a particular circuit or equipment position. Those questions require separate evidence and are outside the scope of identifying a switch category.

## Which technical details require separate evidence?

Even if documentation establishes that an item is a microswitch, that classification does not establish its electrical or mechanical particulars. Voltage, current, load type, terminal arrangement, dimensions, certification, and compatibility each require evidence applicable to the exact item and configuration. None can be inferred from the phrase “adorne switches.” Do not treat the general category as a substitute for an item-specific specification.

Similarly, a description of a mechanism does not verify certification, operating limits, or application. If a needed detail is absent, do not fill the gap with a general assumption based on the phrase, category, or appearance.

Keeping these questions separate prevents a narrow identification claim from being mistaken for a complete product assessment. Evidence that verifies switch type supports only that classification. It does not silently supply ratings, installation details, or approval for a particular use.
