A **dryer lint filter interlock micro switch not working** is one possible explanation for a dryer symptom, not a diagnosis by itself. This troubleshooting path applies only to dryers whose service information documents a micro switch in the lint-filter interlock. Other dryers may use a different sensing arrangement or no switch at this location.

Trace the cause in order: filter seating, mechanical engagement, switch movement, documented contact behavior, wiring, and then other appliance circuitry. Keep each observation tied to what it can establish. A symptom at the dryer does not prove the switch failed, and an isolated switch check does not establish that the complete interlock or appliance works.

## What “dryer lint filter interlock micro switch not working” can and cannot mean

A micro switch is a miniature electromechanical switch that can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement. Such switches are commonly used for position or limit detection. That general description does not establish that a particular dryer uses one to detect its lint filter. Confirm the arrangement in the documentation for the specific appliance before applying this sequence.

A no-start condition, warning, or other reported symptom may be consistent with a problem in an interlock path, but it does not identify the failed part. The cause might be that the filter is not seated as intended, the mechanism does not reach the switch actuator, the switch contacts do not behave as documented, or a connection or other part of the appliance is involved. The symptom alone cannot distinguish among these possibilities.

The switch's contact change also does not have a universal effect on dryer operation. The appliance's circuit determines how that change is interpreted. Do not assume a particular normal state, terminal arrangement, warning response, or start behavior from the presence of a switch. Use the appliance and switch documentation for the exact design. If that documentation does not establish the interlock arrangement or expected behavior, the diagnosis remains unresolved.

## Trace filter seating to the micro switch actuator

Begin with the mechanical input path, but only within the access and inspection limits in applicable service guidance. Determine whether the filter is seated as the appliance documentation requires. If the documented design has a tab, lever, or linkage that operates a micro switch, observe whether the intended part reaches and moves the actuator as designed. Do not assume a linkage shape or direction of travel based on another dryer.

An observable obstruction, misalignment, damaged part, or lack of movement points to a mechanical issue to investigate before concluding that the switch has an internal contact fault. Conversely, apparent movement is only evidence about the mechanical path that was observed. It does not confirm that the contacts changed state or that the appliance circuit responded correctly.

Keep the distinction between input and response clear: the filter and its mechanism provide movement; the actuator receives that movement; the switch may change its electrical contact state; and the appliance circuit interprets the change. A problem at one stage can resemble a problem at another when viewed only through the dryer symptom. If the mechanism cannot be inspected safely or its intended motion is not documented, stop rather than infer how it should operate.

## Check the micro switch only in a documented, isolated state

A continuity check can provide information about an isolated switch only when the applicable service instructions permit safe isolation and the relevant switch documentation identifies the terminals and expected contact behavior for a defined actuator state. Establish those details from the exact switch and appliance information. Do not infer terminal identity from physical position, assume COM, NO, or NC labels, or guess which state should be open or closed.

Keep the actuator state explicit when interpreting a reading. The question is whether the isolated switch's measured behavior matches the documented expectation for that state and terminal arrangement. If either the expected behavior or the required isolation condition is unavailable, the result cannot be interpreted reliably from a generic switch description. Contact resistance, when relevant, is a measured switch requirement whose method, connection points, and limit must come from the applicable specification and test procedure; a generic resistance threshold should not be substituted.

A reading that appears consistent with the documented isolated behavior does not prove that the installed wiring, interlock logic, or dryer is functioning. A reading that appears inconsistent may be useful evidence about the isolated switch under the conditions tested, but does not by itself identify why the appliance showed its symptom. Do not test energized circuitry or extend a component-level result into a whole-appliance conclusion.

## Separate switch faults from wiring and other dryer faults

After considering filter seating and actuator engagement, compare any permitted isolated switch result with the documented contact behavior. Then keep wiring and the rest of the appliance as separate possible causes. A connector or wire may affect the installed circuit even if the isolated switch behaves as documented. Control circuitry or another appliance condition may also be involved. The available evidence here does not specify how to test those parts or identify a particular dryer circuit.

Do not treat an installed-circuit symptom as confirmation of a failed micro switch. The symptom could arise elsewhere in the path, and the actual circuit determines what the contact change means. Likewise, do not treat a switch result as proof that the interlock circuit or dryer is sound. Each result answers only the question asked under the documented test conditions.

For terminal identification, circuit function, and permissible load, rely on the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. A generic physical-layout inference is not enough. If the design-specific service information does not explain how to distinguish a switch issue from wiring or control-circuit issues, use qualified service rather than improvising checks.

## When to stop and seek qualified service

Stop if you cannot establish safe de-energizing and access from applicable service guidance, identify the switch and terminals from documentation, or determine the expected contact behavior for a defined state. Also stop when the next step would require interpreting an undocumented circuit or testing energized circuitry. The available guidance does not support inventing an access procedure or repair method for an unspecified dryer.

Do not bypass or defeat the interlock, and do not infer that changing or replacing a switch is appropriate from the symptom alone. A sound conclusion requires evidence about the particular dryer's documented design and the relevant mechanical and electrical states. If those facts cannot be established within safe, documented limits, qualified service is the appropriate boundary.

In short, diagnose the path rather than assigning the symptom to one component: verify documented filter seating and actuator engagement, interpret an isolated switch check only against its specified terminals and state, and keep wiring and other appliance faults in view. A **dryer lint filter interlock micro switch not working** remains a possibility until the evidence distinguishes it from the other causes.
