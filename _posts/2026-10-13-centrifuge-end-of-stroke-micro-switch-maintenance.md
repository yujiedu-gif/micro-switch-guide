Centrifuge end of stroke micro switch maintenance starts by tracing the equipment’s end-of-stroke movement to the switch actuator, not by assuming the switch has failed. Only some centrifuge designs use an electromechanical micro switch to sense this position. The equipment documentation determines what the switch senses and how its signal relates to the centrifuge’s control system.

Use a decision path: inspect the documented mechanical actuation path, then consider an electrical check only when the applicable procedure specifies isolation, actuator state, measuring method, and expected behavior. A click or one test result cannot establish that the centrifuge’s end-of-stroke function works correctly. If the required procedure or service limits are unavailable, leave electrical diagnosis and corrective work to qualified service personnel.

## What centrifuge end of stroke micro switch maintenance covers

A micro switch is an electromechanical component. In some centrifuges, a moving mechanism engages its actuator at a documented end-of-stroke position. The switch is distinct from the mechanism that moves it and from the control system that receives or uses its electrical signal. Other centrifuge designs may use a different arrangement, so this maintenance path applies only when equipment documentation identifies a micro switch for end-of-stroke detection.

Begin with the equipment’s service documentation and the switch’s applicable instructions. Establish which mechanism should move, which position is relevant, and where the actuator is located. Then inspect the actuation path before drawing a conclusion about internal switch condition. This sequence helps distinguish a mechanism that never reaches the switch from a switch or circuit problem.

Do not infer what the centrifuge should do when the switch changes state. The signal might be part of a control function, but the exact function and response depend on the documented circuit and equipment design. A switch’s presence alone does not establish that it stops the centrifuge, raises an alarm, or acts as an interlock.

The maintenance question is therefore limited: does the documented mechanism reach and engage the actuator as specified, and, if an approved electrical procedure exists, does the isolated switch behave as that procedure expects? Those observations inform a service decision; neither one by itself proves the entire centrifuge system is functioning correctly.

## Trace end-of-stroke movement to the micro switch actuator

Use the equipment documentation to identify the external mechanism that is meant to move the actuator. Follow the documented motion path toward the switch, observing whether the mechanism reaches the specified position and whether it engages the actuator. Do not force the mechanism, move parts beyond their documented range, or adjust the switch or linkage as an informal test.

Record what is observable: the mechanism’s position, whether its movement appears complete, whether it contacts the actuator, and any visible damage or obstruction. These are observations, not diagnoses. Incomplete movement or apparent misalignment may indicate an issue in the actuation path, but the evidence here supplies no universal tolerance or criterion for deciding whether a particular gap, angle, or amount of travel is acceptable. Use equipment-specific service limits for that judgment.

Actuator travel has defined reference points when the applicable specification uses them: pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. Do not use these terms to create an adjustment target. The permitted positions and any relevant limits must come from the applicable switch and equipment documentation.

If the mechanism does not reach the documented position or does not appear to engage the actuator, the observation points first to the mechanical actuation path for further evaluation. It does not prove the switch is sound; nor does it prove a particular linkage component is at fault. If access is unsafe, requires removing guards, or calls for actions not covered by the service procedure, stop and refer the inspection to qualified service personnel.

## Separate mechanical symptoms from contact and wiring symptoms

A failure to produce an expected end-of-stroke response can arise at different points in the signal path. The external mechanism may not complete its movement, the mechanism may fail to engage the actuator, the switch contacts may not behave as expected, terminal identification may be wrong, wiring may be faulty, or the control system may not respond as documented. These are separate possible fault locations, not interchangeable explanations.

If the documented end position is reached and the actuator is engaged, that observation narrows the mechanical question but does not verify the contacts. An isolated contact result that differs from the procedure’s expected behavior points to a different diagnostic branch from an actuator that is never reached. Even then, a test result does not by itself identify the failed part or establish why the installed centrifuge behaves as it does.

Identify terminals only from the exact switch documentation, terminal markings, and equipment circuit information. Do not infer terminal function from physical layout. Use COM, NO, or NC only if the contact arrangement and relevant reference state are documented for that switch. A terminal name without its documented state and circuit context is not enough to predict the centrifuge’s response.

Likewise, do not infer a stop, alarm, or interlock response from a contact change alone. The installed system’s behavior depends on the actual wiring and control logic. If the mechanism and isolated switch appear to meet their documented checks but the centrifuge still behaves unexpectedly, the unresolved issue may be in wiring or elsewhere in the control system. Avoid attributing it automatically to the micro switch.

## Verify the micro switch only under documented conditions

Perform electrical verification only when the centrifuge and switch procedures define the required isolation, actuator state, measuring method, and expected contact behavior. The procedure must also make clear which terminals to test. Follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. If those conditions or the expected result are not documented, do not invent a continuity test or assume a terminal state; limit the finding to authorized visual observations and refer electrical diagnosis to qualified service personnel.

A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not establish continuity, contact condition, correct wiring, or installed-system function. The actuator may move audibly while the electrical result remains unknown. Conversely, a result from an isolated switch test is limited to the tested switch, terminals, actuator state, and method. It cannot prove that the mechanism reaches the right position in operation or that the centrifuge control system responds correctly.

If the applicable procedure specifies contact resistance, treat it as a measured switch requirement. Its method, connection points, and limit must come from that specification and test procedure. Do not substitute an assumed threshold, apply a value from another switch or load, or treat an unspecified measurement as a pass or failure.

Keep the isolation boundary explicit. An isolated switch check is not a test of the complete installed circuit, and it does not establish that an energized centrifuge is safe or functioning correctly. Do not proceed to energized diagnosis based on this general guidance. Where a safe, documented procedure is absent, or the required access and qualification are not established, stop and refer the work to qualified service personnel.

## Decide what maintenance finding supports next action

Make the service decision from documented criteria, not from a single symptom. Record the observed mechanism position, whether the actuator was engaged, visible condition, and any isolated electrical result obtained under the applicable procedure. Include the test state and method so the result is not separated from the conditions that give it meaning.

If movement is incomplete or actuator engagement is uncertain, the finding concerns the mechanical path and needs evaluation against equipment-specific service criteria. If the mechanism reaches the documented position but an isolated switch result differs from the specified behavior, the result supports further switch-level diagnosis under the applicable procedure. Neither finding, by itself, authorizes adjustment, cleaning, or replacement. Use the equipment’s service limits and instructions to determine which actions are permitted.

If documented mechanical and isolated switch checks do not explain the symptom, keep wiring and the control system in scope. Confirm terminal identification and circuit function from the exact switch and equipment documentation. Unresolved installed-system behavior, particularly where diagnosis would require energized access or a procedure not supplied, belongs with qualified service personnel rather than an assumed switch failure.

The useful maintenance conclusion is bounded: identify what was observed, what was tested under defined conditions, and what remains unknown. Centrifuge end of stroke micro switch maintenance can separate an actuation-path observation from a contact-test result, but neither a click nor one isolated result proves correct operation of the complete centrifuge.
