The **antenna rotator closed position micro switch location** depends on the rotator’s mechanical design. There is no universal side, compartment, or mounting point that identifies it. This guide applies only to designs that use a micro switch for the closed-position limit. To find the relevant switch, trace the mechanism that moves toward the closed limit and look for the point where a stop or cam engages a switch actuator. Then verify its function with the equipment unpowered and the terminals identified. Physical position alone does not establish which limit a switch controls.

A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement. Such switches are commonly used for position or limit detection, but that general use does not establish that a particular rotator contains one. A rotator may use a different arrangement, and its internal layout cannot be determined without design-specific evidence. Treat documentation for the applicable rotator as the reference for identifying parts and functions.

## Where the antenna rotator closed position micro switch location is determined

The switch location follows the mechanism’s actuation path, not a standard enclosure layout. Depending on the design, a closed-limit switch may be inside the mechanism or behind an access cover. Neither location should be assumed before checking applicable documentation and the accessible mechanism. The key question is which moving part reaches the closed limit and what it contacts as that movement ends.

With power disconnected, inspect the mechanism visually from accessible areas. Trace the moving assembly toward its closed-position stop or cam. Look for a switch body mounted so that the stop or cam can contact its actuator at the relevant point in the travel. A switch mounted nearby is not necessarily the closed-limit switch: proximity is a clue to investigate, not proof of function.

The actuator’s movement can help explain the contact point. In the terminology of the cited switch standard, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position; overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. This describes actuator travel, not the location of a switch in a rotator. Do not push a mechanism or actuator beyond its permitted movement to search for a contact point.

Use the rotator’s applicable documentation to confirm that the candidate switch serves the closed limit. Documentation should resolve the function where the physical layout alone cannot. If the switch cannot be reached through an accessible area, or the stop-to-actuator relationship is unclear, do not infer the location from a presumed side, cover, or compartment. Keep the identification unresolved until design-specific information supports it.

## How to confirm a micro switch controls the closed limit

Physical identification and electrical verification are separate steps. First, with power disconnected, check whether the moving stop or cam engages the candidate switch’s actuator as the mechanism approaches the closed limit. Do not force the rotator or press an actuator beyond its normal movement to make the relationship clearer. If the engagement point is not visible or cannot be understood without unsafe access, stop at the visual inspection stage.

Before using a meter, identify the switch terminals from their markings or from applicable documentation. Do not guess terminal functions from their position or appearance. The terminals and contact arrangement of an unidentified switch remain unknown until reliable markings or documentation establish them. If the terminal identities are unclear, isolated continuity results may not answer which switch function is being tested.

For an unpowered check, disconnect power, identify the relevant terminals, and use a meter to check whether the contact state changes during controlled movement of the switch actuator. Keep the movement within its permitted range and do not force the mechanism. A state change shows that the contact responds to actuator movement under the test conditions; by itself, it does not prove that the switch controls the closed limit. Relate the result to the observed stop or cam engagement and confirm the function against applicable documentation.

Insulation resistance is a different measurement from a simple continuity check. Under the cited standard, it is evaluated between specified electrically isolated points using the applicable test method and conditions. That definition does not supply a test voltage, acceptance value, terminal pairing, or procedure for an unidentified rotator. Do not treat an unspecified insulation-resistance measurement as a substitute for identifying the switch terminals and checking the intended contact-state change.

## Closed-position switch versus open-position switch

A closed-position limit switch and an open-position limit switch refer to different travel-limit functions. Do not identify one solely because it appears first, last, or closest to a particular side of the enclosure. Where a design uses separate switches, trace each candidate switch to the moving part that engages it and confirm the assigned function through documentation or isolated testing. Other arrangements are possible, so the presence of one switch does not establish that a second switch exists.

Keep the inspection focused on the closed limit. The relevant evidence is the relationship between the closed-direction stop or cam, the switch actuator, and the documented function. A switch that responds electrically during controlled actuator movement may still serve another limit or another function. Likewise, a nearby part that appears to be a stop does not establish which limit it represents without confirming how the mechanism moves and what the documentation specifies.

The related question of where an open-position switch is located is separate. Its position cannot be inferred from the location of a closed-position switch, and this discussion does not assign it a universal location. If a rotator has markings or documentation that distinguish the two functions, use those sources to keep the identifications separate. When they do not, leave the function unresolved rather than applying a general layout assumption.

## When the expected micro switch is not visible

Not every antenna rotator uses a micro switch for the closed-position limit, and designs that do use one need not expose it in the same enclosure area. An absent visible switch does not prove that the rotator lacks a closed-limit function. The switch could be outside the accessible view, or the design could use another arrangement. Without documentation or direct evidence, those possibilities remain unresolved.

Check design-specific documentation and inspect only accessible areas with power disconnected. Do not assume that a particular cover, compartment, side, or mounting orientation is standard. If documentation identifies the limit mechanism but the switch itself is not visible, use that information to guide a safe visual inspection; do not extend the inspection into inaccessible areas merely to find a presumed part.

Stop if the actuator engagement cannot be identified, terminal markings are unclear, or the switch function remains uncertain after isolated testing. Do not probe energized wiring or disassemble beyond safe access to pursue an identification. A physical candidate is not confirmed by appearance alone, and an electrical state change is not enough to assign the closed-limit function without connecting it to the mechanism and documentation.

The reliable conclusion is conditional: in a rotator that uses a micro switch for the closed-position limit, locate it by tracing the closed-direction stop or cam to the switch actuator, then verify the contact-state response with power disconnected and terminals identified. Confirm the assigned limit function from applicable documentation. If those relationships cannot be established safely, do not claim a specific location or function. The **antenna rotator closed position micro switch location** is design-specific, not universal.
