The **air compressor guard interlock micro switch NO and NC terminals** are interpreted by the contact state of the switch, not by the guard’s position alone. NO and NC describe their relationship to the switch’s unactuated state. When the actuator moves, the expected continuity between the common terminal and each contact changes. The guard’s movement may operate the actuator through a linkage, but the exact relationship depends on the installation.

A continuity check can help establish the state of the switch contacts under defined conditions. It cannot, by itself, show that the complete interlock prevents compressor operation. Any check must be limited to a de-energized, isolated circuit and performed by a qualified person under applicable safety procedures. Use the exact switch and equipment documentation to identify terminals and interpret expected states.

## Air compressor guard interlock micro switch NO and NC terminals: what the labels mean

COM means common terminal, while NO means normally open and NC means normally closed. “Normally” refers here to the micro switch’s unactuated state. In that state, the common-NC pair is expected to have continuity, and the common-NO pair is expected to be open. When the actuator moves and the switch changes state, the expected conditions reverse: common-NO has continuity and common-NC is open.

These descriptions concern the contact pairs, not a guarantee about what the compressor does. They also depend on the switch’s documented configuration. Confirm the terminal symbols and connection diagram for the exact switch rather than identifying terminals by their physical position or assuming that every switch uses the same arrangement.

The contact states are a reference for interpreting a check. A measured result that differs from the expected state may reflect an incorrect terminal-pair assumption, the actuator’s actual state, or a reading affected by the connected circuit. The labels alone do not identify how the switch is mounted or which guard position moves its actuator.

## Relate guard movement to micro switch actuation

A micro switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement and is commonly used for position or limit detection. In a guard interlock, the relevant sequence is mechanical: guard movement is transmitted through the installation’s mounting and any linkage, which may move the switch actuator. Actuator movement then changes the contact state. Terminal labels describe the contacts; they do not specify the guard position that actuates them.

Trace that sequence for the actual equipment. First identify the guard position being considered, then determine how the installed mechanism moves the actuator in that position. Only then can the expected contact state be associated with the guard. A guard that is open does not, by itself, prove that the actuator is moved; nor does a closed guard establish that the switch is unactuated. Mounting, linkage, and the documented design determine the relationship.

For each defined guard position, compare the physical actuator state with the corresponding contact expectation: unactuated means common-NC is expected to have continuity and common-NO is expected to be open; actuated means those expected conditions reverse. Do not assume every guard interlock has the same arrangement or that a particular contact state always corresponds to “guard open” or “guard closed.” The exact switch and equipment documentation must establish that mapping.

## Check NO and NC continuity safely

Continuity checks should be limited to a de-energized, isolated circuit and performed by a qualified person following applicable safety and equipment procedures. Do not use a contact reading as a reason to energize the compressor or test the guard by operating the equipment. If the required isolation or qualification cannot be established, the check is outside this procedure.

With the circuit in the required condition, identify the switch and terminal pairs from their markings and wiring documentation. Define the actuator state being checked, such as unactuated or actuated, by reference to the mechanism rather than assuming that a particular guard position guarantees it. Check common-NC and common-NO separately in each defined state and compare the readings with the documented configuration.

The expected contact relationship is straightforward: in the unactuated state, common-NC is expected to have continuity and common-NO to be open; in the actuated state, common-NO is expected to have continuity and common-NC to be open. Keep each observation tied to its terminal pair and actuator state. A result without those references is difficult to interpret.

Readings taken while wiring remains connected to other circuit components can be affected by those connections. If a reading differs from expectation, do not treat it as conclusive evidence that the switch is faulty. Consult the equipment documentation and applicable safety procedures to determine the appropriate qualified verification method. This check addresses contact states only, not the complete interlock function.

## Interpret unexpected terminal readings

An unexpected result has several possible explanations. The terminal pair may have been identified incorrectly, or the actuator may not be in the assumed state. The mechanical travel or linkage alignment may also affect whether guard movement reaches the actuator as intended. A reading taken with other circuit components connected may not represent the isolated contact pair. Contact condition is another possible issue, but one measurement does not establish that diagnosis.

Keep the observations separate: record which terminals were checked, the actuator state, and whether the circuit was isolated as required. Then compare those details with the switch markings, wiring diagram, and equipment documentation. If the indicated guard position and the actuator state do not correspond as expected, the mechanical relationship needs qualified assessment; changing the assumed terminal state will not resolve that uncertainty.

Do not bypass or defeat the guard interlock, and do not operate the compressor to investigate a discrepant reading. If the readings or mechanical sequence remain unclear, stop at the limits of the contact check and use the applicable qualified procedure. A continuity result can narrow what is known about the contacts, but it cannot identify every cause of an unexpected system behavior.

## Know what a continuity test cannot confirm

A continuity test can indicate whether a defined contact pair has continuity in a defined actuator state under the test conditions. It does not prove that guard movement reliably actuates the switch, that the wiring is correct throughout the equipment, or that the complete interlock stops or prevents compressor operation. Those are system-level questions requiring the applicable equipment documentation and qualified procedures.

Do not infer the interlock’s safety behavior from the NO or NC label alone. The effect of a contact change depends on the actual circuit and design. Likewise, a reading that matches the expected contact state does not establish that the guard, linkage, switch, wiring, and control function together as intended. A failed or uncertain safety check should not be treated as permission to operate the equipment; keep it out of service until it has been appropriately assessed.

The key distinction is between a switch-level observation and system-level verification. The air compressor guard interlock micro switch NO and NC terminals provide defined contact-state references, but only the exact documentation and applicable qualified procedure can establish how those contacts relate to the guard and the complete interlock function.
