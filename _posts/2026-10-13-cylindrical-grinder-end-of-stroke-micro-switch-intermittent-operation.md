Intermittent stopping or signaling at a cylindrical grinder’s end of travel does not, by itself, identify a failed micro switch. The phrase cylindrical grinder end of stroke micro switch intermittent operation describes a symptom to investigate, not a confirmed fault. In some grinder designs, a mechanism actuates a micro switch to detect end of stroke; the resulting control response depends on the machine’s actual design and circuit.

Separate the motion that reaches the switch from the switch’s electrical state, then distinguish both from terminal, wiring, and control-circuit issues. An observation or isolated switch check can narrow the possibilities, but neither establishes that the entire grinder is operating correctly.

## Cylindrical grinder end of stroke micro switch intermittent operation: first separate the symptom from the cause

Begin by describing what is inconsistent, without assigning a cause. The reported symptom might be that an end-of-travel signal seems absent on some cycles, that the machine’s response varies, or that the available observation does not establish whether the switch changed state. These descriptions are not interchangeable: a machine response is not a direct measurement of the switch, and a perceived signal does not identify where in the control path a problem lies.

A cylindrical grinder may use a micro switch for end-of-stroke detection, but that arrangement is design-dependent. The mechanism may provide the input, while the effect of a contact change depends on the actual machine circuit and control logic. Do not infer a particular contact arrangement, normal state, stopping response, or signal behavior simply from the presence of an end-of-stroke switch.

Keep four possibilities distinct while investigating:

- The mechanism may not reach or engage the actuator consistently.
- The switch may receive a consistent mechanical input but change electrical state inconsistently.
- Terminal identification or wiring may be incorrect or faulty.
- The problem may lie elsewhere in the grinder’s control circuit or equipment.

These are diagnostic categories, not conclusions. A machine symptom alone does not discriminate among them. Record what can actually be observed, such as whether the mechanism reaches the switch actuator and whether the symptom occurs with that motion. Avoid treating an inconsistent machine response as proof of an internal contact fault.

The useful first question is whether the mechanical input to the actuator is repeatable. If it is not, a switch-only explanation is premature. If the input appears consistent, that observation makes a switch-state check more relevant, but still does not establish that the switch is the cause. Terminal and circuit questions remain separate.

## Follow the end-of-stroke motion to the micro switch actuator

Trace the end-of-stroke mechanism’s motion to the switch actuator before attributing intermittent operation to the switch contacts. The actuator is the part moved by the external mechanism. The relevant observation is whether the mechanism reaches and engages it consistently under the applicable safe inspection conditions, not merely whether the switch appears to move or make a sound.

Compare the mechanical input with the reported symptom. If the mechanism does not reach the actuator on some occasions, or its engagement varies, the inconsistent input is a plausible distinction from a switch receiving repeatable input. That observation does not identify why the motion varies, and it does not establish whether the electrical switch also has a fault. It only shows that the switch’s input may not be consistent.

If the mechanism appears to engage the actuator consistently while the machine response remains intermittent, an electrical-state question becomes more relevant. Even then, external observation cannot prove that the switch contacts changed state correctly. Nor does it rule out a terminal, wiring, or control-circuit issue. Treat the mechanical check as one part of the fault path, not as a complete diagnosis.

Keep the inspection within the applicable machine procedure and safety requirements. Do not create a test condition by moving a mechanism or accessing energized equipment unless that action is covered by the documented procedure and the person doing it is qualified. If the safe conditions for observing the motion are not established, stop at that boundary rather than improvising an inspection method.

A consistent actuator engagement is therefore useful evidence, but limited evidence: it supports moving to a defined electrical check, if one is permitted and correctly specified. Inconsistent engagement points attention back to the mechanical input path, while leaving the switch and the rest of the machine unresolved.

## Separate a micro switch state check from machine-circuit diagnosis

A switch-level check is meaningful only if the switch is isolated as required by the applicable procedure, the actuator state is defined, and the terminals being checked are identified from the exact switch and equipment documentation. Do not infer terminal function from physical layout. Use the documented connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load for that exact switch and equipment; those details cannot be supplied by a generic description of a micro switch.

If the applicable documentation provides an isolated continuity-check procedure and expected behavior for defined actuator states, follow that procedure and compare the result only with its stated expectation. The available evidence here does not specify which terminals should show continuity, which actuator states to use, or the expected reading for this grinder’s switch. Without those details, no particular continuity result can be prescribed or interpreted as pass or fail.

A click may indicate mechanical movement, but it does not establish electrical continuity, correct terminal identification, correct wiring, or suitability for the grinder’s circuit. Likewise, an isolated reading concerns only the tested switch, terminals, and actuator state. It does not prove that the switch is wired correctly when installed or that the grinder’s control logic and other equipment operate correctly.

Keep the following checks conceptually separate:

- **Actuation:** Does the end-of-stroke mechanism engage the actuator consistently?
- **Switch state:** Under a documented, isolated check, does the switch behave as expected at the defined actuator states?
- **Terminals and wiring:** Are the identified terminals and connections consistent with the exact equipment documentation?
- **Control system:** Does the surrounding circuit respond as designed, or is a fault elsewhere affecting the machine symptom?

The first two questions concern the mechanical input and the isolated component. The latter questions concern installation and the surrounding machine. A result in one category does not settle the others. In particular, an isolated continuity result cannot establish installed-circuit behavior, and an inconsistent installed response cannot by itself prove the switch is defective.

Electrical isolation and testing must remain within applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. If the relevant procedure, terminal identification, or expected behavior is unavailable, or the next check requires work outside that documented boundary, leave it to qualified personnel. Do not substitute a generic wiring assumption for machine-specific instructions.

## Decide what the intermittent-operation check establishes

Use the observations to identify the next unresolved part of the fault path, not to declare the grinder or switch good or bad. If actuator engagement is visibly inconsistent, that supports examining the mechanical input path; it does not establish the mechanical cause or rule out an additional electrical fault. If engagement appears consistent, a documented isolated check may help assess the switch state, but only for the terminals and actuator conditions actually tested.

If the isolated result differs from the documented expectation, it supports concern about the tested switch under that test condition. It does not, alone, explain the machine symptom or establish the state of its wiring and control circuit. If the switch-level result matches the documented expectation, that is not proof that the installed connections or the rest of the grinder’s control system are correct.

If terminal identification or wiring is uncertain, the exact equipment documentation is the boundary for interpretation. If the isolated procedure or expected state is not documented, the evidence is insufficient to label a continuity result abnormal. If the remaining question concerns installed wiring, control logic, or work beyond the applicable safety procedure, qualified personnel must address it.

The practical conclusion is limited: trace repeatable end-of-stroke motion to the actuator first, then use only a documented, isolated switch check to assess the switch at defined states. Keep terminal and wiring checks distinct from control-circuit diagnosis. No single observation or switch-only reading proves the cause of intermittent grinder operation or verifies the complete machine.
