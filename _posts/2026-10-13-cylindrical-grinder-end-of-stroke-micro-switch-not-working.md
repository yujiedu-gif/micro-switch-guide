A **cylindrical grinder end of stroke micro switch not working** is a symptom to investigate, not proof that the switch itself has failed. On a grinder design that uses a micro switch at the end of stroke, first separate two questions: does the travel mechanism reach and move the actuator, and does the switch change contact state when actuated? A fault in the surrounding wiring or control system can also produce an end-of-travel symptom.

Keep those fault paths distinct. A mechanism that does not move the actuator does not establish an internal contact failure. A click suggests movement but does not prove electrical operation. An isolated continuity observation can provide evidence about the switch under a defined test condition, but it does not establish that the installed grinder circuit works. The sequence below sets out what each observation can and cannot confirm. It does not cover adjustment, replacement, bypassing, or repair.

## First distinguish a missed actuator from a micro switch that fails to change state

This diagnostic frame applies only to cylindrical grinders whose design actually uses a micro switch at the end of stroke. It does not assume that every grinder has one, that it is mounted in a particular position, or that it uses a particular contact arrangement. The applicable equipment documentation must establish whether a micro switch is present and describe its role.

An end-of-travel symptom alone does not identify the failed part. The initial distinction is between an input problem and an electrical or control problem. In the first case, the grinder’s travel mechanism may not reach or move the switch actuator. In the second, the actuator may move, but the switch may not change contact state, or the change may not be reflected by the surrounding circuit.

These possibilities require different evidence. Observing whether the mechanism reaches and moves the actuator addresses mechanical engagement. A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not establish that the contacts changed state. An isolated contact test addresses the switch’s behavior only under its defined test conditions. Neither observation alone proves that the complete grinder control circuit is functional.

The sequence matters: consider the mechanical input first, then evaluate contact behavior only when a safe, documented, isolated test can be performed. Keep terminal identification and the wider circuit as separate questions. Do not infer the switch’s normal state, contact labels, or control effect from the fact that it sits at a travel limit.

## Cylindrical grinder end of stroke micro switch not working: check the actuation path first

With the machine safely out of service, consider whether the travel mechanism appears to reach and move the switch actuator. This is an observation, not an invitation to move, adjust, or defeat the mechanism. If the mechanism does not appear to reach or move the actuator, the evidence points to an actuation-path question. It does not confirm an internal switch failure, because the switch may not have received the mechanical input needed for its state to change.

If the mechanism appears to reach the actuator, that narrows the question but does not settle it. Reaching the actuator does not prove that its movement is sufficient to operate the switch, nor does it prove that the contacts respond. Those details depend on the documented switch and equipment arrangement, which should not be guessed from appearance.

A click is similarly limited evidence. It can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not establish continuity, correct terminal identification, or the condition of the installed circuit. No click is not, by itself, a complete diagnosis either: the available observation does not distinguish every possible mechanical or electrical cause.

Do not treat a symptom at the travel limit as permission to operate the grinder while the cause is uncertain. If safe observation cannot establish whether the mechanism engages the actuator, preserve that uncertainty rather than forcing movement or altering the setup. Adjustment and repair are outside this diagnostic sequence.

## Verify contact change only under a documented, isolated test condition

A continuity test can address whether an isolated switch changes contact state under defined conditions. It is meaningful only when the switch is safely isolated, the actuator state is defined, and the tested terminals are identified from applicable documentation. Without those conditions, a reading may not answer whether the switch itself behaves as documented.

Use the connection diagram and terminal markings for the exact switch and equipment. Do not identify terminals by wire position or a generic physical-layout assumption. In particular, do not assume COM, NO, or NC labels or a contact arrangement that the applicable documentation does not establish. Compare observations across the documented reference and actuated states, rather than relying on an unspecified “open” or “closed” expectation.

Pretravel and overtravel describe different parts of actuator motion. Under the stated definitions in MIL-PRF-8805K sections 6.5.14 and 6.5.16, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These terms do not supply a travel value for a particular grinder or switch. Do not infer from them how far a grinder mechanism should move or how a specific installation should be adjusted.

A result that matches the documented contact states supports only a limited conclusion: the isolated switch showed the documented behavior under the tested conditions. A result that does not match them leaves the switch or the test setup in question. Neither outcome proves the condition of the installed wiring, control logic, or complete grinder assembly.

## Separate switch, terminal, wiring, and control-system possibilities

If the mechanism appears to actuate the switch but a correctly identified, isolated test does not show the documented contact-state change, the switch may be implicated, but the test setup and terminal identification remain relevant. Do not turn one unexpected reading into a definitive diagnosis unless the isolation condition, actuator state, measurement method, and terminal identities are all established.

If the isolated switch behavior matches its documentation, that finding does not clear the rest of the grinder circuit. Terminal identification, connections, wiring, and control logic remain distinct possibilities. A problem elsewhere in the equipment can produce a symptom that resembles a switch failure. The contact observation alone cannot locate such a problem or show that the installed signal path is sound.

For any connection or rating question, rely on the exact switch and equipment documentation: its connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load. Do not infer a rating or function from a terminal’s physical position, a wire’s appearance, or a generic switch description. Do not transfer a documented condition to an undocumented terminal arrangement or load.

Electrical testing and work must remain within applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. This article does not provide a live-circuit test procedure or a method to bypass the switch. Leave energized testing and work beyond the safe isolation procedure to qualified personnel. If the necessary documentation or safe test conditions are unavailable, the evidence is insufficient to distinguish the switch from the surrounding circuit.

## Decide what the observations do, and do not, confirm

Attributing an end-of-stroke symptom to a micro switch requires more than noticing that the grinder did not respond as expected. The useful distinctions are whether the travel mechanism reaches and moves the actuator, whether the isolated switch changes state in defined actuator states, and whether the terminal identities and expected states are supported by applicable documentation.

If the mechanism does not reach or move the actuator, the observation raises an actuation-path question rather than confirming a failed contact. If it does move the actuator, an isolated test that disagrees with the documented states leaves the switch or test setup in question. If isolated behavior matches the documentation, the result still does not prove the installed wiring or control system is functional. Each finding narrows the question without deciding every other fault path.

A click is not proof of electrical contact operation. One continuity reading is not proof that the whole switch, wiring, control circuit, or grinder is good or bad. Keep the component-level observation separate from the installed-system symptom, and do not infer the grinder’s control response from the presence of a switch alone.

When the available observations do not establish the actuation path, terminal identities, or test conditions, leave the cause unresolved and seek qualified evaluation under the applicable safety requirements. Do not bypass, adjust, replace, or operate the grinder on the strength of an incomplete diagnosis. For a **cylindrical grinder end of stroke micro switch not working**, the defensible conclusion is limited to what the mechanical observation and documented isolated test actually show.
