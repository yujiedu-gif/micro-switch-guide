Adjustment of a rotary indexing table guard interlock micro switch is specific to the switch, guard mechanism, and host equipment. The rotary indexing table guard interlock micro switch adjustment can establish only the documented mechanical relationship between the guard mechanism and a micro switch the equipment actually uses. It cannot, by itself, establish that the complete guard interlock safety function works.

The switch is one component in a chain: guard movement may pass through a cam, lever, or other linkage before it moves the actuator. The switch's contact behavior then feeds into the equipment's wiring and control logic. A click, a change in actuator position, or one continuity reading is therefore not proof of correct system behavior. Use the equipment-specific instructions and acceptance criteria to assess the mechanical relationship and verify the full interlock. Do not infer that all rotary indexing tables use a micro switch or share one adjustment method.

## What rotary indexing table guard interlock micro switch adjustment can establish

The purpose of adjustment is limited: correct the documented mechanical relationship between the guard mechanism and the micro switch, if the equipment documentation identifies that component and permits adjustment. It is not a general method for proving that a table is safe to operate, and it does not establish how the control system responds to every guard state.

Treat the guard, its mounting, any linkage, the micro switch, and the control circuit as related but distinct parts. The guard may move correctly while a linkage fails to transfer that movement to the actuator. The actuator may move while the switch contacts fail to behave as documented. The switch may behave as documented while a terminal, wiring, control-logic, or other equipment fault prevents the expected system response. These possibilities require different observations and must not be collapsed into a single diagnosis.

A click indicates movement of a snap mechanism when that mechanism is present, but it does not prove continuity, correct terminal identification, wiring, or suitability for the equipment's safety function. Likewise, a continuity result concerns only the switch under the particular test state and conditions. Neither observation alone verifies the complete guard interlock.

Before making an adjustment, establish that the equipment documentation identifies the switch, describes the allowed adjustment, and gives a procedure and acceptance criteria for verifying the complete interlock. If any of those are missing, the available evidence does not establish a correct adjustment or a safe operating condition. Do not substitute a generic dimension, an assumed contact arrangement, or an informal functional check for the documented requirements.

## Trace guard movement to the micro switch actuator

Start by identifying the documented actuation path. Determine which part of the guard moves, whether that movement is transferred directly or through a cam, lever, or other linkage, and where it contacts the micro switch actuator. Confirm the switch identity and actuator from the equipment and switch documentation rather than guessing from physical appearance. The actuator is the switch part moved by the external mechanism; the guard itself is not necessarily in direct contact with it.

Observe the path under the conditions allowed by the host equipment's inspection procedure. Check whether the guard reaches the position specified by the equipment instructions and whether its movement transfers consistently through the mounting and linkage to the actuator. Look for obstruction, looseness, binding, or inconsistent contact along that path. Such observations can identify where movement appears to be lost or impeded, but do not alone establish the underlying cause or prove the safety function.

Interpret actuator movement using the documented operating and release points. These are distinct events: an operating point is where the switch changes state as the actuator moves in the operating direction; a release point is where it returns as the actuator moves back. The separation between the events relates to differential travel. The equipment and switch documentation must determine what relationship is acceptable in this installation; do not assume that one point or a click describes both events.

Where the documented design and procedure address actuator travel, preserve their limits. Pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position. Overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These definitions do not supply a permissible amount of travel for a particular switch or installation. Do not move a mounting, linkage, or actuator beyond the limits specified for the exact equipment and switch. No universal adjustment dimension or force value can be inferred here.

A repeatable mismatch between guard movement and actuator movement may point toward alignment, mounting, or linkage concerns. A binding or obstructed path is a mechanical observation, not proof that the switch is defective. Conversely, apparently smooth actuator motion does not prove that the contacts or downstream control function correctly. Keep each observation tied to the component and state actually examined.

## Separate alignment symptoms from electrical and control faults

If the guard does not reach or release the actuator as the equipment documentation specifies, investigate the documented mechanical path first. Misalignment, mounting movement, an obstruction, or linkage binding may explain why guard motion is not transferred as intended. The observation does not identify which of these is responsible, so do not make an undocumented adjustment merely to make the actuator move.

If actuator movement occurs but the isolated switch does not show the documented contact behavior in the specified test state, a switch or contact fault may be involved. That conclusion depends on the actual contact arrangement, terminal identification, actuator state, and test method documented for the exact switch. Do not infer that COM, NO, or NC terminals are present, or assign their states, unless the switch documentation establishes them. A test of an isolated switch is not a test of the installed control circuit or the complete safety function.

If the switch-level behavior matches its documentation but the equipment responds unexpectedly, the cause may lie in terminal identification, wiring, control logic, or another part of the equipment. A correct-looking mechanical movement does not resolve those possibilities. Use the connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. Do not infer terminal function from physical layout or assume a general rating or circuit behavior.

These fault paths can overlap. For example, an observed system response that differs from expectation does not establish whether the switch, wiring, logic, or another equipment component is at fault. The useful distinction is what has actually been verified: guard-to-linkage movement, actuator travel, isolated switch behavior, or complete system response. A result at one level must not be represented as proof at another.

## Use the equipment's documented adjustment and verification procedure

Follow the host equipment's isolation and access procedure before inspection or adjustment. Do not improvise a procedure, bypass the interlock, or use an attempted bypass as a test. For electrical work, the cited United States workplace rule generally requires exposed live parts to be deenergized before work, requires a qualified person to verify the deenergized condition with test equipment, and limits work on energized parts to qualified persons. Verify all applicable requirements; this boundary does not replace the equipment-specific procedure.

Make only adjustments that the host equipment and switch documentation allow. Preserve the documented mounting constraints, operating and release relationship, and permissible actuator travel. Do not use an assumed universal dimension, force, or position. If the documentation does not identify an adjustment limit or explain how to assess it, the correct limit remains unresolved; stop rather than inventing one.

After an allowed adjustment, use the host equipment's stated procedure and acceptance criteria to verify the complete guard interlock function. A click or isolated continuity check may provide a limited switch-level observation when performed in the specified state and conditions, but neither is complete safety verification. Do not conclude that the table is safe to operate from either observation alone.

If documentation, safe access, or complete verification criteria are unavailable, stop and refer the work to qualified personnel. The rotary indexing table guard interlock micro switch adjustment is established only to the extent supported by the equipment-specific mechanical limits and verification results. The switch's actuation is one part of that assessment, not proof that the whole safety function works.
