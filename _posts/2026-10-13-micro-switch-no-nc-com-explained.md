COM, NO, and NC identify terminals in a documented contact arrangement, but the labels do not by themselves describe how an installed circuit behaves. This guide to **micro switch no nc com explained** ties the terms to the contact diagram and its stated actuator reference condition. Check the documentation for the exact switch: not every micro switch has this arrangement, and terminal position or appearance alone does not establish which terminal is which.

## micro switch no nc com explained: what the labels identify

In a documented changeover contact arrangement, COM means common, while NO and NC identify the normally open and normally closed terminals. These are terminal labels tied to a particular contact arrangement, not universal labels that can be assumed to appear on every micro switch. The exact device marking or schematic controls.

“Normally open” and “normally closed” are meaningful only relative to the reference condition specified for the switch. A diagram may define that condition by an actuator state. Do not assume, without checking, that the reference is always unactuated. The labels identify contact relationships at the stated reference condition; they do not guarantee a particular current flow in an installed circuit.

A contact diagram is the key to interpreting the labels. It shows which terminal is common and how the documented contact paths relate at the reference and operated actuator conditions. Use the diagram for the exact switch rather than inferring terminal identity from a familiar layout, a physical position, or a label seen on another device.

These distinctions keep three questions separate: what the terminal markings mean, which contacts are connected in a defined actuator state, and what the surrounding circuit does. The markings and diagram can address the first two within their documented scope. They do not, on their own, explain the wiring or behavior of the host equipment.

## Read NO and NC from the stated reference condition

NO and NC describe contact state at a specified reference condition. Start by locating that condition in the switch documentation or contact diagram. If it identifies an unactuated state, interpret the labels against that state. If it specifies another state, use that instead. If the reference condition is not clear, do not supply one by assumption; consult the exact device documentation.

At the stated reference condition, the diagram identifies which contact path is shown as open and which is shown as closed in the documented arrangement. When the actuator moves to a separately documented operated state, the diagram may show a change in those paths. This is a statement about the switch’s documented contacts, not a promise about current flow, appliance response, or the result of any particular wiring.

The word “normally” can mislead if read as “always.” It does not mean that a terminal keeps the same contact relationship through every actuator state. Nor does it independently tell you whether the host circuit is energized, what load is connected, or what the equipment will do when the actuator moves. Those matters depend on the installed wiring and circuit, which these terminal labels do not specify.

For accurate interpretation, keep the reference condition beside the label. For example, describe a contact state as the state shown for the documented reference actuator condition, rather than saying simply that NO is “open” or NC is “closed” in every circumstance. This wording preserves the condition that gives the terms their meaning and avoids turning a switch label into an unconditional electrical claim.

If the documentation does not state the actuator condition or does not provide a clear contact diagram, the available labels alone may not resolve the contact state you need to identify. Treat that point as unknown until the exact documentation clarifies it. Do not infer a reference state from the equipment’s apparent operation or from the labels of a different switch.

## Relate COM to the documented contact paths

Where the exact documentation shows a changeover contact arrangement, COM is the common terminal in that arrangement. Read the diagram to see which contact path is shown between COM and the other identified terminal at each documented actuator state. The diagram, not terminal placement or appearance, establishes that relationship.

The contact diagram may show that as the actuator moves between the stated reference and operated conditions, the contact connected to COM changes. Make that interpretation only when the relevant diagram documents the arrangement and states the conditions being compared. Do not generalize it to switches without that documented arrangement, or infer details the diagram does not provide.

A useful reading sequence is to identify the actuator condition shown, locate COM in the diagram, and then trace the documented contact relationship for that condition. Repeat for any other state that the documentation specifies. This maps the switch’s contacts without assuming a terminal layout, a normal state not stated by the source, or a result in the surrounding circuit.

That last distinction matters. An isolated switch diagram describes contact relationships within the component. Once connected, wiring and host-circuit behavior introduce questions that terminal labels cannot answer. A change in the documented contact path does not, by itself, establish whether equipment starts, stops, signals, or remains energized. Those outcomes depend on the actual circuit and cannot be determined from COM, NO, and NC alone.

If markings on the switch and its documentation seem inconsistent, do not resolve the discrepancy by guessing from physical position. The exact switch marking or schematic controls, and unclear identification remains unresolved until checked against the applicable documentation. This is a limit on what the labels alone can establish, not a conclusion about the component’s condition.

## Verify labels on an isolated micro switch, within limits

A bounded check begins with an isolated switch, a defined actuator condition, and the documented contact diagram. With the switch isolated, a continuity check can be compared with the relationship the diagram specifies for that actuator state. If another state is documented, the observation can also be compared with that state’s diagram. Keep the tested state and the documentation’s reference condition explicit when describing the result.

This check is limited to the isolated switch’s contacts under the conditions tested. A reading that agrees with the diagram supports that particular contact observation; it does not establish that the switch is wired correctly in equipment, that the host circuit behaves as intended, that a load is suitable, or that the surrounding equipment is safe. A reading by itself also does not identify what caused an installed system to behave a certain way.

If the observed contact relationship does not match the documented one, first make sure the comparison uses the exact switch documentation and the stated actuator condition. If the reference state, terminal identity, or diagram is unclear, the observation cannot settle the interpretation. Do not turn an ambiguous check into a claim about the installed circuit or equipment.

The practical boundary is straightforward: use COM, NO, and NC to identify the documented contact arrangement, and use the stated actuator reference condition to interpret NO and NC. Use the exact diagram to map contact paths. A continuity check on an isolated switch can compare a defined switch state with that documentation, but neither labels nor one reading establish installed wiring, load suitability, or circuit safety.
