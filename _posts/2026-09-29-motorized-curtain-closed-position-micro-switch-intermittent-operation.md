In a motorized curtain, intermittent behavior at the closed position may involve inconsistent movement of a micro switch or changes in its contacts, but the symptom does not identify a single cause. The phrase motorized curtain closed position micro switch intermittent operation applies only to designs that use a micro switch as part of their closed-position input. Linkage movement, terminal connections, wiring, and control-system behavior are separate parts of the diagnostic picture.

A useful distinction is whether the mechanism reaches and moves the switch actuator consistently, whether the isolated switch changes contact state consistently at defined actuator positions, and whether the installed system responds as expected. Each observation addresses a different part of the path. None, on its own, proves the condition of the complete curtain system.

## motorized curtain closed position micro switch intermittent operation

Some motorized curtain designs use a micro switch to detect a closed position; others may use a different arrangement. The switch, where present, is a component in the system, not the curtain or its controller. The effect of a contact change depends on the actual circuit and design, so an intermittent closed-position report should be treated as a symptom to differentiate, not as proof that the switch itself has failed.

The relevant path has several links: curtain movement may move a linkage or other mechanism; that mechanism may move the switch actuator; the switch contacts may change state; and the connected control system may interpret that input. A problem or inconsistency at any of these links could affect the reported position. Without documentation for the actual design, the intended contact arrangement, normal state, and control response remain unresolved.

Start by separating three observations. First, does the curtain mechanism engage and move the actuator consistently at the closed position? Second, does the isolated switch show repeatable contact behavior at defined actuator states, when its arrangement is known and a safe test is supported? Third, does the installed system’s reported position agree with those observations? Comparing these points can narrow the path to investigate, but does not establish a definitive fault or justify replacing a component based on the symptom alone.

## How a micro switch can signal a curtain’s closed position

In a design that uses a micro switch, movement of the curtain or its linkage can act on the switch actuator. When the actuator reaches the switch’s operating point, the contacts change state. The system connected to those contacts then interprets the input according to its circuit. This is a conditional description, not a claim that every curtain uses this arrangement or responds to the input in the same way.

The actuator is the switch part moved by the external mechanism. The operating point is the event at which the switch changes to its operated state. The release point is a different event: the force or torque on the actuator must be reduced to permit the contacts to return to the unoperated position after operation. The release-force definition in MIL-PRF-8805K section 6.5.17 describes this return condition. These distinct events matter when comparing movement in opposite directions; observing one does not establish what happens at the other.

Do not infer a contact state from the phrase “closed position.” If the exact switch has a documented changeover contact, its terminals may be labeled common, normally closed, and normally open. Those labels do not replace the device’s diagram or establish how the curtain controller uses the contacts. The exact switch marking or schematic controls, including the reference condition for interpreting contact states.

Likewise, do not infer terminal identity from physical position or appearance. Use the connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. The controller’s interpretation depends on the circuit, so a contact transition alone does not prove that the system should stop, report closure, or take any other particular action.

## Separate inconsistent actuation from electrical intermittency

Begin with the mechanical input path. Observe, without assuming a fault, whether the curtain or linkage reaches the actuator in a consistent way at the closed position. If engagement varies, or the actuator’s movement differs between attempts, that observation points toward variable mechanical engagement as a path to investigate. It does not prove that the switch is defective or that the linkage is the only cause. The switch may still have an electrical issue, and the system may have additional faults.

A click is not a substitute for observing actuator movement or checking contact behavior. It can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but does not prove continuity, correct wiring, correct terminal identification, or suitability for the circuit. A click heard on one attempt and not another may be a useful observation, but it cannot establish which part of the system caused the intermittent report.

If the contact arrangement is known and an isolated test is supported by the exact documentation, compare the switch’s contact behavior at defined actuator states. The test concerns the isolated switch, not its installed wiring or the controller. A result that changes inconsistently under the same defined conditions may implicate the switch contacts, the test setup, or the consistency of actuator positioning. It is not, by itself, a complete diagnosis. The meaning of a reading depends on the documented contact arrangement and the state being tested.

If isolated switch behavior is repeatable but the installed system’s response is intermittent, the discrepancy shifts attention to the rest of the path. Possible areas include terminal connections, wiring, how the input is interpreted, or another problem in the control system. This observation does not identify which one is responsible. It only shows that repeatable isolated behavior has not, by itself, explained the installed symptom.

## What checks can establish, and what they cannot

An isolated continuity check can establish contact behavior for the tested switch at the defined actuator states and under the stated test conditions. It cannot establish that the installed wiring is intact, that terminals have been identified correctly in the equipment, that the controller interprets the input as intended, or that the whole curtain system is safe or functional. Keep the boundaries of the test attached to any conclusion drawn from it.

Electrical testing must stay within the documented contact arrangement and applicable isolation and qualification requirements. Do not improvise a live-circuit test or infer a safe procedure from a general switch description. If the circuit cannot be safely isolated, the terminals are uncertain, or the required method is unclear, leave electrical work to qualified personnel. The available evidence does not supply a curtain-specific test procedure or authorize a repair or rewiring method.

A continuity reading is meaningful only when the tested switch is isolated, the actuator state is defined, and the expected behavior is known from the exact switch documentation. A reading taken without those conditions may be difficult to interpret. Even a repeatable result applies only to the tested switch and states; it does not establish how the complete installed assembly performs during curtain movement.

Insulation resistance is also a distinct measurement from contact continuity. Under the MIL-PRF-8805K insulation-resistance requirement and referenced test method, it is evaluated between specified electrically isolated points using the applicable test method and conditions. This definition does not provide a curtain-specific test value or a substitute for the documentation of the actual switch and equipment.

## When to stop diagnosing the micro switch alone

If the actuator is reached consistently and isolated contact behavior is repeatable at defined states, the intermittent installed symptom may lie elsewhere in the mechanism-to-controller path. That is a decision boundary, not a definitive diagnosis. The observations do not determine whether the remaining cause is a terminal connection, wiring, input interpretation, or another system problem.

If actuator engagement varies, keep the mechanical input path in view rather than labeling the switch failed from the symptom alone. If isolated contact behavior is inconsistent under a documented, controlled test, that may justify further evaluation of the switch and test setup, but it still does not establish the condition of the wiring or controller. In either case, distinguish what was directly observed from what remains unknown.

Do not assume a universal closed-state contact, a particular shutdown response, or a specific repair. Use the curtain and switch documentation to understand the actual circuit and test boundaries. Circuit-specific or energized work that is uncertain or outside a safe, documented procedure belongs with qualified service personnel. For motorized curtain closed position micro switch intermittent operation, the useful conclusion is limited: compare consistent mechanical actuation, isolated contact behavior in defined states, and installed-system response, then pursue the unresolved part of the path without treating any single observation as proof of the whole system’s condition.
