A **centrifuge end of stroke micro switch location** is determined by the particular machine’s design, not by a universal mounting position. In a centrifuge that uses a micro switch for end-of-stroke sensing, identify the relevant moving mechanism in the service documentation, then trace how its motion reaches the switch actuator. The switch may be mounted on a support or linkage, but its position and actuation path vary by design.

Some centrifuge designs may use another sensing arrangement or no micro switch for this function. The physical location of a component also does not, by itself, show what its contacts do or how the centrifuge responds. Use design-specific documentation to establish both component identity and the limits of any inspection.

## What “centrifuge end of stroke micro switch location” means

Here, “location” means the physical position of a micro switch that a particular centrifuge design uses to detect a motion endpoint. It does not name a standard point shared by centrifuges. A design may use a micro switch, a different sensing arrangement, or no micro switch for end-of-stroke sensing. Do not assume that a switch is present just because the machine reaches an endpoint or stops there.

A micro switch is an electromechanical component. In this context, its actuator is the part moved by an external mechanism. The moving centrifuge member and the switch are separate parts: the moving member may contact or move the actuator, while the switch body is mounted in a position that allows the documented mechanism to operate it. The mounting point, linkage, and direction of movement are design-specific, so a general description cannot identify the component’s location in an unexamined centrifuge.

Keep the physical-location question separate from the machine’s control behavior. Finding a switch near a moving member does not prove that it detects the endpoint, that its contacts have a particular state, or that it stops the machine. Those relationships require documentation for the exact centrifuge design. The location method is therefore a trace from documented motion to documented actuation, not a guess based on where a switch might commonly be placed.

## Trace the end-of-stroke motion path to the micro switch

Start with the centrifuge’s service documentation or parts diagram. Use it to identify the moving member associated with the relevant endpoint and the documented position or event that the design treats as end of stroke. Do not substitute a visually convenient moving part for the one named in the documentation. A diagram may help establish which parts belong to the mechanism, but it should not be read as proof of an electrical function that it does not specify.

Next, follow the documented actuation path from that moving member toward the sensing component. The path may involve direct contact or a linkage, depending on the design. Trace the sequence of physical parts: the moving member, any documented part that transfers its motion, and the switch actuator. The switch body is the component that contains the contacts; the actuator is the part it moves. Neither should be confused with the centrifuge member that supplies the motion.

The switch may be mounted on a support or linkage. That possibility is not a universal placement rule. A drawing or service description for the specific design must establish where the support, linkage, actuator, and switch body are in relation to one another. If the documentation does not show a complete actuation path, the available evidence may not be enough to identify the switch’s exact location. Avoid filling gaps by assuming that a nearby component is the end-of-stroke switch.

Use the physical trace to form a location candidate, then compare that candidate with the machine’s documentation. A component’s shape or apparent position alone is not confirmation. In particular, do not treat every small electrical component near a moving part as a micro switch: it could serve another purpose or be a different type of component. The documentation-led method narrows the search by connecting a specified endpoint to a specified actuator and switch, while leaving unidentified parts unresolved.

## Confirm the switch’s role without inferring circuit behavior

After tracing the mechanical path, consult the wiring diagram or service documentation to verify that the identified component is designated as the end-of-stroke micro switch. Use the exact switch and equipment documentation rather than relying on a generic physical-layout inference. The mechanical drawing can help explain how motion reaches an actuator; the wiring diagram or service information is needed to establish the component’s documented electrical role.

Physical position does not establish contact state or circuit function. Do not infer whether contacts are normally open or normally closed, whether a common terminal is used, or how the control circuit interprets a contact change unless the applicable documentation says so. Nor does the presence of a switch at a motion endpoint establish that the centrifuge stops, completes a cycle, or follows any particular sequence. Those behaviors depend on the specific machine’s design and control system.

Likewise, apparent actuator movement or an audible click is not proof of electrical operation. It does not establish continuity, correct wiring, or the machine’s response. Identifying where the switch sits and confirming its documented role are narrower tasks than determining whether the entire sensing and control system works. This location method does not diagnose a centrifuge that fails to stop or complete a cycle.

If the service information and wiring diagram do not agree, or if the documentation does not identify the component and its role, do not resolve the uncertainty by guessing from location. The exact relationship remains unconfirmed until the design-specific information is clarified by an appropriate service source. This boundary prevents a plausible physical match from being mistaken for evidence about circuit behavior.

## Use design-specific safety instructions before inspection

Before any inspection near the mechanism, follow the centrifuge’s applicable isolation and access procedure. The safe method depends on the particular machine and its documentation; a general location guide cannot replace those instructions. Do not reach into or operate exposed machinery to watch for movement or to find which part contacts a switch. Do not bypass or adjust components as part of locating them.

If the service documentation does not establish a safe way to access the relevant area, or does not establish the component’s identity, stop short of inspecting it and leave the task to qualified service personnel. The location should be treated as confirmed only to the extent that the design-specific documentation supports it. The **centrifuge end of stroke micro switch location** is a documented relationship between a particular moving mechanism, its actuation path, and a particular switch, not a standard coordinate or a conclusion about the machine’s electrical behavior.
