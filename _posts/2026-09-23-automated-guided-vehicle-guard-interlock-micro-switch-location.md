The **automated guided vehicle guard interlock micro switch location** depends on how the particular interlock is built. The switch, when the design specifies one, is a sensing component within the guard-interlock arrangement. It is not the guard itself, the actuator that moves with or responds to the guard, or the control device that processes the switch signal. There is no universal mounting point, orientation, or distance that applies to every assembly.

To locate it, follow the mechanical path: begin with the moving guard, identify the part that actuates the interlock, then trace that part to the switch’s operating point. Confirm the intended position and relationship using the drawings and approved documentation for the equipment. A switch indication may show that the switch changed state, but it does not alone prove that the complete safety function has been validated.

## What “automated guided vehicle guard interlock micro switch location” means

This question concerns where a micro switch sits in a particular guard-interlock assembly, not where the vehicle’s navigation or obstacle-detection sensors are located. Those are separate systems and are outside this location question. The switch’s role, if present, is to respond to movement transmitted through the interlock’s mechanical arrangement. The guard, actuator, switch, wiring, and safety-control device are distinct parts, even when they are packaged close together.

Not every guard interlock uses a micro switch. Some designs may use a different arrangement, so first establish from the equipment and interlock documentation that a micro switch is actually specified. Do not infer its presence from the appearance of the guard or housing, or assume that a visible electrical component is the relevant switch.

The word “location” can also refer to different things: the switch’s physical position, the point where the actuator operates it, or the remote control device that receives its signal. For finding the switch, focus on its physical position and operating point within or alongside the interlock assembly. A remote indication or controller location does not identify where the switch is mounted.

## Trace the guard-to-micro-switch actuation path

Start at the guard and identify how it moves in the documented assembly. Next, identify the interlock part that the guard moves or engages. That part may serve as the actuator, but its exact form and relationship to the guard depend on the design. Follow its movement into or against the interlock housing. The switch’s operating point is where the documented actuator path acts on the switch to produce its intended response.

This tracing method separates mechanical actuation from electrical signaling. The switch is not the guard, the actuator, the wiring, or the safety-control device. A control unit or remote indication may show a response to a switch signal, but it is not the switch’s physical location. Keeping those components separate helps prevent a display, cable, or housing feature from being mistaken for the sensing point.

Use the assembly drawing or other approved documentation to confirm the path rather than inferring it from a generic layout. If the actuator cannot be followed clearly, or the documented assembly does not match what is present, the location remains unresolved until the discrepancy is reviewed. Do not guess based on a typical interlock shape or move parts to discover an assumed operating point.

## Where the micro switch may be mounted in the assembly

Depending on the interlock construction, a specified micro switch may be mounted inside the interlock housing or adjacent to it. These are design-dependent possibilities, not universal placements. The housing, the actuator’s permitted movement, and the switch’s applicable operating limits all constrain where the switch can be positioned. The documentation for the actual assembly determines which arrangement applies.

Do not infer a mounting orientation, distance, or numerical travel limit from the fact that a component is a micro switch. Numerical travel limits are requirements of the applicable specification sheet, not values that can be assumed from the component category. The applicable specification and interlock documentation must establish the relevant limits and intended actuator relationship.

The release force, also called releasing force, has a specific meaning in MIL-PRF-8805K 6.5.17: it is the force or torque to which force on the actuator must be reduced to permit the contacts to return to the unoperated position after operation. This definition describes a switch characteristic; it does not provide a universal mounting location or establish how a particular guard interlock is assembled. Apply it only where that standard’s definition is relevant to the specified component.

## How to verify the location and alignment safely

Use the equipment and interlock drawings, installation instructions, applicable specification sheet, and approved service procedure to identify the intended mounting position and actuator relationship. Follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. If the documentation does not identify the switch or its position clearly, do not substitute a generic layout or assumed dimension; have the unresolved point reviewed through the appropriate qualified service process.

With the system in a safe state, inspect whether the installed switch and actuator arrangement correspond to the documented assembly. Check the specified relationship and alignment without defeating or bypassing the guard function. Do not force the guard or actuator beyond the prescribed procedure, and do not treat an improvised movement test as a substitute for approved validation.

Use the prescribed validation procedure to check guard movement and interlock response. The wiring diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load must be taken from documentation for the exact switch and equipment. A generic physical-layout inference is not enough to identify terminals or establish ratings. If the assembly is damaged, differs from its documentation, or cannot be inspected or tested as prescribed, stop treating its location as confirmed and seek qualified review.

## What location alone cannot confirm

Finding the switch, or observing a change in its contact state, does not by itself establish that the complete safety-related control function is validated. Switch actuation is only one part of the arrangement. Wiring integrity, control logic, fault response, and required system-level validation are separate matters that cannot be confirmed from the switch’s physical position alone.

Likewise, a remote indication is evidence of an indicated response, not proof that every part of the guard-interlock function operates as required. Any conclusion about the system must follow the applicable documentation and approved validation procedure, not just a visible switch or a basic movement check. If a test fails, the assembly differs from its approved documentation, or the intended location remains unclear, obtain qualified review rather than inferring that the function is satisfactory.

The **automated guided vehicle guard interlock micro switch location** is therefore established by the documented actuation path and assembly-specific mounting details, not by a universal position. Trace the guard through the actuator to the switch operating point, verify that relationship against approved documentation, and keep switch location distinct from system-level safety validation.
