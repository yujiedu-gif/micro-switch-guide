For **dock leveler guard interlock micro switch adjustment**, the decision is not to move a switch merely because the interlock behaves unexpectedly or a click can be heard. Follow the specific equipment procedure for the intended guard position and adjustment method. If that target, the safe isolation requirements, or the switch state to be checked is unclear, stop and seek qualified service. A micro switch may be actuated by a guard mechanism in some dock leveler designs, but this arrangement is not universal.

The key is to separate the guard’s mechanical alignment, the movement delivered to the switch actuator, the switch’s operating and release events, and the control system’s response. These are related, but they are not interchangeable adjustments or observations. A switch-state result alone cannot establish that the assembled interlock behaves correctly.

## What dock leveler guard interlock micro switch adjustment actually changes

In a design that uses a micro switch for a guard interlock, movement of the guard may reach the switch through direct contact, a linkage, or another part of the mechanism. The guard is the moving equipment part; the actuator is the part of the switch moved by that mechanism. The switch changes state as its actuator moves through the relevant operating event, and it changes again as the mechanism returns through the release event.

Adjustment changes the relationship between guard movement and switch operation, as defined by that equipment’s design. It does not, by itself, prove that the guard is aligned correctly, that the linkage moves as intended, or that the host control system responds correctly. A mismatch could involve more than switch position, so treat adjustment as a documented, design-specific task rather than a general fix for a symptom.

Do not assume every dock leveler has a guard interlock, uses a micro switch, or uses the same actuation arrangement. The interlock label does not establish a terminal layout, contact state, adjustment range, or control behavior. Those details remain specific to the switch and equipment documentation.

## Before dock leveler guard interlock micro switch adjustment: identify the documented target

Use the instructions for the specific equipment to establish the intended guard position, the prescribed adjustment method, and the expected switch state at the defined position. Identify the switch actuator and the relevant mounting point from that documentation. Do not infer terminal function from where a terminal sits physically or assume a contact arrangement from the interlock’s name.

The connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment are the relevant references for terminal identification and applicability. A generic layout cannot establish which connection applies. Likewise, a state label has meaning only at its documented reference condition; do not assign an expected state without knowing that condition.

Before any assessment or adjustment, follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements and the equipment’s prescribed steps for isolating and securing it. Do not substitute a guessed procedure for missing instructions. If the target guard position, adjustment method, safe isolation, or terminal identity is uncertain, do not move or test the switch on a trial-and-error basis. Request qualified service.

## Trace guard movement to the micro switch operating and release points

Assess the actuation path in sequence, using the equipment’s prescribed procedure: guard movement, any intervening linkage or contact, actuator movement, switch operation, and release as the guard returns. The purpose is to locate where the observed sequence differs from the documented one, not to assume in advance that the switch mounting position is the cause.

Guard alignment and switch position are separate matters. The guard can move relative to its intended position, or a linkage can affect how movement reaches the actuator. The actuator’s movement is the switch input; the switch’s operating point is where its operation occurs, while its release point is a distinct event on return. Overtravel has a specific definition: the distance or angle between the operating position and the extreme position to which the actuator may be moved. This definition does not provide a permitted adjustment range for a particular dock leveler or switch.

A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not establish electrical continuity, identify the contact state, or verify the control system response. Listen for or observe an event only as part of the documented assessment. Do not use the presence or absence of a click as a sufficient reason to shift a mounting point. If guard motion, linkage motion, or actuator contact does not match the documented sequence, preserve that distinction when describing the problem to qualified service rather than attributing it automatically to switch position.

## Verify switch state separately from dock leveler control response

A switch-state check and an assembled-interlock check answer different questions. An isolated-switch continuity check concerns the switch under a defined actuator state; it does not establish the condition or behavior of the surrounding circuit. Perform such a check only when the equipment-specific safe procedure permits it and the relevant contact arrangement, actuator state, measuring method, and expected behavior are known.

Compare any permitted result with the documented behavior at that defined state. COM means common, and NO and NC describe contact state at a specified reference condition; without the applicable arrangement and reference condition, those labels do not establish what a particular terminal should show. Do not infer the expected contact state from the guard’s general description or from a click.

The control system’s response must be evaluated separately, under the equipment-specific procedure. Circuit logic and safety behavior cannot be inferred from the fact that a micro switch is called an interlock. A switch may change state while the assembled system responds differently than expected, or the system may have a problem outside the switch. Do not treat a component reading as proof that the guard, wiring, controls, or complete interlock is correct. The permissible load and connection details must also come from the documentation for the exact switch and equipment, not from a generic diagram.

## When to stop and request qualified service

Stop the assessment if guard movement, the actuator sequence, terminal identity, a permitted switch-state check, or the control response conflicts with the equipment documentation. Also stop if the instructions do not establish the intended target or safe method, or if the work requires an undocumented adjustment. These are boundaries for escalation, not evidence that one particular component has failed.

Do not bypass the interlock, improvise wiring, or move the switch until an audible click occurs. Do not infer safe operation from an isolated reading or from an apparent change in switch state. Keep the observations distinct: what the guard did, how movement reached the actuator, whether operation and release were observed, what a permitted isolated check showed, and how the assembled system responded under its prescribed procedure.

The adjustment decision is therefore conditional: proceed only by the specific equipment procedure, with its documented target and required safeguards. If the procedure or results leave the mechanical cause, terminal identity, or system behavior uncertain, request qualified service rather than treating **dock leveler guard interlock micro switch adjustment** as a universal setting or repair.
