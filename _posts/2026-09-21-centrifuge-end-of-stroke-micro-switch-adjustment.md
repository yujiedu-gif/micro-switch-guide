A centrifuge end of stroke micro switch adjustment concerns the point at which the installed mechanism actuates its switch near the end of a stroke. It is specific to the centrifuge and documented switch assembly: not every centrifuge uses this arrangement, and no universal adjustment position or control response can be assumed. Before changing anything, trace how the moving mechanism reaches the switch actuator and confirm the documented adjustment method, travel limits, and applicable safety procedure. The switch’s mechanical actuation, its electrical contact behavior, and the centrifuge’s response are separate questions. Checking one does not establish the others.

## What centrifuge end of stroke micro switch adjustment can and cannot establish

Adjustment, where the installed design provides for it, changes when the mechanism actuates the micro switch in relation to the stroke. It does not by itself establish that the switch is electrically sound, correctly connected, or producing the expected equipment response. Those conclusions require evidence for the exact switch and centrifuge.

An endpoint function does not prove that a centrifuge uses a micro switch. If documentation identifies one, it should also establish the switch assembly’s role and how its contact behavior relates to the equipment. The effect of a contact change depends on the documented circuit and equipment design; it cannot be inferred from the fact that the switch sits near a stroke endpoint.

Accordingly, first identify the actual arrangement. Do not treat a visible switch, a moving linkage, or a reported endpoint symptom as proof of a particular normal state, terminal assignment, or control action. The task is to assess the documented mechanical actuation point, then verify switch behavior and equipment response separately under the applicable procedure.

## Trace the endpoint mechanism to the micro switch actuator

Start with the centrifuge’s documentation and identify the moving component whose stroke is being monitored. Follow its mechanical path to the switch actuator. The path may include a cam, lever, or linkage, but the actual arrangement must come from the equipment documentation or direct identification of that documented assembly. Do not assume that a particular component or geometry is present.

Observe how the identified mechanism approaches and contacts the actuator, using the procedure permitted for the equipment. The question is whether the moving part reaches the actuator in the intended relationship at the documented endpoint. If it does not, a position or alignment issue in the mechanical path may be relevant. Lost motion or an obstruction may also affect whether the actuator is reached. These are possible mechanical causes, not diagnoses established by the symptom alone.

This distinction matters because changing the switch position cannot necessarily correct a problem elsewhere in the mechanism. If the moving part fails to reach the actuator as intended, identify why before treating switch adjustment as the remedy. Conversely, apparent actuator movement does not establish that the switch contacts change as expected. Keep mechanical observation separate from electrical testing and from interpretation of the centrifuge’s response.

Do not infer that an endpoint signal is missing because the switch position is wrong. A symptom can arise from the mechanical path, the switch, terminal identification, wiring, or another part of the equipment. The documentation and controlled checks must narrow those possibilities before an adjustment is justified.

## Use documented operating and release points to assess adjustment

The operating point and release point are distinct events. The operating point is where the switch operates as the actuator moves; the release point is where it returns as the actuator moves back. Their separation is related to differential travel. Therefore, an endpoint assessment should consider both the intended actuation and the return behavior, when the documented procedure calls for checking them. A single observed movement does not define both events.

For travel terminology, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These boundaries do not supply a setting for a particular centrifuge. Use only the adjustment direction, range, actuator state, and travel limits documented for the installed assembly. If those details are unavailable, do not substitute a general setting or infer a safe amount of movement from the switch’s appearance.

Where adjustment is documented, relate the setting to the mechanism’s position at the specified stroke endpoint. The goal is not to force the actuator farther or to make the switch operate at an assumed universal point. Respect the permitted actuator travel and any equipment-specific limits. If the procedure does not establish how the adjustment is made or what endpoint condition is expected, the evidence is insufficient to prescribe a change.

A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not establish the operating point electrically, prove contact continuity, or show that the centrifuge received the intended signal. Treat sound or feel only as a mechanical observation, not as a pass result.

## Verify switch behavior separately from centrifuge response

Use the centrifuge’s documented isolation and verification procedure, including its applicable safety requirements. Do not improvise live adjustment or testing instructions. If the safe procedure, the identity of the switch assembly, or the permitted test method cannot be confirmed, stop and refer the work to qualified service.

An isolated continuity check is appropriate only where the equipment and switch documentation permit it. Define the actuator state being tested and the expected contact behavior from the documentation for that exact switch. A reading has meaning only in that stated condition and with the switch isolated as required by the procedure. Do not infer installed-circuit safety or correct equipment behavior from an isolated reading alone.

Keep three observations separate: whether the mechanism reaches and moves the actuator, whether the isolated switch behaves as documented in the specified actuator state, and whether the centrifuge responds as its equipment documentation specifies. A click addresses, at most, possible mechanical movement. A continuity result addresses the isolated switch in the tested state. Neither alone proves that wiring, the installed circuit, or the wider control system functions correctly.

If the documented switch behavior is correct but the endpoint response remains wrong, do not keep moving the switch to compensate without evidence. Wiring or another equipment-level cause may be involved. Terminal identification and permissible load must come from the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and documentation for the exact switch and equipment, not from a generic inference based on physical layout. Electrical or equipment-level diagnosis beyond the documented procedure belongs with qualified service.

## Decide when adjustment is not the supported remedy

Do not proceed if the switch arrangement cannot be confirmed, the adjustment method or travel limits are undocumented, the mechanism-to-actuator path is unclear, or the applicable safe procedure is unavailable. These are limits on what can be established, not reasons to try a guessed position.

A single observation or test does not prove that the complete centrifuge assembly is safe or functional. If the mechanical path, isolated switch behavior, and documented equipment response do not agree, preserve those distinctions and escalate the unresolved cause rather than assuming that adjustment is sufficient. The centrifuge end of stroke micro switch adjustment is supported only when the specific assembly, its allowed adjustment, and its verification procedure are known; otherwise, qualified service is the appropriate boundary.
