For painting robot end of stroke micro switch maintenance, first confirm that the particular robot actually uses a micro switch at the end-of-stroke point. Maintain that component within the robot’s approved procedure; do not treat a switch inspection as a general robot, spray-system, or paint-booth service. The switch is a component actuated by the robot mechanism. Its contacts provide an input, but the input’s effect depends on the documented circuit and control logic. A click or an isolated contact reading alone cannot confirm that the robot system functions correctly.

## What painting robot end of stroke micro switch maintenance covers

Begin by identifying the specific end-of-stroke switch from the robot’s equipment documentation. Do not infer that a robot uses a micro switch simply because it has an end-of-stroke function. If documentation confirms one, keep the maintenance scope on that switch and the path that actuates it.

The robot mechanism moves the switch actuator. The switch’s contacts then provide an electrical input to the relevant circuit. The control system determines what that input means and what, if anything, follows. The switch, its actuator, the surrounding mechanism, wiring, control input, and painting equipment are distinct parts of the system. A fault or unexpected result cannot be assigned to the switch based only on the fact that the event occurs at the end of a stroke.

This is a component-focused maintenance guide, not a general procedure for servicing a robot or painting system. Use the equipment documentation to establish what switch is present, what inspection is authorized, and how the end-of-stroke function is verified. If that information is unavailable, do not fill gaps with assumptions about the switch’s contact arrangement, wiring, function, or service needs.

## Start with the robot's approved isolation and maintenance procedure

Before inspecting or manipulating the switch, consult the robot’s applicable maintenance documentation. It should identify the relevant switch and actuator, the circuit and safe state, and the required authorization and isolation boundaries. Follow the applicable procedure for controlling hazards from robot motion, electrical energy, and the painting system. Do not approach or move the mechanism if those hazards have not been controlled as required.

This article does not prescribe a lockout method, a safe state, or a maintenance interval. Those details must come from the documentation and requirements applicable to the particular equipment and work. If the procedure does not authorize a check, or the switch and circuit cannot be identified, stop at that boundary and seek qualified assistance rather than improvising.

Keep the work limited to the approved switch inspection. A switch may sit near paint or cleaning agents, but their presence does not establish that residue caused a problem, nor does it authorize applying a particular cleaner. Any cleaning method, if permitted at all, must be specified by the applicable equipment procedure. Do not extend switch maintenance into general robot, spray-system, or booth servicing.

## Inspect the end-of-stroke actuator path before judging the switch

Within the approved procedure, inspect the switch mounting and the mechanical path that brings the robot mechanism to the actuator. Check whether the mounting appears secure, whether the actuator can move as intended, and whether the mechanism reaches the documented actuation point. Do not force the actuator or move the robot mechanism unless the procedure permits that action and the required controls are in place.

Look for visible damage, obstruction, or paint residue that could affect movement. These are observations to assess, not proof of a cause. A mechanism that does not reach or move the actuator presents a different possibility from a switch that is actuated but does not produce the expected electrical input. Keep those possibilities separate until the permitted checks provide evidence.

Pretravel and overtravel describe portions of actuator travel when those terms apply to the switch design: pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. This distinction does not establish the travel values or adjustment requirements for the switch in a particular robot. Use only the actual equipment documentation for those details, and do not make an adjustment based on a generic definition.

## Check contacts and connections only under documented conditions

A click can indicate movement of a snap-action mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, contact condition, correct wiring, or correct operation of the robot input. Do not treat an audible or tactile response as a complete electrical check.

If the approved procedure permits an isolated contact check, first use the connection diagram and terminal markings documented for the exact switch and equipment. Do not infer terminal identity from physical layout or assume a COM, NO, or NC arrangement. Those labels and contact states are meaningful only when documented for the switch and the specified reference condition.

An isolated check also needs defined conditions: the required isolation, the actuator state being tested, the measurement method, and the expected result. Take those details from the applicable documentation. Without them, a reading cannot be interpreted reliably. Do not use an isolated reading to infer that installed wiring is correct, that the control input responds, or that the robot is safe to operate.

Inspect terminal and wiring condition only within the authorized procedure. Use the documented circuit function and permissible load for the exact switch and equipment rather than assuming a rating or function from appearance. If terminal identification, test conditions, or expected behavior are not documented, leave them unresolved and refer the issue to qualified personnel.

## Separate switch symptoms from wiring and robot-control faults

An end-of-stroke input that appears absent or inconsistent can have several possible causes. The mechanism may not reach the actuator, the actuator may not move as intended, the switch contacts may not change state, a terminal or wiring issue may interrupt the signal, or the robot control input or logic may not respond as expected. These are different parts of the path, not interchangeable diagnoses.

Use only observations and checks authorized by the equipment procedure to distinguish among them. For example, inspection of the mechanism’s approach to the actuator can address whether the actuation path appears to reach its intended point. A documented, isolated contact check can address the switch contacts under the stated test conditions. Neither observation alone verifies the wiring, control input, or complete robot function.

Do not conclude that the switch has failed merely because the robot’s end-of-stroke behavior is unexpected. Equally, do not conclude that the switch is sound because it clicks or passes an isolated check. If diagnosis requires testing wiring, control logic, or equipment behavior beyond the approved switch inspection, refer that work to qualified personnel under the applicable procedure.

## Verify the maintenance result through the approved return-to-service process

After any permitted inspection or maintenance, use the robot’s documented return-to-service process and functional check. That process must establish whether the end-of-stroke input is recognized and whether the resulting behavior matches the documented control behavior. Do not infer either result from actuator movement, a click, or an isolated continuity reading.

If results are inconsistent, a test condition is unclear, or the input and robot behavior do not match the equipment documentation, record the unresolved observation and follow the procedure’s direction for the equipment’s status. Do not claim the switch or system is repaired based on a partial check.

The maintenance boundary is therefore straightforward: confirm the switch’s identity and authorized work, inspect its mechanical actuation path, perform electrical checks only under documented conditions, and leave wiring or control-system diagnosis to the appropriate procedure and personnel. Painting robot end of stroke micro switch maintenance is not complete merely because the switch moves or clicks; system-level verification must follow the robot’s approved return-to-service process.
