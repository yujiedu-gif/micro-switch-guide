A power shear guard interlock micro switch replacement should be approved only after the equipment documentation establishes that the interlock uses a micro switch and that the replacement meets the documented requirements. A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement and is commonly used for position or limit detection, but that general function does not identify a particular interlock design.

Appearance alone cannot establish suitability. Verification must account for the switch and its mechanical interface, the documented contact and electrical requirements, and the equipment’s specified safe service and post-service verification procedures. A switch-only check cannot establish that the guard, wiring, control circuit, or complete safety function operates as intended.

## What must match in a power shear guard interlock micro switch replacement?

First confirm from the equipment documentation that the specific power shear guard interlock is designed with a micro switch. Power shear designs vary, so the application name does not establish that a micro switch is present or identify how one is used. If documentation does not establish the switch type or requirements, do not treat an apparently similar part as an approved replacement.

The replacement decision depends on documented requirements for both the switch and its interface with the equipment. That includes the mechanical relationship through which the guard or linkage moves the actuator, along with the documented contact arrangement, terminal identification, and permissible load for the applicable circuit. These are separate checks: matching a mechanical feature does not establish electrical suitability, and matching an electrical description does not establish that the switch will be actuated as intended.

Do not infer the contact arrangement, normal state, or compatibility from the interlock’s purpose, the switch’s physical layout, wire positions, or resemblance to an installed part. The equipment connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment are the basis for identifying connections and requirements. A description such as “guard switch” alone does not establish whether a contact is normally open or normally closed at a specified reference state.

If the required documentation is unavailable or does not resolve a relevant requirement, the evidence is insufficient to approve the replacement. Keep the machine within the equipment’s specified safe service process while the uncertainty is addressed. Do not use a trial installation or an isolated switch reading as a substitute for documented compatibility and verification.

## Identify the switch-to-guard mechanical interface

Establish how the documented switch actuator interacts with the guard or linkage. The actuator is the part moved by an external mechanism. The equipment documentation must support the actuator type and the intended movement through the switch’s operating range; a visual match between two switch bodies does not establish that their actuators respond correctly to the same mechanism.

Keep the switch’s actuation separate from guard position, mounting, and alignment. The guard or linkage supplies the mechanical movement; the actuator receives it; the switch changes contact state according to its design. Whether the intended movement occurs depends on their actual relationship in that equipment. An apparent fit does not by itself establish correct alignment or intended operation.

A mismatch could involve the switch interface or how the guard or linkage reaches it. These are different possibilities, not a diagnosis from appearance alone. The relevant documentation must establish the intended relationship. Do not assume that another power shear uses the same mounting, linkage, guard arrangement, or actuation path. The application category does not provide those design details.

If the documented interface cannot be identified, do not infer it from the position of an old switch or nearby parts. A switch that appears to fit may not receive the required actuator movement, while an apparent movement does not prove that the contact changes as specified. Mechanical interface and electrical behavior therefore need distinct evidence before suitability can be established.

## Confirm contact and electrical requirements from documentation

Use the equipment documentation and the documentation for the exact switch to establish the contact arrangement, terminal identification, and permissible load for the applicable circuit. A micro switch can change an electrical contact state in response to actuator movement, but that general description does not establish which terminals are used or what state the interlock requires.

COM means common, and NO and NC describe contact states only when the actual contact configuration and reference state are documented. Do not assume a reference state or infer terminal function from physical position. Wire location on an existing assembly is not enough to identify terminals reliably, and a diagram for a different switch or equipment design cannot establish the requirements for this one.

Ratings and contact behavior must remain tied to the exact documented switch, equipment, circuit function, and applicable load. Do not transfer a rating from an unrelated switch or assume that a similarly shaped part has the required electrical characteristics. If the documentation does not establish the relevant load or terminal information, the replacement remains unverified.

A wiring diagram is useful only within its stated scope. Follow the connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. Keep electrical work within the equipment’s applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. Do not treat a terminal description or component-level match as proof that the complete circuit is correctly connected or suitable for service.

## Keep replacement verification separate from a continuity check

An isolated continuity test can address the switch alone only when the test condition is defined. That means identifying the actuator position and switch state being tested, and knowing the expected result from reliable documentation. Without those conditions, a continuity reading does not establish that the switch is behaving as required for the equipment.

A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, correct terminal identification, suitable electrical characteristics, or correct installation. Likewise, a single continuity result cannot establish that the guard is aligned, the wiring is correct, the control circuit responds as intended, or the interlock’s safety function is complete.

Use the equipment’s specified isolation and post-service verification process. Do not add live testing steps or substitute a component-only check for that process. Keep the work within the equipment’s stated safe procedure and applicable qualification requirements; refer work outside that boundary to qualified personnel. The available evidence does not support a universal test sequence for every power shear interlock design.

Verification must cover the boundaries that a switch-only test leaves unresolved. The switch’s documented actuation and contact behavior are distinct from the guard’s mechanical alignment and the surrounding wiring and control logic. A test result for one element cannot establish the correct operation of the others. Follow the equipment-specific verification procedure rather than treating a successful isolated reading as approval to return the machine to service.

## When the evidence is insufficient to approve a replacement

Do not approve a replacement when the switch identity, mechanical interface, electrical requirements, or equipment-specific verification procedure cannot be established. These are meaningful gaps, not details that can safely be filled by resemblance or assumption. Stop at the documented decision boundary and have unresolved work handled through the applicable service process.

A reported problem may arise from the switch, its actuator, guard or linkage alignment, terminal identification, wiring, or another part of the control circuit. Those possibilities are distinct. A symptom by itself does not show which one is responsible, and a continuity result from an isolated switch does not resolve all of them. Follow the equipment’s specified verification process to distinguish the relevant conditions; do not infer a complete diagnosis from a single observation.

Do not bypass or defeat the guard interlock. Do not return the machine to service based only on physical resemblance, a click, or an isolated switch test. If the required documentation or safe verification procedure is missing, or if the work goes beyond that procedure, leave the replacement unapproved and refer the matter to qualified personnel under the equipment’s applicable service requirements.

The decision is therefore conditional: a power shear guard interlock micro switch replacement is supportable only when the equipment’s documentation establishes the switch and interface requirements, the documented electrical requirements are met, and the specified safe verification process is completed. A component match alone does not establish that the guard interlock or machine safety function works as intended.
