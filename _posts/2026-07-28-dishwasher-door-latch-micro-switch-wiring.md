A dishwasher door latch micro switch wiring question cannot be answered with a universal wire-color or pin-position rule. First establish whether the particular latch design uses a micro switch, then identify that switch from its documentation and distinguish its terminals from the appliance harness and control circuit.

A micro switch is an electromechanical component, not the latch assembly or the dishwasher’s control logic. The guidance below is limited to designs that use one in the latch. Use the connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment. If those details are unavailable or uncertain, the wiring remains unidentified; do not fill the gap with a generic layout.

## Dishwasher door latch micro switch wiring: what can be identified safely

There is no universal dishwasher latch wiring pattern established here. The first task is to locate documentation that applies to the specific switch and appliance, and to confirm that the latch design actually contains a micro switch. Some designs may use one; that fact cannot be generalized to all dishwashers or latches.

Keep three layers separate. The micro switch has a contact arrangement and terminal markings. The latch assembly is the mechanical part that may move the switch actuator. The appliance harness and control circuit determine how the switch is connected and what a contact change means to the dishwasher. Knowing the switch terminals does not, by itself, identify the harness wires or establish the appliance’s operating behavior.

For identification, rely on the exact switch’s diagram and markings together with the equipment documentation. Do not infer a connection from a wire’s color, a connector’s apparent position, or the physical placement of terminals. A physical layout is not a substitute for documented terminal identity or circuit function.

Ratings also have a specific scope. Use only the rating stated for the applicable current type, load, and test condition. An AC rating does not establish a DC rating, and a rating for one load or test condition does not establish another. If the applicable rating or connection information is missing, the evidence is insufficient to determine whether a proposed connection is suitable.

## How latch motion can operate a micro switch

In a latch design that uses a micro switch, movement of the latch mechanism may move the switch actuator. That mechanical input can cause a contact change in the switch. The precise movement and contact behavior belong to the documented design; the presence of a switch alone does not show what the dishwasher does with that change.

Keep the mechanical event distinct from the electrical consequence. The latch may move an actuator, and the switch may change its contact state, but the appliance circuit determines how that state is used. The available information does not establish a universal door-interlock arrangement, normal contact state, or shutdown behavior. Do not infer that a dishwasher stops, starts, or indicates a particular condition simply because a latch-mounted switch is present.

Where the specific design documentation describes actuator travel, use its terms carefully. Pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These descriptions do not supply a travel value or prove how a particular latch moves the actuator. They are useful only when the applicable documentation identifies the relevant positions and design.

A click may indicate mechanical movement, but it does not establish electrical continuity, correct terminal identification, correct harness wiring, or appliance function. A switch can move while the cause of an appliance symptom lies in its terminals, the harness, the control circuit, or elsewhere. Mechanical observation alone cannot distinguish those possibilities.

## Identify COM, NO, and NC from the switch documentation

COM, NO, and NC are not universal promises about a switch’s physical layout or the appliance’s wires. A generic changeover contact may be labeled common, normally closed, and normally open, but the exact device marking or schematic controls. Confirm that the documented arrangement applies to the switch being examined before using those names.

Where that arrangement is documented, COM means the common terminal. NO and NC describe contact states relative to the stated reference condition. The reference condition matters: do not treat “normally” as an unqualified description of every latch position or of current flow in the installed dishwasher. Use the device documentation to establish the reference state and the expected contact behavior.

Then keep terminal names separate from harness identification. A label on the switch identifies a switch terminal only within the documented arrangement. It does not identify which appliance connector pin or wire should connect there unless the equipment connection diagram establishes that relationship. Do not translate COM, NO, or NC into a wire color, connector position, or connection recipe by assumption.

Use the documented connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load for the exact switch and equipment as a set. Check the applicable specification sheet for ratings, and preserve its current type, load, and test-condition limits. A value stated for one condition cannot establish another. If the diagram or marking is unclear, or the switch cannot be matched to its documentation, terminal identification remains unresolved.

## Verify the isolated switch without treating it as an appliance diagnosis

Continuity testing is limited to the switch itself and a defined actuator state. Before any test, require power isolation and a suitable documented procedure. The applicable deenergization and qualification requirements must be followed; this explanation is not a substitute for appliance-specific safety instructions. If safe isolation or the appropriate procedure is uncertain, stop rather than test or reconnect by guesswork.

Only describe a continuity check as meaningful when the switch is isolated from the circuit, the actuator state is defined, and the expected contact behavior comes from the applicable documentation. Compare the measured result with that documented expectation. Do not invent a test state or assume that a switch should read open or closed in an unspecified latch position.

A click is not a continuity result. It may show that movement occurred, but it does not establish that the contacts behave as documented. Conversely, an isolated reading at a defined state addresses only the switch under that test condition. It does not establish harness integrity, identify an appliance connector pin, prove control-circuit behavior, or determine overall appliance safety.

Keep the test result narrow: it can be reported as an observation about the isolated switch in the tested state, compared with the expected behavior in the documentation. It cannot alone diagnose the dishwasher. If the switch result matches the documented expectation, that still does not rule out a harness or other appliance problem. If it does not match, the result does not by itself establish why; first ensure the switch identity, isolation, actuator state, and test procedure were correct.

## When wiring remains uncertain

Do not reconnect wires based on resemblance, color, terminal position, or a presumed standard arrangement. If terminal identity, the applicable diagram, or safe isolation cannot be established, the wiring question is unresolved. A qualified person can address work that is outside the documented safe procedure; do not extend an isolated-switch check into unsupported appliance-level electrical work.

Separate possible fault paths rather than treating every symptom as a failed switch. The issue could involve mechanical input to the actuator, the switch contacts, terminal identification, the harness, control behavior, or another part of the appliance. The evidence available from a click or one isolated continuity reading does not determine which path applies. Use the exact documentation and an appropriate verification procedure to distinguish them; if that information is unavailable, leave the cause open.

The practical boundary is straightforward: identify the switch from its own markings and documentation, identify the appliance wiring from the equipment diagram, and interpret any test only under its documented conditions. Dishwasher door latch micro switch wiring cannot be safely inferred from a generic contact diagram or from the presence of a latch switch alone. When the documentation does not establish the connection or the procedure, do not guess.
