## What bifurcated contacts mean in a micro switch

“Bifurcated” describes a divided or branched contact geometry. In a micro switch, the term concerns the shape or arrangement of a contact element. It does not, on its own, identify which terminals are connected, the switch’s actuator shape, or the state of the contacts at a given point in operation. The accepted evidence does not provide a more detailed physical definition, such as a required number of branches or a prescribed contact profile, so those details should not be inferred from the word alone.

This distinction matters because several different features can be visible or documented on a switch. Contact geometry describes the contact element. Terminal identification describes the electrical connection points. The actuator is the part moved by an external mechanism. These are related parts of a switch assembly, but one feature does not establish the others. A split-looking feature therefore cannot establish terminal count, wiring, or actuator behavior.

The term also does not specify contact state. A contact’s open or closed state depends on the documented reference condition and the switch’s configuration. Without that information, a description of bifurcation cannot establish which contacts connect in an unactuated or actuated state. Nor does the geometry alone reveal the installed circuit’s response; that depends on the circuit and equipment in which the switch is used.

Use “bifurcated contacts” as a bounded description unless the documentation supplies additional detail. If the exact switch documentation identifies a bifurcated contact design, that supports describing its geometry. If the only evidence is a photograph or an observed split, the intended design remains unresolved: appearance may be consistent with intentional geometry, but it does not confirm it.

## Intended contact geometry versus possible damage

A split appearance can be consistent with intentional bifurcated geometry, so it is not by itself evidence of damage. Conversely, visual appearance alone cannot establish that a contact is sound or performing as intended. A photograph or unaided visual observation may show a shape, but without the design reference it may not distinguish an intended branch from damage or another feature. Do not diagnose failure solely from the fact that a contact looks divided.

Keep possible contact-surface damage separate from faults elsewhere. A problem could concern the contact element, a terminal, wiring, actuator movement, or another part of the equipment. The observed appearance does not identify which fault domain applies. Nor does a symptom in the host equipment prove that the contact geometry caused it: the installed circuit and its other components may affect the result.

This is a limit on diagnosis, not a claim that visual inspection has no value. Avoid converting an observation into a conclusion about electrical performance unless the applicable documentation or a defined test supports it.
