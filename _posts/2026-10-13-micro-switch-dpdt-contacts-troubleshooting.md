A DPDT micro switch has two changeover contact poles, and each pole should be checked independently against the switch’s own terminal diagram and actuator-state documentation. In micro switch DPDT contacts troubleshooting, a continuity reading is meaningful only when the switch is isolated, the terminals are identified, and the actuator condition is recorded. Even then, the result describes the switch under that test condition, not the complete installed circuit.

## Direct answer: check the two DPDT poles independently

DPDT describes two changeover poles. Treat them as two separate contact paths for diagnosis: identify the documented terminals for each pole, test each relevant pair in defined actuator states, and compare the readings with the specific switch’s documentation. Do not assume that the poles share the same condition or that a result from one establishes the behavior of the other.

The terminal mapping and contact state must come from the particular switch’s diagram or markings. Where the documentation identifies common and associated contact terminals, use those identifications rather than inferring terminal function from physical position. Likewise, use documented actuator reference states. An unpressed or pressed position does not, by itself, establish which contact pair should show continuity.

Keep the test boundary clear. An isolated continuity check can help assess the switch in the recorded state. It does not establish that the installed wiring, equipment, or circuit is safe or functional. If the symptom occurs in equipment, the cause could be within the switch or elsewhere, so a component reading alone cannot settle the full diagnosis.

## micro switch DPDT contacts troubleshooting: establish terminals and test conditions

Before checking continuity or resistance, disconnect and isolate the switch from the circuit, following applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. Do not rely on an isolated reading as evidence that the surrounding equipment is safe. If isolation cannot be confirmed, or if the appropriate test method is unclear, stop and seek qualified help rather than improvising a live-circuit procedure.

Use the switch’s own diagram or markings to map the two poles. Record the terminal identifiers for each documented common and its associated contact terminals. If the diagram does not resolve a terminal’s function, leave that identification unresolved; do not fill the gap by guessing from terminal placement or from a generic DPDT layout.

Set up a record before measuring. For each reading, note the terminal pair, which pole it belongs to, the actuator condition, and the observed continuity result. Describe the actuator condition plainly, such as the specific position or condition used, rather than writing only “normal” or “operated” when those terms are not defined by the documentation. The reference state matters because contact behavior is interpreted against the switch’s documented state, not an assumed convention.

This record keeps terminal-identification questions distinct from contact questions. A surprising reading could reflect a mistaken terminal mapping or an actuator condition that does not match the documented reference. Confirm both before treating the reading as evidence of an internal contact problem.

## Check each pole across defined actuator states

With the switch isolated, test only terminal pairs documented for the first pole. Record the actuator condition for each measurement and the observed continuity result. Then repeat the same process for the second pole, keeping its terminal pairs and readings separate. Do not combine results across poles or treat one pole as a substitute for the other.

Compare each observation with the contact states shown in the specific switch’s documentation for the corresponding actuator condition. Do not substitute a general convention for the documented states. If the documentation does not specify the relevant terminal relationship or actuator reference, the evidence is insufficient to label a reading as correct or incorrect.

If a result differs from the documented expectation, first verify that the switch was isolated, the intended terminals were identified, and the actuator reached the recorded condition. Then repeat the measurement under that defined condition if appropriate. Rechecking these facts helps distinguish an uncertain setup from a result that remains inconsistent with the documentation. It does not, by itself, identify the cause of a persistent discrepancy.

Keep the conclusion narrow: state which documented pair was tested, on which pole, in what actuator condition, and what continuity result was observed. Report a discrepancy as a discrepancy under those conditions, rather than declaring the whole switch or installed equipment good or bad from one reading.

## Separate switch symptoms from wiring and equipment faults

A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not establish electrical continuity, correct terminal identification, or suitability for a load. A click and a continuity result answer different questions: the click concerns movement, while the reading concerns the isolated switch’s measured state under the recorded conditions.

Consider possible causes separately. A mechanical input or linkage may not be moving the actuator as intended. Internal contacts may not match the documented behavior under a defined test. Terminals may have been misidentified. Wiring may be responsible for the installed symptom, or a fault may lie elsewhere in the equipment. These are distinct possibilities, not conclusions that follow automatically from a single unexpected reading.

Use the observations to narrow the question without overstating certainty. If both poles are tested against their documentation, a discrepancy on one pole should remain a finding about that pole and test condition; it does not establish the other pole’s condition. If isolated readings agree with the documentation but the equipment still does not behave as expected, the remaining cause may be in wiring or elsewhere in the equipment. The isolated check does not diagnose those parts of the system.

Similarly, a click without the expected measured contact state does not, by itself, identify whether the problem is mechanical, electrical, or in the test setup. Confirm terminal mapping, isolation, and actuator condition first. If those are established and the discrepancy remains, preserve the measured facts and avoid assigning an unsupported cause.

## When the result remains uncertain

Use the switch’s authoritative diagram and the equipment’s circuit information to resolve terminal or state questions. The connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment are the basis for applicability; a generic physical-layout inference is not. Do not extend a continuity result into a claim about load suitability or the entire circuit.

If the result cannot be interpreted from the available documentation, or if safe isolation and testing are uncertain, do not improvise repair or live-circuit steps. Have a qualified person address work outside the established safe procedure. This boundary matters especially when a reading is being used to make a decision about installed equipment rather than the isolated component.

The defensible conclusion is limited to the observations: the documented terminal pair tested, its pole, the actuator condition, and the measured continuity result. In micro switch DPDT contacts troubleshooting, assessing both poles independently can identify where a documented contact-state discrepancy appears, but it cannot prove that the complete switch assembly or installed equipment is sound or faulty.
