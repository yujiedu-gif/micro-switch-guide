## What a gear selector Park-position micro switch does in designs that use one

A micro switch is an electromechanical component. In designs that use one for Park-position detection, selector movement may be transmitted through a lever, cam, linkage, or another mechanical arrangement to the switch actuator. If the actuator moves, the switch can change an electrical contact state. The exact mechanism and the state associated with Park depend on the particular design.

This conditional description matters because the selector, any intermediate mechanical parts, the switch, and the vehicle control circuit are separate parts of the path. Selector movement does not by itself prove that the switch actuator moved. Actuator movement does not by itself prove that the contacts changed state. And a contact-state change does not by itself establish that the vehicle control system received or interpreted the signal as intended.

The contact change’s effect depends on the actual circuit and design. A micro switch’s physical appearance or location cannot establish its terminal functions, wiring, or role in a specific vehicle. For the exact switch and equipment, use the documented connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load. Do not infer terminal identity or circuit behavior from a generic layout.

These distinctions keep the diagnosis focused: first consider whether selector movement reaches the switch actuator; then consider whether the switch changes state; then consider whether the electrical connection and surrounding control system carry and use that state. None of those stages should be treated as a confirmed fault merely because a Park-related symptom is present.

It would not, by itself, identify which part is responsible or establish the intended movement for every design.

## What observations can and cannot establish

Start by separating mechanical observations from electrical ones. Selector movement and visible actuator engagement concern the mechanical path. A contact-state measurement concerns the switch under a defined test condition. An installed-system symptom concerns the switch together with its wiring and the host control system. These are different observations and should not be substituted for one another.

A switch-level electrical result is interpretable only when the test conditions and expected behavior are known. For a continuity check, the switch must be isolated and the actuator state, measurement method, and expected result must be specified.
