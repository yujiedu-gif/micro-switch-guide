A DPDT micro switch has two separate contact groups, so checking its contacts means identifying and testing each pole in defined actuator states. **How to check micro switch DPDT contacts** depends first on the terminal markings or reliable documentation for the exact switch; terminal positions alone do not establish which contacts to measure. The switch must also be isolated from its circuit before you make an isolated continuity check.

Compare the documented contact pairs for each pole with the actuator released and then actuated. Record the terminal pair, actuator state, and test result for every measurement. A click is not an electrical reading, and an isolated continuity result does not establish how the installed circuit or the rest of the equipment behaves. If the switch cannot be safely isolated or its terminal pairs cannot be identified, the check cannot be interpreted reliably within this procedure.

## Direct answer: isolate the switch and check each DPDT pole in two actuator states

A DPDT micro switch has two poles, meaning two separate contact groups that change state in response to actuator movement. Check the documented contact pairs in each group, first with the actuator in a defined released state and then in a defined actuated state. The comparison, rather than a guessed terminal layout or a click, is the basis for interpreting the contact observations.

Before measuring continuity, disconnect the switch from the circuit so the measurement concerns the isolated switch rather than paths through connected equipment. Follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. If you cannot establish safe isolation, do not treat an in-circuit measurement as an isolated contact test; leave work beyond your qualification to qualified personnel.

This method has a limited conclusion: a reading applies to the tested switch, terminal pair, actuator state, and measurement setup. Even readings consistent with the documentation do not prove the installed wiring or host equipment is functioning correctly. Conversely, an unexpected reading by itself does not identify a particular cause.

## Identify the terminals on a DPDT micro switch before testing

Treat the switch as two contact groups and identify each group independently. Where the device documentation identifies a common terminal and normally open and normally closed contacts, use those designations and the documented contact relationships. The labels describe contact states at their specified reference condition; do not assign them from appearance or presume that a particular physical terminal position has a universal meaning.

Use the switch's markings or reliable documentation to determine which terminals belong to the first pole and which belong to the second. Then identify the documented pairs to check within each group. Do not infer a pin arrangement from the shape of the case, terminal spacing, or a layout used by another switch. If markings are unclear or documentation does not establish the pairs, terminal identification remains unresolved, and a continuity reading cannot be confidently assigned to a particular documented contact.

Keep the two poles distinct in your notes. A result for one pole does not stand in for a result for the other, even if the contact groups appear similar. Record each pair by its identified terminal labels or other unambiguous identifiers, together with the source used to identify it. This makes it possible to compare each reading with the correct contact relationship without relying on memory or physical position.

## How to check micro switch DPDT contacts with continuity measurements

With the switch safely isolated and the terminal pairs identified, establish the actuator's released state. Keep the actuator at that defined state while checking each documented pair in the first pole, then check the documented pairs in the second pole. Use the same measurement setup for the comparisons and note what state the actuator was in when each reading was taken.

Next, move the actuator to a defined actuated state and repeat the measurements for the same pairs, one pole at a time. Do not substitute the sound or feel of a click for a continuity observation. A click can indicate mechanical movement, but it does not verify the electrical condition of either pole.

Record results in a simple state-by-state table or equivalent notes. For each observation, include the pole, terminal pair, actuator state, and measurement result. Compare those observations only with the contact behavior documented for that exact switch. The available information here does not establish a universal expected reading, meter threshold, or terminal arrangement, so do not add one. If the documentation does not say what a pair should do in the tested states, the result cannot be judged against an assumed pattern.

A controlled comparison helps keep the question narrow: whether the measured pair's observed continuity changes or remains the same between the specified states under the recorded setup. It does not establish a load rating, suitability for a circuit, or behavior under operating conditions. Nor does a single pair's result establish the condition of both poles.

## Interpret changed, unchanged, and inconsistent readings

Compare each result with the documented contact behavior for that pair and the tested actuator states. A change between states is meaningful only in relation to the documented expectation. An unchanged result is likewise not automatically a failure: without the specific switch's stated behavior and a valid test setup, the observation alone does not show whether it is expected.

If a reading differs from what the documentation leads you to expect, keep several possibilities separate. The terminals may have been identified incorrectly; the measurement setup or isolation may not have represented the intended test; the actuator may not have reached the defined state; or an internal contact issue may be present. One observation does not distinguish conclusively among these possibilities. Recheck the terminal identification, state definition, and test conditions before drawing a narrower conclusion, while maintaining the safety boundary for isolation.

A mechanical click and an electrical continuity result answer different questions. The click concerns movement of the mechanism; the continuity reading concerns the tested electrical pair under the stated conditions. Neither one, by itself, verifies every contact in both poles. Record and assess each documented pair rather than treating one successful or unexpected reading as a verdict on the complete switch.

## What an isolated contact check cannot establish

An isolated continuity check does not diagnose the equipment's wiring or other components. If an installed system has a symptom, that symptom may involve the switch, its terminal identification, external wiring, or something elsewhere in the equipment. The isolated readings described here do not determine which of those is responsible, and they do not establish how the circuit behaves when connected or operating.

Continuity alone also does not establish permissible load, electrical ratings, safety, or the switch's overall condition. Those questions require documentation and evidence appropriate to the exact switch and equipment. A reading on one pair is not proof that both poles are correct, while readings that match documented behavior in the tested states are not proof that the entire host system is good.

The practical boundary is therefore specific: identify the documented pairs, isolate the switch safely, compare each pair in defined released and actuated states, and report only those observations. If safe isolation, terminal identification, or interpretation is uncertain, stop short of an installed-circuit conclusion and leave work beyond your qualification to qualified personnel.
