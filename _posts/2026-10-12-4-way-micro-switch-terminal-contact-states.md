A **4 way micro switch** is not a universally precise description of a switch’s internal contacts. “Four-way” may refer to a circuit function or to a particular contact arrangement, but the name alone does not establish how many terminals, poles, or throws the device has. To identify the contacts, use the switch’s own markings and schematic, then compare them with the switch’s behavior in an unpowered continuity check.

A miniature snap-action switch can change an electrical contact state in response to mechanical actuator movement. That makes this type of switch commonly useful for position or limit detection, but it does not resolve what “four-way” means for a particular device. The reliable task is to identify the documented terminals and verify which contacts change between the unoperated and operated conditions.

## What “4 way micro switch” means

The phrase “four-way” is ambiguous without a device-specific diagram. It can describe a circuit function or a contact arrangement. Those meanings are not interchangeable: a circuit description does not, by itself, tell you the switch’s pole-and-throw configuration or how many terminals are present.

Do not count terminals or infer the internal arrangement from the name. A switch’s external appearance and the word “four-way” do not establish which terminals share a contact, which contact is closed at rest, or what changes when the actuator moves. Those facts require the actual switch schematic or terminal markings.

Where the switch has common, normally open, and normally closed markings, the terms identify contact roles. COM means common terminal, NO means normally open terminal, and NC means normally closed terminal. This terminology does not prove that every switch described as four-way uses those three terminals, nor does it establish their physical positions. Confirm that the symbols apply to the exact device and circuit diagram before interpreting them.

A wiring or application diagram may show how a switch is used in a larger circuit. It is not automatically a complete representation of the switch’s internal contact construction. Keep the circuit function separate from the contact arrangement, and use the switch’s own schematic or markings to settle the terminology.

## How the actuator changes micro switch contact states

A micro switch changes electrical contact state in response to mechanical movement of its actuator. For interpreting contact labels, the key reference is the unoperated position: normally open and normally closed describe their respective contact conditions in relation to that state. When the mechanism operates, the normally open contact changes from open toward closed, while the normally closed contact changes from closed toward open. Verify the actual behavior against the device diagram rather than assuming a particular construction.

The actuator may be moved in different ways on different switch designs. The available evidence does not establish a universal actuator shape, direction of movement, travel, or switching sequence for every device described as four-way. Do not assume that pressing, releasing, or moving an actuator in a particular direction will have the same effect across designs. Use the instructions or schematic for the exact switch to establish which condition is unoperated and how to reach the operated condition.

This distinction matters during identification: contact labels describe states, not terminal locations. A terminal’s physical position cannot tell you whether it is COM, NO, or NC unless the device markings or schematic identify it. Likewise, a contact-state description does not establish a permissible load or prove that the switch is suitable for a particular circuit. For those questions, the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for the exact switch and equipment govern.

## Identify terminals and verify contact states safely

Start with the switch’s terminal markings and connection diagram, if available. Record the labeled terminals and the contact arrangement shown. Do not infer terminal function from a row, side, shape, or presumed layout. Generic changeover terminology can help interpret COM, NO, and NC, but the exact device schematic must confirm that those markings apply.

For a continuity check, isolate power and disconnect the switch from the circuit before testing. Use a continuity tester across a pair of terminals with the actuator in its unoperated position, then compare that result with the result when the actuator is operated. Repeat for the relevant terminal pairs, keeping track of the actuator condition for each reading. This is a comparison of contact states, not a way to establish an undocumented rating or application.

The observations can help identify which pair changes state. A pair that is open at rest and closed when operated behaves as a normally open pair across those tested conditions; a pair that is closed at rest and open when operated behaves as a normally closed pair. If the switch has a documented common terminal, compare it with the other terminals and check whether the observed changes match the schematic. Do not assign COM solely because one terminal appears central or occupies a particular physical position.

Record the observed states and compare them with the device’s stated diagram and markings. If they conflict, do not resolve the conflict by guessing from “four-way,” by treating an application diagram as proof of internal construction, or by assuming that a familiar layout applies. Leave the terminal identities unresolved until the documentation for the exact switch and equipment clarifies them.

## Limits and common interpretation errors

The most common interpretation error is treating “four-way” as a terminal-count specification. It does not establish four terminals, four poles, or a particular throw arrangement. Nor does it establish that a device uses a specific common, normally open, and normally closed configuration. Those details depend on the switch’s own diagram and markings.

Another error is treating labels as a universal physical map. COM, NO, and NC identify terminal roles and contact conditions, but terminal layouts vary. Identify them from the markings or schematic, then use the continuity observations to verify switching. If the observed result does not agree with the documentation, keep the identification open rather than forcing a match.

Continuity testing belongs on an unpowered switch disconnected from its circuit, not on an energized circuit. A circuit application drawing also should not be mistaken for a complete internal-contact diagram. For the exact switch and equipment, use the connection diagram, terminal marking, circuit function, and permissible load documented for that combination, following applicable deenergization and qualification requirements.

In short, interpret “four-way” as a description that needs context, not as proof of a specific internal arrangement. Identify contacts from device-specific documentation and compare the documented states with an unpowered continuity check. The phrase **4 way micro switch** alone cannot establish terminal count, contact configuration, or terminal location.
