A **stacker crane guard interlock micro switch adjustment** has no universal position or setting. The correct relationship depends on the actual guard, any intervening mechanism, the switch mounting, and the equipment’s documented requirements. Adjustment should therefore begin with the equipment-specific procedure and acceptance criteria, not with an assumed actuator position. A change to one component alone cannot establish that the installed interlock performs its full safety function.

This guidance concerns the physical actuation relationship between a guard and a micro switch in a particular stacker-crane design. It does not supply a setting, live-work method, wiring instruction, or general safety test. Follow the applicable equipment procedure and deenergization and qualification requirements. If the required documentation or safe procedure is unavailable, do not improvise an adjustment; have qualified personnel assess the equipment.

## What stacker crane guard interlock micro switch adjustment can, and cannot, establish

The adjustment question is whether guard movement produces the documented actuation of the micro switch through the equipment’s actual mechanical arrangement. That arrangement may include a cam, lever, or other linkage, but its presence and configuration must be established from the specific equipment. A switch’s location or appearance does not reveal a universal correct position.

Even when the switch actuator moves, that observation establishes only movement at the component. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that the guard is aligned, that the switch contacts behave as intended in the installed circuit, or that the equipment’s safety logic responds correctly. Those are related but distinct parts of the assessment.

Before work, consult the applicable equipment documentation for the approved adjustment provisions, actuation conditions, safe procedure, and post-adjustment acceptance criteria. Use the switch’s documented connection diagram and terminal markings for terminal identification. Do not infer terminal function from physical layout or assume a contact arrangement, rating, or permissible load that is not documented for the exact switch and equipment.

If the procedure does not identify an adjustment or acceptance criterion, the missing information is not a reason to invent one. Keep the equipment out of any action that the applicable procedure does not authorize, and refer the unresolved task to qualified personnel. A component-level observation or change cannot substitute for the equipment’s documented verification process.

## Trace the guard-to-micro-switch actuation chain

Assess the mechanical path in order, using only the approved safe inspection method. Start with the guard’s position and seating. Then follow any cam or linkage, the switch’s mounting, and finally the point where the mechanism contacts the switch actuator. This sequence helps locate where motion may be lost, obstructed, or delivered inconsistently without assuming every stacker crane uses the same layout.

At the guard, observe whether it reaches the position specified by the equipment procedure. If it does not, a switch adjustment may not address the cause. The issue may lie at the guard or its relationship to the mechanism. Do not use switch position to compensate for a guard that is not reaching its documented position unless the equipment instructions explicitly authorize that adjustment.

Next, inspect any documented cam or linkage for the movement and relationship described in the procedure. A problem at this interface can prevent expected motion from reaching the switch even if the switch itself is mounted as intended. Conversely, movement at the linkage does not establish that the switch receives the required actuation. Keep each interface distinct when recording an observation.

Then assess the switch mounting and actuator contact against the actual design documentation. A displaced mounting or inconsistent contact between the mechanism and actuator may affect actuation. Do not bend, reshape, or otherwise alter parts unless documented instructions authorize that work. No numerical travel, force, clearance, or adjustment position can be supplied without evidence for the specific design.

Record which point in the chain was observed and under what procedure. If access, movement, or inspection conditions are not covered by the approved method, stop rather than create an inspection method. A clear mechanical observation can narrow the question, but it does not verify the electrical response or the equipment’s safety logic.

## Separate alignment symptoms from switch and circuit faults

A guard that does not reach its documented position, a cam or linkage that does not transfer motion consistently, and a switch that appears displaced are different observations. Do not label all of them “switch adjustment.” Identify the first point in the actuation chain where the expected relationship is not observed, and compare that observation with the equipment-specific criteria.

If the mechanical path appears consistent but the contact indication is not, the cause remains unresolved. It may involve terminal identification, wiring, switch contacts, or control logic, among other possibilities. A physical symptom alone does not distinguish these electrical or control-related possibilities. Use only the applicable documentation and approved verification method to determine what evidence is required.

A click can indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove continuity, correct wiring, correct installed operation, or suitability for the circuit. An isolated continuity result applies only to the switch in the tested state and under the test conditions used. It does not establish that the switch is correctly actuated when installed, that the circuit is connected as documented, or that the complete interlock function is verified.

Keep the test boundary explicit: a component check and an installed equipment check answer different questions. Do not treat an isolated reading as evidence that the stacker crane or its guard interlock is safe to operate. If an indication conflicts with the observed mechanical movement or the documented expected behavior, do not choose the most convenient explanation. The discrepancy requires further verification under the equipment procedure or qualified service.

## Make only a documented adjustment and verify the installed function

Adjust only a provision that the documentation for the actual equipment and switch identifies, and remain within its stated limits. The available evidence here does not specify a position, dimension, travel value, or adjustment method, so none should be inferred. Do not bend or alter a guard, cam, linkage, mounting, or actuator unless the equipment documentation expressly authorizes that work.

Follow the site’s equipment-specific safe procedure, including applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. This article does not provide instructions for live work or a substitute test sequence. If adjustment requires a step outside the approved procedure, or if the procedure does not explain how to perform it safely, leave the work to qualified personnel.

After an authorized adjustment, verify the installed guard and interlock behavior in the states and conditions required by the equipment documentation. Compare the observed behavior with its documented acceptance criteria; do not invent a pass condition from a click, a component reading, or the apparent fit of the guard. The verification must address the installed function as specified, not merely whether the switch actuator moves.

If results are ambiguous, inconsistent, or outside the documented criteria, stop and escalate for qualified service rather than making further unsupported changes. The adjustment task is complete only when the required verification is performed and meets the applicable criteria. Without that evidence, the switch’s movement or an isolated test result cannot establish the safety function of the guard interlock.
