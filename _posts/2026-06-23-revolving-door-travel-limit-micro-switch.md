A **revolving door travel limit micro switch** can provide a position-related contact change when a door-linked mechanism actuates it. The micro switch is an electromechanical component within a particular design, not the revolving door itself or the controller that interprets its signal. Some designs may use this arrangement; it should not be assumed for every revolving door.

The essential distinction is between three events: door movement reaches a mechanical interface, that interface moves the switch actuator, and the resulting contact change is interpreted by the equipment’s circuit and control logic. The switch’s movement and contact state do not, by themselves, establish what the door will do next. The intended limit position and any system response depend on the specific design.

## What a revolving door travel limit micro switch does

Where a revolving-door design uses one, a micro switch can mark a travel-related condition by changing its contact state when a door-linked part actuates it. This makes the switch a source of a position-related electrical signal. It does not make the switch the door’s travel mechanism, the controller, or a complete safety function.

The mechanical arrangement is design-specific. A moving door may operate a cam, lever, linkage, or another mechanism that reaches the switch actuator at a selected point. The resulting actuator movement operates the switch. This general relationship does not establish the exact point in the door’s movement, the shape of the mechanism, or the contact arrangement.

A travel limit signal is therefore best understood as information available to the design’s circuit, not as an unconditional statement that the door has stopped or reached a particular universally defined position. The circuit and controller determine how that information is used. Without evidence about those elements, it is not possible to infer whether the door stops, changes direction, signals an alarm, or responds in another way.

Nor does the presence of a micro switch prove that a particular revolving door uses this arrangement. Identifying the function in a real installation requires evidence about that equipment’s design and the part actually connected to the actuator.

## How the door mechanism reaches the micro switch actuator

The causal path begins with movement of the door or a door-linked part. In a design using a travel limit micro switch, that movement may carry a cam, lever, linkage, or other mechanical feature toward the switch. The feature then moves the switch actuator. The switch responds to actuator motion; it does not directly sense the door’s position without such a mechanical relationship.

This description is conditional, not a standard construction. The available information does not identify a universal cam shape, mounting location, linkage, actuator style, or adjustment. A particular design may arrange the mechanical interface differently, and the trigger geometry must be established from that design’s documentation or direct component identification rather than inferred from the equipment category.

Actuator movement and switch operation are also not interchangeable descriptions of a complete travel limit. The relevant mechanical feature must reach the actuator in the intended way, and the actuator must move to the operating position for the switch to operate. The precise operating point is specific to the switch and its installation. Do not assign a travel distance, force, or adjustment value without the applicable specification and design evidence.

For general terminology, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These terms describe actuator travel; they do not identify where a revolving door’s travel limit lies. Numerical travel limits are requirements of the applicable specification sheet and must not be inferred from the component category.

A click may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not show that the switch has the intended contact state or that the connected circuit has interpreted the signal correctly. The mechanical path explains how a door-linked part might operate a switch, not what the whole door system must do in response.

## What the contact change means, and does not mean

When the actuator operates, the switch’s contacts change state according to the documented contact arrangement and reference condition. That change can provide an input to a circuit. The meaning of the input depends on how the particular switch is identified and connected, and how the door’s controller uses it.

Do not assume a normally open or normally closed state, assign terminal names, or describe current flow without evidence for the actual contact arrangement and reference condition. A travel limit’s location also cannot be inferred from a contact label. Contact state describes the switch under a stated condition; it does not, by itself, define the door’s position or the controller’s interpretation.

The electrical signal and the system-level action are separate. A controller might use a contact change as one input in its logic, but the supplied information does not establish a particular response. A switch’s operation cannot be taken to mean that the door stops, reverses, enters an alarm state, or reaches a safe condition. Those outcomes depend on the actual circuit and control logic.

This distinction matters when describing a limit function. The switch may provide evidence that a door-linked mechanism has reached the switch’s operating point. It does not alone establish that the mechanism is correctly positioned, that the signal reaches the controller, or that the controller acts as intended. Each of those statements requires evidence beyond the component’s basic mechanical and contact operation.

## How to identify the function in a particular door design

Start with the equipment documentation and identification of the switch used in that installation. The documentation should establish which door-linked part actuates the switch, the reference condition for any stated contact behavior, and the intended travel-related function. If those details are not available, the exact trigger point, terminal behavior, and control response remain unresolved.

Keep the evidence at the level it supports. A drawing or description of a cam contacting an actuator may establish a mechanical relationship, but not necessarily the controller’s response. A circuit description may establish how a contact is used, but it should not be treated as proof of the mechanism’s physical position unless it also documents that relationship. Use the applicable specification sheet for device-specific travel limits rather than deriving numerical values from the term “micro switch.”

If the equipment documentation specifies a test, its stated isolation condition, actuator state, measuring method, and expected result must be considered together. Do not substitute an unspecified check or treat a result from one state as evidence about another. A continuity check, where documented, concerns the isolated switch in a defined actuator state. It does not establish that the installed circuit, controller, or complete door function works as intended.

This is an identification and evidence boundary, not a wiring or repair procedure. A test of the component alone cannot verify every part of the door system. Work beyond the documented safe test procedure is outside what can be established here and should be left to qualified personnel.

## Limits of what the micro switch alone can establish

Actuator movement or an audible click can show that a mechanical event occurred, but neither proves continuity, correct terminal identification, correct wiring, or the controller’s response. Likewise, a contact change in an isolated switch does not establish that the installed door system receives or correctly interprets the signal.

A travel-related symptom can have more than one possible source. The mechanical input may not reach the actuator as intended; the switch contacts may not produce the expected state; terminal identification or wiring may be at issue; the controller may interpret its input differently; or another part of the equipment may be involved. These are distinct possibilities, not a single diagnosis. The evidence for a particular installation is needed to distinguish among them.

Keep conclusions narrow. Documentation that links a door mechanism to an actuator can support a claim about the mechanical input. A defined, properly conducted check may support a claim about the isolated switch’s contact state. Neither fact alone proves the response of the controller or the operation of the complete revolving door.

In short, a revolving door travel limit micro switch can provide a position-related contact change when a door-linked mechanism operates its actuator. The switch’s exact function, contact meaning, trigger point, and effect on the door remain design-specific. Establishing those details requires the applicable equipment documentation and evidence about the actual switch, mechanical interface, and control logic.
