A centrifuge end of stroke micro switch not working is a symptom, not yet a diagnosis. First establish whether the particular centrifuge uses a micro switch to sense the end of stroke. Then check whether its mechanism reaches the switch actuator before drawing conclusions about electrical behavior. A missed mechanical input, a switch-level issue, incorrect terminal identification, wiring trouble, or a fault elsewhere in the control system can produce different problems. These possibilities should not be treated as interchangeable.

Do not force the actuator, bypass a switch, or infer the centrifuge’s contact state or control response from the switch’s presence. Electrical checks belong only within an applicable safe procedure and documented contact behavior. If the procedure, circuit, or safety implications are unclear, stop and refer diagnosis to qualified service.

## First confirm the centrifuge uses a micro switch at end of stroke

The fault description alone does not establish how the centrifuge detects end of stroke. Confirm the switch’s role from documentation applicable to the exact centrifuge, or through qualified inspection. Some equipment may use a micro switch for this function, but that cannot be generalized to every centrifuge. A nearby switch, a visible actuator, or a reported end-of-stroke fault is not enough by itself to establish how the control system uses that component.

Also establish what the documentation says about the switch’s function, connection, and expected contact behavior. Do not infer that the switch is normally open or normally closed, or that a particular contact change starts, stops, or signals something, merely because it is located near a moving mechanism. The resulting control response depends on the actual equipment design and circuit.

Before any observation or check, follow applicable deenergization and qualification requirements. If the equipment cannot be placed in a condition where the relevant mechanism can be observed safely, do not create movement or access by improvising. Leave inspection to qualified service. This diagnostic path is not a procedure for defeating a guard or interlock, and it does not establish that the switch is a safety device.

## Centrifuge end of stroke micro switch not working: observe mechanical actuation

If observation is permitted in a safe, non-operating state, start at the mechanism rather than assuming the electrical part has failed. Observe whether the end-of-stroke mechanism reaches the actuator at the intended point. The actuator is the part moved by the external mechanism. The key question is whether the centrifuge’s mechanism provides that input, not whether the switch can be made to move by hand.

If the mechanism does not appear to reach the actuator, incomplete travel, misalignment, or a linkage issue are possibilities to investigate. That observation does not prove which part is responsible, nor does it prove the switch itself is sound. Conversely, visible actuator movement does not establish that the contacts change as documented or that the rest of the circuit responds correctly.

Do not force the actuator to make the switch operate. Do not adjust or manipulate a linkage based on an assumed travel requirement, and do not defeat a guard or interlock to observe movement. The applicable documentation or qualified inspection must determine what motion is intended and whether it is occurring. Without that reference, an apparent gap or movement cannot be judged as correct or incorrect.

A switch can make an audible click as its mechanism moves, but a click alone does not prove electrical continuity, contact condition, correct wiring, or proper operation of the centrifuge. Treat sound as an observation about movement only. If the mechanism reaches the actuator and a fault remains, continue only to a documented, safe electrical verification; otherwise refer the mechanical or electrical diagnosis to qualified service.

## Separate contact behavior from terminal and wiring faults

Electrical verification must start with information for the exact switch and equipment. Use its connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load rather than guessing from the physical position of terminals. Do not assume that COM, NO, or NC terminals are present, or identify them by a generic layout. Where those designations are documented, their meaning still depends on the specified reference condition.

An isolated continuity check is appropriate only if an applicable safe procedure supports it. The procedure must establish the isolation boundary, the actuator condition to test, and the expected behavior of the documented contacts. Without those details, a meter result cannot be interpreted reliably. Do not infer an expected open or closed state from the switch’s location, from a click, or from a presumed centrifuge control sequence.

If a documented isolated test does not match the expected contact behavior, that may indicate a switch-level issue. It is not conclusive by itself: terminal identification, the actuator condition, the test method, or the test conditions could be wrong. If the isolated result matches the documented expectation, that establishes only the tested switch behavior under those conditions. It does not prove the installed wiring, control logic, or complete centrifuge operation is sound.

Keep the diagnostic boundaries separate. A switch-level check concerns the isolated component under a defined condition. Diagnosing the installed circuit involves the connection and control system, which may have faults beyond the switch. Do not treat an isolated reading as evidence that the equipment is safe to operate or that its end-of-stroke function works as intended. If the applicable procedure does not clearly support further checks, stop rather than improvising measurements on the installed circuit.

## Decide what the evidence supports, and when to stop

Use observations to identify the next question, not to jump to a replacement or repair conclusion. If the mechanism does not reach the actuator, the evidence points toward investigating the mechanical input or linkage; it does not automatically establish a failed switch. If the mechanism does reach the actuator, the observation does not establish correct contact behavior. A documented isolated test can address that narrower question, but only with the specified terminal identification, actuator condition, and test method.

If the isolated result differs from documented expected behavior, a switch-level problem is possible, while errors in terminal identification or test conditions remain possible. If the isolated result matches, the unresolved causes may include the installed wiring or control system. Neither result alone proves that the centrifuge’s end-of-stroke function works or fails as a whole.

Stop and refer diagnosis to qualified service when the circuit or switch function is not documented, the test procedure is unclear, safe isolation cannot be established, or the issue involves a safety system. Do not bypass an interlock, apply an assumed contact state, or continue into electrical diagnosis beyond the applicable safe procedure. The useful conclusion from a limited check is correspondingly limited: identify whether mechanical actuation was observed and whether a supported isolated test matched its documented expectation. Further conclusions require evidence about the specific equipment and its circuit.
