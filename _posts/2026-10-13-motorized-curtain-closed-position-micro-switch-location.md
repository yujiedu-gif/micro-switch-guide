A motorized curtain closed position micro switch location cannot be identified from the curtain’s closed endpoint alone. Some designs use a micro switch to provide a position-related input, but there is no universal mounting point, and some designs use another sensing arrangement. To locate a switch in a particular system, distinguish the switch body and its actuator from the cam, lever, or linkage that may move it, then observe the actual mechanism’s relationship at its documented closed position.

The identification task is limited: finding a switch and observing mechanical actuation do not establish its wiring, contact state, adjustment, or effect on motor control. Use the equipment’s mechanism documentation where available, and do not open energized equipment to look for a component.

## What the closed-position micro switch does, and what its location means

In some motorized curtain designs, a micro switch serves as a position-related input. The closed travel limit is a condition in the mechanism; it is not, by itself, a description of where a switch is mounted. A curtain can reach its closed endpoint while the associated switch is elsewhere in the drive or limit mechanism, or the system may use a different sensing arrangement entirely. The endpoint alone therefore does not identify a micro switch or its location.

When a micro switch is used, separate three parts of the arrangement. The switch body is the component mounted to the mechanism. Its actuator is the part moved by an external mechanism. A cam, lever, or linkage may be the feature that moves the actuator as the curtain approaches its documented closed position. These parts can occupy different places, so identifying the feature that contacts the actuator is not the same as identifying the switch body’s mounting point.

A micro switch is an electromechanical component, not a generic name for every sensor or control device. A small component near a curtain drive, rail, or end position cannot be identified by appearance or proximity alone. The actual mechanism documentation and observable mechanical relationship are more meaningful than assuming that every curtain system has the same layout.

The switch’s snap-action operating principle, where that design applies, concerns the movement of its contacts, not a standard mounting location in a curtain system. Likewise, actuator travel terminology does not tell you where a particular switch is installed. Those distinctions matter because a component’s operating characteristics cannot substitute for evidence about its place or function in a specific assembly.

## Motorized curtain closed position micro switch location: identify the installed arrangement

Start with the documentation for the particular curtain mechanism, if available. Look for its limit or position-sensing arrangement and any component layout. Do not assume that the switch is on a particular side, at the end of a rail, or inside the motor housing. The supplied evidence does not establish a standard location for motorized curtains, and layouts may differ.

If the relevant drive or limit mechanism is visible and safe to inspect, trace the physical arrangement rather than searching only at the curtain’s closed edge. Look for a small electromechanical switch and note whether it has an actuator positioned to be moved by a cam, lever, or linkage. The moving feature may be associated with the drive or limit mechanism rather than located at the curtain endpoint itself. This observation can help distinguish the switch body from the part that operates it, but it does not prove what the switch does electrically.

Treat visual resemblance as a lead, not a conclusion. A nearby sensor or other control component may have a different sensing method, and proximity to a moving part does not establish that a component detects the closed position. Identification requires a relationship between the suspected switch’s actuator and the mechanism feature that moves it, supported by the system documentation or direct observation of the installed arrangement.

The purpose of tracing the mechanism is to answer a narrow physical question: where is the switch body, and what feature appears to operate its actuator? If that relationship is hidden, undocumented, or impossible to observe safely, the location remains uncertain. Do not infer it from a diagram for a different arrangement or from the point where the curtain stops.

## Verify the closed-limit relationship without assuming circuit behavior

Where the mechanism can be observed under its documented safe operating conditions, note which feature contacts or moves the identified actuator as the curtain reaches its documented closed position. This observation supports a mechanical relationship between that feature and the switch actuator. It can help distinguish a candidate closed-limit switch from a component that is merely nearby.

Keep the conclusion within that observation. Seeing an actuator move does not establish electrical continuity, terminal function, contact state, wiring, or control logic. It also does not prove that the switch causes the curtain to stop. The stopping behavior depends on the actual system arrangement, which cannot be inferred from a physical location or movement alone.

Do not use the sound or movement of the curtain as proof that a particular switch has been identified. A visible endpoint is not a component label, and a moving mechanism may contain multiple parts. The evidence needed here is the observed mechanical link between the relevant feature and the actuator, not an assumption that the curtain’s final position maps directly to a specific switch.

If observing the arrangement would require opening energized equipment, stop rather than proceeding with an internal inspection. Electrical access or testing is outside this identification guidance. Use qualified service when the component cannot be identified through documentation and a safe, visible observation. This boundary keeps a location check from turning into unsupported electrical diagnosis or adjustment.

## When the location remains uncertain

The location remains uncertain if the mechanism documentation is unavailable or unclear, the suspected switch cannot be distinguished from other components, or the actuator’s relationship to the closed-limit mechanism cannot be observed. A closed curtain alone does not resolve any of these uncertainties. Some designs may use another sensing arrangement, and a switch used in one layout need not occupy the same place in another.

Consult documentation for the specific mechanism or a qualified technician rather than relocating, adjusting, or bypassing a suspected component. Identifying a physical switch does not establish whether it is correctly set, how it is wired, or whether it is responsible for the system’s stopping behavior. Those are separate questions and require evidence beyond the switch’s apparent location.

Keep the open-position function separate as well. The open and closed limits need not share a location or arrangement, so locating one does not identify the other. The practical conclusion is narrow: in a system that uses a micro switch for the closed limit, identify its body by tracing the documented or safely observed mechanism feature that moves its actuator at the closed position. If that relationship cannot be established, the motorized curtain closed position micro switch location is not confirmed.
