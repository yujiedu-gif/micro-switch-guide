An **elevator car closed position micro switch adjustment** cannot be assigned a universal setting or procedure. The appropriate adjustment, if the specific elevator design uses a micro switch for this function, must come from that switch’s documentation and the elevator’s service procedure. Without those sources, there is no supported basis for prescribing a position, dimension, or sequence of steps.

The key distinction is between the switch’s physical relationship to its actuator and the elevator system’s interpretation of the switch contact. Observing actuator movement or a contact change does not establish how the controller responds, or whether the door system is safe. Adjustment and installed-system functional testing belong within the equipment-specific procedure and should be performed by appropriately qualified personnel.

## What elevator car closed position micro switch adjustment can establish

At most, an adjustment establishes the switch-to-actuator relationship specified for a particular elevator design. It does not establish a general setting for all elevators, nor does it by itself establish that the elevator controller recognizes a closed position in the intended way. Some designs may use a micro switch for this function; the presence of a switch in one arrangement is not evidence that every elevator uses one.

The actuator is the part of the switch moved by an external mechanism. In a documented installation, that mechanism may bring the actuator into engagement as the car reaches a relevant position. The required relationship between mechanism and actuator, however, must be identified from the actual equipment documentation. No universal engagement point, travel amount, force, or adjustment dimension is established here.

A switch contact change is also not an explanation of the elevator’s control behavior. The resulting effect depends on the documented wiring, circuit function, and controller logic for the specific equipment. A contact state alone does not show what the controller will do, whether other conditions are satisfied, or whether the overall door system is safe. Those conclusions cannot be inferred simply from the component’s name or from observing its actuator move.

This makes the adjustment question conditional rather than generic. If the exact switch, linkage, connection information, and elevator service procedure are unavailable, the adjustment boundary is unknown. Do not substitute a setting taken from a different switch or elevator arrangement. The evidence needed to make a specific adjustment decision is equipment-specific, and the available facts do not supply it.

## How the actuator relationship affects the switch state

The relevant mechanical sequence, where documented, is that an external mechanism moves the switch actuator; the switch may then change contact state at its operating event. As the actuator moves back, a distinct release event may occur. Movement, operation, and release are not interchangeable observations. The switch’s operating and release events describe its own behavior, not the elevator controller’s interpretation of the resulting contact state.

Actuator travel terms can help describe that mechanical relationship when they are defined for the applicable switch and procedure. In the referenced standard context, pretravel runs from the free position to the operating position, while overtravel runs from the operating position to the extreme permitted actuator position. These definitions do not supply an elevator adjustment value. Nor do they establish that a particular switch installation should be adjusted to any specific point within that travel.

A click, if one is heard or felt, may indicate movement of a snap mechanism, but it does not prove electrical continuity, correct terminal identification, correct wiring, or suitable system operation. It is therefore insufficient as the sole verification of an adjustment. Likewise, observing that a linkage touches or moves the actuator does not demonstrate that the documented contact behavior occurs under the required conditions.

Keep three questions separate: Does the external mechanism engage the actuator as specified? Does the isolated switch exhibit the documented contact behavior at the specified actuator state? Does the installed elevator system pass its service procedure’s functional verification? Evidence for one question does not automatically answer the others. In particular, a component observation cannot establish the controller response or certify the overall operation of the elevator.

The connection diagram, terminal markings, circuit function, and permissible load must be taken from documentation for the exact switch and equipment. A generic physical-layout assumption cannot identify terminals or establish how a contact is used. Because controller behavior varies by design, do not infer door-interlock behavior, movement behavior, or any other system response from a switch contact change alone.

## What must be verified before any adjustment decision

First, the decision requires the exact elevator service procedure and the acceptance criteria it states for the relevant switch and mechanism. Those documents determine whether adjustment is permitted, what relationship is required, and how the result must be checked. Without them, no specific adjustment dimension, setting, or step sequence can responsibly be supplied.

Mechanical observations and electrical checks answer different questions. A visual or mechanical observation may describe whether a mechanism appears to engage the actuator, but it does not establish electrical contact behavior. An isolated continuity check, if called for and documented, concerns the switch rather than the installed elevator system. Its result is meaningful only when the switch is isolated as required, the correct terminals are identified from the equipment documentation, the actuator state is defined, and the expected behavior is specified by that documentation.

Do not treat an unspecified meter reading as proof. The available evidence does not establish which terminals to test, what state should be measured for a particular elevator, or what reading its service procedure accepts. Terminal designations and contact arrangement cannot be presumed. In particular, use the exact switch diagram and markings rather than guessing from terminal position or appearance.

An isolated component check cannot substitute for installed-system verification. Functional testing of the elevator after any authorized adjustment must follow the specific equipment procedure and its applicable safety requirements. That installed-system verification belongs to appropriately qualified personnel. This article does not provide a do-it-yourself adjustment or test sequence, and no isolated reading or actuator observation should be used to infer that the elevator or its door system is safe.

The evidence also sets a firm limit on what can be concluded without documentation. It does not specify an elevator model, switch identity, terminal arrangement, linkage geometry, controller response, or acceptance threshold. If a procedure calls for measurements or states, those requirements must be obtained from that procedure rather than supplied by general assumptions. If those requirements cannot be established, there is no evidence-based adjustment decision to make.

## When the adjustment question cannot be answered generically

The adjustment cannot be determined generically when the switch design, actuator mechanism, terminal arrangement, wiring, or controller logic is not documented for the actual equipment. These details may differ between designs, and none can be filled in by assuming that the same switch name implies the same installation or behavior. A procedure for a different elevator or switch is not a basis for transferring dimensions, contact states, or acceptance criteria.

If documentation is unavailable, incomplete, or unclear, stop short of changing the switch position. Obtain the applicable elevator service information and have the situation assessed by appropriately qualified personnel. This is especially important because the adjustment question concerns a component in an elevator system, while the available evidence does not establish the system’s control logic or safe functional state.

A symptom near the closed-position indication does not by itself identify a bad switch or prove that adjustment is needed. Possible areas requiring diagnosis include the switch, the actuator mechanism, wiring, or another part of the equipment. Distinguishing among them requires the applicable service procedure and observations or tests it specifies. Do not attribute the symptom to one component merely because the switch is associated with a closed-position function.

The decision boundary is therefore straightforward: use only the exact equipment documentation to determine whether an adjustment is specified and how its result is accepted; leave the installed-system verification to qualified personnel under the stated procedure. Without that evidence, the responsible answer to **elevator car closed position micro switch adjustment** is not a guessed setting, but that the adjustment remains unresolved for the specific elevator.
